# <img src="assets/icons/legal-01.svg" width="20" height="20" alt="" /> due-diligence

> **How to read this**
>
> This skill verifies. It does not profile. Profiling asks how a person thinks and how to approach them; this skill asks what is verifiably true about a person or a company, and where the risk sits, before something real is committed to them. The two skills are siblings with a one-way feed: the fact sheet this skill produces is the record — the hardest evidence tier — that psychological-profiling reads. This skill never infers personality, motives, or character. It reports what the record shows, what it contradicts, and what it cannot reach.

## What this skill is

Every commitment has a moment before it where the facts are still cheap: before the partnership is signed, the wire is sent, the hire is made, the contract is awarded. After that moment, the same facts cost lawyers. This skill works in the cheap window. It takes a person, a company, or a person and their company, and checks them against the public record — registries, dockets, regulators, licensing boards, and the claims they themselves have made — and returns a fact sheet where every item carries a status, a source, and a date.

The discipline is verification, not investigation theater. Three habits do the work. Identity is resolved before anything is attributed, because public records are full of namesakes and a finding about the wrong person is worse than no finding. Allegations are kept separate from outcomes, because a filing is a claim and a judgment is a conclusion, and confusing them is how diligence turns into defamation. And claims are checked against primary sources wherever a primary source exists, because the gap between what a person says about themselves and what the record says is the single most decision-relevant output a check can produce.

## The procedure

### Step 0 — Name the commitment and set the depth

One check serves one commitment: this partnership, this investment, this hire, this contract. Write the commitment down, because it decides everything downstream — which modules run, how deep to dig, and how a finding grades. A fraud judgment matters differently to a hire than to a one-off vendor; the grading in Step 4 is relative to the commitment, not to morality.

Set the depth:

- **Light.** A call, a first meeting, a small engagement. Identity resolution, sanctions and watchlist screening, and a headline pass over adverse media. For pure conversation prep, psychological-profiling's built-in legal sweep is usually enough and this skill need not run at all.
- **Standard.** A business relationship: a collaborator, a vendor of consequence, an advisor, a counterparty. Every module whose trigger the commitment meets, at registry-and-docket depth.
- **Deep.** Money, equity, partnership, employment — any commitment where their failure becomes your loss. Every triggered module, primary sources for every material claim, and a re-run before signing if the first sweep has aged.

**Depth follows the access granted, not the size of the deal.** A short trial, a small contract, a minor vendor — any of them can hand a person or a company the keys: production systems, admin credentials, signing authority, custody of funds. When a commitment grants access like that, set the depth by the access, not by the engagement's price or length: a twelve-day trial with production keys is a Deep check wearing a Light price tag. And a person check that granted access carries a closing exit item, answered when the engagement ends: was the access revoked, and was every shared key the person could have reached rotated? An exit item nobody answered is reported open, like any other gap.

### Step 1 — Resolve identity

Nothing is attributed until identity is resolved. Records collide: the same name belongs to strangers, and companies share names across jurisdictions.

**For a person:** fix the legal name and its variants, then anchor with independent identifiers the decision legitimately provides — city, profession, employer, the companies they are tied to. A record counts as the subject's only when **two independent identifiers** tie it to them. Every match that fails the test goes in the namesake log with the fact that separates it (a different city, employer, or field). The log is part of the output: it proves the sweep was discriminating, and it protects the next reader from re-finding the wrong person.

**The mechanics.** Resolution runs on variants and pivots, not on the name as given. Generate the variant set first: misspellings the press and registries actually produce, name-order and initial forms, maiden and former names where the record carries them, transliterations for names that cross scripts, and phonetic equivalents for names that cross languages. Then work the selector pivots — each anchor the decision provides (a handle, an email domain, an employer, an entity, a city) is a door into the others: a handle found on one platform is tried on the rest, an entity's filing names its officers, an officer's other entities widen or narrow the set. Keep every live candidate as a named entry with what would confirm it and what would eliminate it, until it resolves or is excluded; silently dropping a candidate is how two people get fused into one record. Widen when the anchors return nothing (drop the city, add the variant), narrow when they return too much (add the employer, the entity, the year) — and stop when two independent identifiers tie the record, or when the remaining candidates cannot be separated in public sources, which is reported as the finding it is.

**For a company:** fix the legal entity name, jurisdiction, entity type, registration number where the registry gives one, status (active, dissolved, revoked), registered officers or directors, assumed names (DBAs), and parent or subsidiary links. Then resolve the people behind it: who owns, who directs, who signs — because most company risk is a person's history wearing an entity's name, and most entity games (dissolve, re-form, rename) are visible in the registry if the chain is followed.

Most real checks do not start from a legal name. They start from a brand, a website, an invoice, or a pitch deck — and a brand, its operator, and its owner are routinely three different entities. Resolution routes from the surface to the entity: the website's own statutory disclosure and terms page (many jurisdictions require the operating company to be named there), the trademark register (the proprietor of the brand is a named legal person), the invoice's company and tax numbers, the filing system for listed parents, and from any one confirmed group member, the chain walked in both directions — parents, subsidiaries, siblings.

If identity cannot be resolved with confidence — a common name, a thin footprint, conflicting anchors — the fact sheet says so and stops. An unresolved identity is a finding, and it grades as one.

### Step 2 — Screen

Screening is its own step, run at every depth, because its output is a different kind of evidence: a negative that has to be worth something later. Screen the subject, every entity resolved in Step 1, the parents and subsidiaries in the chain, and every named principal — against the sanctions lists (the OFAC Specially Designated Nationals and consolidated lists, and their equivalents in each jurisdiction the chain touches), the politically-exposed-persons (PEP) lists, and the debarment and exclusion lists that bear on the commitment. Aggregators such as OpenSanctions search across lists and serve as the working tool; a hit is confirmed at the primary list before it is reported, and a hit that fails the two-identifier rule is a namesake, logged like any other. One list is also searched directly, as a step of its own: the US sanctions lists are run through OFAC's own search tool (sanctionssearch.ofac.treas.gov), the publisher's own route to the SDN and consolidated lists. The aggregator pass does not substitute for it — an aggregator can lag or mis-map a list update, and the direct search is cheap. The screening log names the direct search alongside the aggregator pass, so a reader can see both were run.

**Log the screen.** The fact sheet records the lists searched, the names and variants searched, and the date. A clean result without its log is an assertion; with its log, it is evidence — someone can see exactly what "no match" covers, and re-run it.

### Step 3 — Sweep the modules

Each module returns items, and every item carries a status (Step 4), a source, and a date. The source map, with access notes and reliability grades, is in `references/the-sources.md`. Several sources have free programmatic routes that beat reading pages, and the sweep uses them where they exist: the CourtListener API for federal dockets (free account token), the GLEIF API for LEI records (free, no key), the SEC's EDGAR APIs (free, no key), and the govinfo API for court opinions (free key). Any key a source needs belongs to the user and lives in the user's own credential vault — never inside the skill and never printed in a fact sheet.

**Sequence: records before contact.** The record modules are read-only, and the subject cannot see them run. The contact modules — verification calls, and reference checks through the `reference-checks` skill — are different: they are moves the subject can learn about, because an institution can mention a call and a reference can repeat the questions. So the sweep runs in that order. Every record module first; contact last, and never before the record is settled. A subject who learns a check is running can coach a reference, revise a claim, or tidy what can still be tidied. A contact made early spends the check's one invisible pass.

**Entity and corporate record.** Work the registry as a record, not a summary page. Search by company number where the registry allows it — names collide, numbers do not. Read the filing-history timeline in order: formation, amendments, annual reports, forfeitures and reinstatements, name changes, dissolutions — the timeline is where dissolve-and-reform games and cured lapses live. Collect every former name and run the later searches under those names too. Read the charges and secured-party registers (UCC and equivalents): a lender with a charge over the assets is a hidden counterparty whose consent may gate the very deal being checked. **At Deep, this search is required, not optional, for any entity the commitment puts money into** — an investment, a loan, an acquisition. Search the UCC index in the entity's formation state and its home state, under its current and former names, and report every live financing statement: the secured party (the lender), the filing date, and what the collateral covers. The reason is ordering: a lender who filed first holds a claim on those assets ahead of any new money, and the index is the only public place that claim shows. Access varies by state — some indexes are free online, some sit behind a state account at about a dollar a search; the cost is logged as a cost of the run, and a state that could not be searched is named as a gap, never passed over in silence. Pivot on the officers (every other entity each officer touches) and on the registered address (every entity sharing it). Parent and ownership chains are read from the filings and, where they exist, the LEI record and its parent data (GLEIF). Mirrors such as OpenCorporates are indexes that point to the registry, never the source itself; offshore and leak databases (the ICIJ Offshore Leaks database, OCCRP's Aleph) are lead-grade — they open questions, they never close them. Ownership tracing fails in four known ways — nominees, trusts, layering across jurisdictions, and disclosure thresholds that stop just below the subject's holding — and when the trail ends at one of them, the documented stopping point is itself reported as a finding: "ownership could not be traced past this point, by this mechanism" is diligence output, not a shrug. The shell-company indicator catalogue in `references/shell-company-indicators.md` supports this module. What it catches: dissolved-and-reformed companies, serial entities, a claimed company that does not exist, a real company the subject is not actually an officer of, and structures built to be untraceable.

**Procurement records — revenue a third party confirms.** A registry proves a company exists; it does not prove the company sells anything. Public procurement records close that gap. Governments and many large buyers publish their contract awards, tender results, and vendor registrations: who won, for what, and for how much. An award record is kept by the buyer, not the seller, so it counts as primary-source evidence of real revenue and a real customer — the strongest free evidence of actual operations there is, and the evidence class a claimed trading history is checked against first. Search the award and vendor databases for every jurisdiction the company claims to sell into, under its current and former names. A company that claims years of substantial sales and appears in no buyer's records has not been contradicted — private buyers publish nothing — but a claim of government or institutional customers that no award record supports grades unverified, and an award the company claims that the buyer's own record shows went to someone else is a contradiction. What it catches: paper companies with a real registry entry and no operations behind it, and revenue claims inflated past anything a buyer ever paid. Ownership is traced up until it ends at natural persons — the ultimate beneficial owners — or at an endpoint that needs no piercing: a listed company or a regulated institution, whose ownership is dispersed and supervised. Every layer between the target and that endpoint is named, screened (Step 2), and read in its own registry. Ownership is traced down through every subsidiary the target controls, the same way. Where the trail cannot be pierced — a nominee, a trust, a disclosure threshold — the trace ends at a documented stopping point and the sheet reports it as a finding: a structure that cannot be traced to a person is itself an answer about the commitment.

**Principals and partners are checked in proportion to their control.** Control persons — officers, directors, owners at or above the standard 25% beneficial-ownership line, and anyone with signing authority over the commitment's money — get the full pass at Deep: identity resolution, the legal and regulatory modules, and the pivot to their other entities, whose litigation, distress, and dissolutions are findings here too, because a partner whose other companies keep failing is telling you something about this one. Below the control line — minority holders without authority, co-investors, vendors, advisers — the treatment is screening plus adverse press, and anything found grades by its bearing on the commitment: a shady name with no control and no access to the money is a note; the same name with signing authority is a must-explain, and where the finding is severe, a block. The check follows control and money, not every name in the building.

**Digital estate — the timeline check.** Every company and most people leave a dated trail online, and the trail has a start. This step reads that start and sets it against the claimed timeline. Collect the earliest verifiable traces: the registration dates and history of the subject's domains (kept by the domain registries, not by the subject), the earliest archived copies of their websites in the public web archives, and the creation dates of long-standing public accounts and profiles where the platform shows them. Then compare. A company that claims a decade of operating history whose domain was registered last year, whose site first appears in the archives last year, and whose profiles are all younger still, has a timeline problem: the claim and the earliest trace disagree, and the item grades like any other claim-versus-record mismatch — the founding claim is contradicted or partially verified, with the dates quoted. Run the check the other way too: infrastructure shared with unrelated brands — the same server, the same analytics or advertising account identifiers across sites that claim no connection — is a lead toward a common operator, worked under the entity module's pivots. Two cautions keep the step honest. An aged domain can be bought, so an old registration alone verifies nothing — the trace has to show the claimed activity, not just an old name. And a thin early web is normal for a small or offline business, so absence of an early trace grades unverified, not contradicted, unless the claim itself is about an online presence. What it catches: backdated founding stories, recently built operations dressed in a long claimed history, and brands quietly run by the same operator as brands already known. Required at Standard and above whenever money or assets move under a contract: an investment, a loan, an advisory or services agreement where the pay matters. Every other module reads the record about the counterparty; this one reads the document in front of you, because a clean counterparty under bad paper is still a bad commitment. Five questions, answered from the signed or draft text:

- **Forum and governing law.** Is there a dispute clause at all — where would a fight happen, under what law, in court or in arbitration? A contract with no forum or governing-law clause is a finding, not a footnote: the first question in any dispute would have no agreed answer.

- **Is our claim secured?** If they owe money, tokens, or deliverables, does anything stand behind the promise — a filed security interest (a UCC-1 filing; UCC is the Uniform Commercial Code, the state law that governs secured loans), collateral over tokens or receivables, a guarantee from a parent or a principal? If not, the sheet says it in its own words: we are an unsecured creditor, standing behind every lender who did file.

- **Staged or all up front?** Is delivery staged, escrowed, or tied to milestones — or does everything move on day one against a promise? All-up-front delivery against an unsecured promise is the structure this module exists to catch.

- **Records and audit rights.** Does the paper give a right to records, reports, or an audit — with a deadline for producing them and a consequence for refusing? A records right with no deadline and no consequence is a courtesy, not a right.

- **Pay in their own paper.** How much of the consideration is the counterparty's own paper — their token, their equity, their note — and what is the deal worth if that paper goes to zero? Price the commitment at the zero case before signing, not after. When the paper is a token, this question prices the structure; the token-offerings module prices the instrument itself.

A missing dispute clause, or an unsecured all-up-front structure, grades in Step 4 as a finding — must-explain at minimum, and a block where the commitment's money has no other protection.

**Legal record.** Federal dockets (PACER, with CourtListener's free archive) and state court portals: civil litigation in the person's professional capacity and against their entities, criminal matters, bankruptcy filings. Read for outcomes: filed is a claim; dismissed, settled, judgment, plea, and sanction are what a process concluded. Volume and pattern matter more than any single case — litigation is ordinary in business life, and one suit across a career sits near the base rate for founders and executives. **At Standard depth and above, the state layer is worked by residence, not skipped:** for each state the person lives in now or has lived in, sweep the state or county court index for their name, the lien and judgment records, the property deeds for places they held property, and the Secretary of State's UCC index (loans secured against business assets). Most of these searches are free online; where a portal charges per page or per document, the cost is logged as a cost of the run. What each holds differs from the federal layer: the federal sweep finds lawsuits in federal court and federal filings; the state layer finds the everyday record — county lawsuits, tax liens, judgments, property, and secured loans. A legal sweep that covers only the federal layer is a gap, and the fact sheet names it as one until the residence states are done.

**Regulatory and licensing.** Enforcement actions and published proceedings: securities and markets regulators, trade and consumer-protection agencies, state attorneys general. (Sanctions and watchlist screening is Step 2, run before the modules.) Professional licenses where the work needs one: status (active, lapsed, revoked), discipline history, the name the license is actually held under.

**Financial-distress signals — public only.** Bankruptcy filings, federal and state tax liens, civil judgments, foreclosure filings where public. That is the honest extent of the public financial record. Credit reports and personal debt data are not public records in the United States — they are consumer reports behind federal law — and this skill does not treat people-search sites' claims about them as evidence. A distress signal is a fact with a date, not a verdict: an old, discharged bankruptcy grades differently from a fresh lien, and both grade by their bearing on this commitment. **For a crypto counterparty, add the market record.** A project's own token is a public distress gauge that moves faster than any court filing: its price against its launch price, its trading liquidity, the exchange listings it has gained and lost. A token down nearly all its value from launch, or delisted from the exchanges that carried it, is a distress finding even when the court and lien records are clean — labeled market data, dated like everything else, and graded as a signal that sharpens the other modules rather than a verdict on its own.

**Vendor cyber and data protection.** Runs when the commitment is a vendor of consequence — above all a vendor that will hold the user's data, money, or customers. Tier the vendor first by what it will touch (public content, internal data, regulated data, funds), because the depth of this module follows the exposure. The public sweep: certification claims verified at the issuer, not on the vendor's page (an ISO certificate is checked against the certification body's register; a claimed SOC 2 is noted as a claim — the report itself is not public); breach and incident history (breach-notification registers, regulator enforcement, domain-level breach exposure of the Have I Been Pwned kind — which services were breached, never anyone's credentials); external posture that anyone can read (email authentication — DMARC, SPF, DKIM — TLS, a published security.txt or trust center); and continuity signals in the ordinary modules (distress, litigation, key-person concentration). What the sweep cannot reach publicly — the SOC 2 report, a penetration-test summary, the data-processing agreement, the subprocessor list — goes on a document-request list the user sends as themselves, as legitimately held material once provided. The sweep never obtains documents by pretense, and a vendor's refusal to provide them is reported as a finding, graded by the tier.

**On-chain record.** Runs when the commitment supplies wallet addresses or contracts — a protocol, a vault, a token, a pseudonymous counterparty. For addresses: the explorer history (age, counterparties, flows) and sanctions screening of the addresses themselves — the OFAC lists carry crypto addresses, and Step 2's screen extends to them. For contracts: who can actually do what, read from chain state at a noted block — the owner, the proxy admin, who holds upgrade and pause authority, a multisig's threshold and signer count, any timelock and its delay, and for vaults the curator, the caps, and the oracles. Control is the diligence question: a contract nobody can upgrade and a contract one key can rewrite are different commitments wearing the same name. Clustering and attribution heuristics (this address probably belongs with that one) are labeled inference wherever they appear, never reported as fact. **Read both sides of the wire.** The control read runs on our side too, before access is granted or funds move: the contracts the commitment touches on the user's own side — who holds the admin key, whether it is one key or a multisig, the threshold, any timelock — because a commitment that hands a counterparty, a contractor, or a vendor a path to a single-key contract has its risk set by that key, whatever the counterparty's record says. And on the far side: the destination wallets that will hold the assets — who owns them, what signing threshold protects them, named people or anonymous keys. Custody concentrated in one staff member's wallet is a finding about the commitment, readable before the transfer, not after it. A counterparty that stays pseudonymous after the sweep is an unresolved identity, and grades as one — that is a finding about the commitment's risk, not a puzzle the sweep keeps chewing on.

**Token offerings — Deep, for token commitments.** Runs when the commitment buys a token, or takes one as pay. The question is not only who is selling but whether the sale itself holds up. Check the offering on EDGAR (the SEC's filing system): a registration statement, a Form D (the notice filed when securities are sold under an exemption), or a stated exemption the facts support — an offering with none of the three is an unregistered sale, and that grades as a finding about the commitment, not a technicality. Read the token contract's control: who can mint, who can pause transfers, who holds the treasury keys. Get the insider allocations — team, investors, advisors — and the unlock schedule in writing; an allocation claim no document supports is unverified, graded like any other claim. And compare shipped against promised: what the whitepaper or deck said would exist by now against what exists, dated. A token whose insiders can mint, whose unlocks are unwritten, and whose product is still a promise is three findings wearing one ticker.

**Claim verification.** Pin the claims first, at intake, before the sweep: every material claim is written in checkable form and assigned to the module or primary source that will test it. A claim pinned after the subject learns the questions tests nothing — they can revise it to fit — and a material claim no source can test is logged at intake as untestable, not discovered as a surprise gap at the end. The subject's own claims, checked: employers and titles, degrees and credentials, licenses, board seats, companies founded, deals done, amounts raised. Each claim is quoted as made, then checked against the primary source where one exists — the institution, the registry, the filing, the company's own record. Specific claims are checkable; vague ones ("advisor to several funds") return unverified, which is itself reported, because a biography built from uncheckable claims is a finding about how the subject presents. Inconsistencies are quoted claim-against-record, without adjectives. The worked example in `references/fact-sheet-format-and-example.md` shows this module catching a fabricated biography in public.

**Adverse media.** News archives and trade press, swept for the subject and their entities. Two disciplines: repetition is not corroboration — ten outlets carrying one wire story are one source — and a reported allegation stays an allegation in the fact sheet, with its outcome tracked wherever the story continued. Media is a lead generator for the record modules: a story about a lawsuit sends the sweep to the docket, where the outcome lives. A media item grades nothing on its own; it enters the fact sheet as a lead until a record module closes it.

**Non-US subjects — the international sweep.** Runs when the subject, the entity, or the chain sits outside the United States. Work the home registry first: the registry in the jurisdiction of formation is the primary source, the same as a secretary of state at home. OpenCorporates is the map across jurisdictions — lead-grade, as everywhere in this skill: it finds the entity and its number; the registry behind it is the finding. The LEI record (LEI: Legal Entity Identifier, the global company ID system run by GLEIF) anchors identity and reported parent relationships across borders. Several national registries are free and primary: UK Companies House, France's company directory, Germany's company register, Canada's federal corporations register, and Australia's ABN and ASIC lookups. The ICIJ Offshore Leaks database is the lead source for offshore structures — it opens questions that close, if they close, at a registry. Screening widens to the global lists: OFAC, the EU consolidated list, the UK list, the UN Security Council list, the World Bank's debarred firms — with OpenSanctions as the aggregator across them, hits confirmed at the primary list as always. Courts run through the free-law network: BAILII (UK and Ireland), CanLII (Canada), AustLII (Australia), and the wider WorldLII — coverage is strong in common-law countries and thins fast elsewhere. The limits, stated plainly in the sheet: some registries charge per document (Singapore, Hong Kong, India), and some are effectively closed to direct search (Cayman filings run through registered agents); European privacy law makes people records far thinner than company records; and names crossing alphabets need variant spellings — transliteration belongs in the Step 1 variant set, not in an afterthought. Verification follows the same wall: the verification-call route reaches US numbers, so outside the US an item closes by email, by LinkedIn, or through the entity's registered agent — under the same open, one-at-a-time approval rules.

**Verification calls — Deep only, one approval at a time.** Some items close only by asking the institution that holds the fact: a university registrar confirming a degree, an employer's HR desk confirming dates and title, a registry clerk confirming an entity's status. At Deep depth, when an item matters to the commitment and no document route has closed it, a call may be proposed. The rules are strict. The call is open: it says it is a verification call, names who is asking, and states only the person's name and the single fact to confirm — never the wider purpose, never a pretense. Where the institution requires the person's consent (registrars and employers normally do), consent comes first or the call is not made. **Every call is approved by the user before it is placed, one call at a time; there is no standing approval.** The outcome joins the item's evidence: who confirmed it, the date, and exactly what they confirmed. A refusal or a "we can only confirm with consent" is itself recorded — it closes the item as unverified-by-this-route, not as a negative.

**Reference checks — optional, Deep only; runs the `reference-checks` skill.** The record modules read what institutions kept; references ask what people remember — the names the subject offers, tested against the record, and names found independently of the subject. That work lives in its own skill now: the `reference-checks` skill carries the witness verification (confirming each reference is who they claim to be before their answers count), the question bank, the channel forms, and the grading of what comes back. Run it from here when the commitment warrants it, after the record sweep (see the sequencing rule above), and log its outcomes in the fact sheet like any other module's items. One rule stays in this skill's own text, because it governs every contact this skill can set in motion: **every contact is approved by the user in advance, one at a time; there is no standing approval.** Public complaint records for an entity — the Better Business Bureau file, regulator complaint summaries, review patterns — are not contact at all: they stay here, swept read-only under adverse media.

### Step 4 — Grade the findings

**Statuses.** Every item in the fact sheet carries one: **verified** (a primary source confirms it), **partially verified** (some elements confirm, some do not), **unverified** (no primary source could be reached either way), **contradicted** (a primary source says otherwise), **not found** (the named sources were swept and do not carry it — always stated with the sources, never as a blanket negative).

**Red flags, graded for this commitment.** A flag is a finding that bears on the decision. Grade by consequence:

- **Blocks the commitment** — a fraud judgment or a fraud plea in the same kind of dealing now proposed; an active sanctions match; a license revoked for cause where the work requires the license; identity that cannot be resolved.
- **Must be explained** — a bankruptcy or a pattern of partner litigation inside the relevant window; a contradicted credential; a dissolved entity whose obligations the new one would inherit; distress signals that touch the commitment's money.
- **Note and proceed** — an old dismissed suit; a satisfied lien; litigation volume at the base rate for the person's field and station.

Grading is relevance, not morality: the same record grades differently for a hire, an investment, and a vendor, and the fact sheet grades for the commitment named in Step 0.

### Step 5 — Write the fact sheet and hand off

Fixed format (full template in `references/fact-sheet-format-and-example.md`): identity resolution first, module findings with statuses and dated sources, red flags graded, inconsistencies quoted, gaps stated. The summary for the human leads with what would change the decision — the blocks, then the must-explains — in plain words. The summary restates graded findings only: every sentence in it traces to an item in the sheet below it. A summary is the checker's voice, and the checker's voice is not a witness — the record is. No new claim enters at summary level.

**Then run the writing pass. This step calls two sibling skills. It is a procedure, not a style reminder.** Everything the human reads goes through it: the summary and the sheet's prose. A fact sheet delivered in analyst register has failed this step, however sound the checking is: a sheet that reads like a form teaches its reader to skim it, and the one finding that mattered gets skimmed with the rest.

1. **Humanizer.** Run the work-humanizer skill in rewrite mode over the summary and the sheet's prose. Statuses, quotes, names, and dates never change in the pass — the evidence never gets paraphrased into softness. What changes is the register: findings explained the way you would say them out loud.
2. **Plain style.** Apply the writing-styles skill's plain rules to the result: short sentences, everyday words, numbers spoken the way a person says them, bold only on the few facts that matter.
3. **Two layers, two jobs.** The summary is the reader version: plain, and it leads with what would change the decision. The full sheet — statuses, dated sources, quoted inconsistencies — is the record under it. The summary is the deliverable; the sheet ships with it as the workings.
4. **The gate.** The sheet is not done until the pass has run on the exact text being delivered. Two habits ride with the style. **Explain every registry term in plain words the first time it appears** — LEI, CIK, CRD, the form names (Form D, Form ADV) — one short clause is enough; the reader should never need a glossary the sheet did not give them. And **write bulleted lists loose**, a blank line between items, so each finding stands on its own.

The handoff: the verified facts, formatted as a record block psychological-profiling can cite as its hardest evidence tier — identity, entities, legal and regulatory outcomes, distress signals with dates, verified and contradicted claims. Profiling takes it from there; this skill's work ends at what is true.

## Guardrails

1. **Public or legitimately held material only.** Registries, dockets, published proceedings, and material the subject or the user legitimately holds. Nothing behind a pretext, nothing obtained by misrepresenting who is asking or why.
2. **No contact as investigation.** The sweep does not call the subject's employers, schools, or associates under any pretense, and does not contact the subject at all. The sweep is likewise read-only on every platform: no following the subject, no friend or connection requests to them or their circle — a profile visible only to friends or connections is logged as private, never unlocked by asking. The exceptions to no-contact are exactly two: verification calls, a module in Step 3, and reference checks, which run the separate `reference-checks` skill. Each runs under its own strict rules — open, no pretense, consent where the institution requires it, Deep only, and approved by the user before it happens, one contact at a time; there is no standing approval for either.
3. **Identity before findings.** Nothing is attributed on a name match alone. Two independent identifiers, namesakes logged.
4. **Allegations are not findings.** A filing, a charge, an arrest, a headline is reported as exactly what it is, with its outcome wherever the record carries one. The fact sheet's language never upgrades a claim into a conclusion.
5. **Professional scope.** The sweep covers the professional and financial record the commitment touches. Family-court matters, health, and private life are out of scope by default — the same line psychological-profiling draws.
6. **This is not a consumer report.** Where the law regulates background checks — employment, credit, housing — regulated channels, consent, and disclosure rules apply (in the United States, the Fair Credit Reporting Act and state law, which also restrict uses of arrests and credit history). This skill flags that boundary; it does not satisfy it, and nothing in a fact sheet is legal advice.
7. **Absence is stated, never celebrated.** A clean run reads "nothing found in the sources swept," with the sources listed. Sealed, expunged, and un-digitized records are invisible to any public sweep, and the fact sheet says so rather than implying a clean bill.
8. **Collect for the decision.** Sweep what the commitment needs and no more. The fact sheet names its sources and its date so the user can re-run it, act on it, or discard it.

## Output contract

Every run returns:

1. The commitment and the depth (Step 0).
2. The identity resolution: anchors used, the variant set, the candidate set with its confirm/eliminate outcomes, confidence, and the namesake log.
3. The screening log: the lists searched, the names, variants, entities, and addresses screened, and the date — with any hits resolved at the primary list or logged as namesakes.
4. The module findings — every item with its status, its source, and its date.
5. The red flags, graded for this commitment: blocks, must-explains, notes.
6. The inconsistencies: claims quoted against the record that contradicts or fails to support them.
7. The gaps: modules not run at this depth, sources unavailable, sealed, or paywalled, and anything identity resolution could not settle.
8. The handoff block: verified facts formatted for psychological-profiling's record tier.

## Limits

- **Public records are a sample with known holes.** Coverage varies by jurisdiction, digitization is uneven, and registries lag events. The fact sheet lists what was swept so the size of the hole is visible.
- **Identity resolution sometimes fails.** Common names and thin footprints can leave two people inseparable in public records. The honest output is "cannot resolve," never a best guess dressed as a finding.
- **Verification verifies claims, not character or the future.** A clean sheet is a floor — nothing in the public record contradicts proceeding — not a forecast. Judgment about the person belongs to the user and, for the approach, to psychological-profiling.
- **Records age.** A fact sheet is true of its sweep date. For a live commitment, re-run before signing; registries and dockets move.
- **The legal boundary varies.** What may be checked, and what a finding may be used for, differs by jurisdiction and by use — hiring most of all. The skill flags the boundary; a lawyer decides.
