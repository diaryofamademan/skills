# The sources, graded

Every module's sources: what they carry, how to reach them, and how far to trust them. Grades: **primary** (the record itself, kept by the body that made it), **official mirror** (a faithful copy of a primary record), **lead** (points to a primary source; never cited as the finding).

## Identity and entity registries — grade: primary

Secretaries of state and their equivalents keep the corporate record: entity status, formation date, registered agent, officers or managers where the jurisdiction files them, assumed names, merger and dissolution history. Coverage and search quality vary widely by state; some registries are free and complete, some are paywalled or name-search only. The registry is the primary source for what an entity is and who stands behind it on paper. Its limit is the paper: a registry shows who is listed, which is evidence about control, not proof of it. UCC filing indexes (state-level) are primary for secured-party claims against a business's assets. Court-name and DBA indexes at county level fill gaps the state registries miss.

Read the registry's filing history, not only its summary page: the timeline of formations, amendments, forfeitures, reinstatements, and name changes is a primary record in its own right, and former names are search keys for every later module. The charges register and UCC index name the secured lenders — primary, and often the only public trace of a company's real financial relationships. The LEI system (GLEIF) is primary for the identifier itself and for the parent relationships filers report into it. Mirrors and indexes — OpenCorporates above all — are graded **lead**: excellent for finding the entity and its number across jurisdictions, never cited as the finding; the registry behind the mirror is. Offshore and leak databases (the ICIJ Offshore Leaks database, OCCRP's Aleph) are likewise lead-grade: assembled from filings and leaks of varying provenance, they open lines of inquiry that close, if they close, at a registry or a filing.

For people, there is no primary public registry of identity in the United States, which is why this skill resolves identity by anchors (city, profession, employer, entities) rather than by lookup. Voter, property, and professional-license rolls serve as anchor sources where public: property records (county assessor/recorder, often free online) tie a name to a place and a transaction history; license rolls tie a name to a profession and a status.

## Digital-estate records — grade: primary for dates, mirror for content

Domain registration records are kept by the registries and registrars, not by the site owner, so a domain's creation date and its history of transfers are primary for when the name entered use — though not for who truly held it in each period, which registration privacy often hides. Public web archives (the Wayback Machine is the working example) hold dated copies of pages as they appeared: an official mirror of a site's past content, and the practical source for a first-appearance date. Account and profile creation dates shown by a platform are the platform's own record — primary for the account's age, and nothing more. Shared-infrastructure traces (hosting, analytics and advertising identifiers visible in a site's own published code) are read from the site itself: primary as published configuration, but only ever a lead about common operation — the pivot is worked at a registry or a filing before it becomes a finding.

## Court records — grade: primary as documents

Federal: PACER carries every federal docket — civil, criminal, bankruptcy — public and paywalled by the page, with fee caps and waivers that make a name search cheap but not free. CourtListener's RECAP archive is a large, free, growing mirror of PACER documents (grade: official mirror) and its search is the practical first pass; anything material gets confirmed against PACER itself. State courts vary from full free portals (a minority) through name-search-with-fees to courthouse-only. The docket is the primary source for what was filed and what was decided. Two reading rules from practice: the complaint is one side's document and reads like it; and the absence of a docket entry for a rumored case usually means the rumor, the jurisdiction, or the name is wrong — in that order of likelihood.

Bankruptcy is federal, so it is the most completely searchable financial record a person has: filings, schedules, discharges, dismissals, all on PACER, all public.

## Regulators and sanctions — grade: primary

Enforcement is published by the enforcer. Securities regulators (in the US, the SEC's litigation releases and administrative proceedings, on EDGAR and the agency site; FINRA's BrokerCheck for broker and advisor records, including disclosures and discipline) — primary, free, and searchable by name. Trade and consumer protection (the FTC's actions; state attorneys general's published actions), commodities (CFTC), banking regulators — same pattern. Sanctions: the OFAC Specially Designated Nationals list and the consolidated screening list are primary, free, and the one check that runs at every depth, because a true match is an absolute block and a false match is a namesake problem the two-identifier rule exists to solve. The direct route to them is OFAC's own search tool (sanctionssearch.ofac.treas.gov), run as a named step in its own right (Step 2) — the publisher's own search, not only an aggregator's copy of the lists. Cross-list aggregators (OpenSanctions is the working example) search sanctions, PEP, and debarment lists across jurisdictions in one pass and are graded **lead**: a hit is confirmed at the primary list it came from, and a negative is reported with its log (Step 2) rather than as a blanket clearance. Debarment and exclusion lists (SAM.gov exclusions, the World Bank's debarment list, and their equivalents) are primary for the fact of exclusion. Professional licensing boards (state-level, by profession) are primary for license status and discipline; a claimed license that a board has never issued is a contradiction from a primary source.

## Financial-distress records — grade: primary where they exist

Bankruptcy (above). Federal tax liens are filed publicly (county recorder, and the IRS lien record is searchable through them); state tax liens likewise vary. Civil judgments appear in the court record that produced them. Foreclosure filings are court or recorder records by jurisdiction. What does not exist as a public source, stated once more because the market pretends otherwise: credit reports, credit scores, bank balances, and personal debt schedules are consumer data behind the Fair Credit Reporting Act's permitted purposes. No lawful public sweep reaches them, and any vendor implying otherwise is selling inference as fact. One addition for crypto counterparties: token market data — price history against the launch price, liquidity, exchange listings and delistings, read from exchange pages and market aggregators — is graded **signal**: dated, labeled market data, a gauge that sharpens the record modules and never a finding on its own.

## Procurement records — grade: primary

Contract awards, tender results, and vendor registrations published by the buying body — a government procurement portal, a public tenders database, a large institution's vendor register. The buyer keeps the record and has no reason to flatter the seller, so an award is primary evidence of the thing registries cannot show: that the company has a real customer who really paid. That makes procurement records the first check on a claimed trading history and the strongest free evidence of real operations — ranked alongside the registry and filing evidence, above the subject's own claims, its press, and any aggregator. The limits are the buyer's side of the market: private buyers publish nothing, award values are sometimes framework ceilings rather than sums paid, and a registration as an approved vendor proves eligibility, not a single sale. Read the award, not the registration, wherever both exist.

## Claim-verification primaries — grade: primary, sometimes gated

Degrees: the institution's registrar is the primary source; the National Student Clearinghouse verifies most US degrees but operates as a consent-and-fee channel, which makes it a hiring-process tool rather than a public sweep source. The public version of the check is the institution's own published records — commencement lists, alumni directories where public, the institution's statements when a claim becomes news. Employment: the employer's own filings and publications (officer lists in securities filings, archived leadership pages, press releases) are primary for senior roles; for the rest, employment claims often return unverified, and the fact sheet says so. Licenses and certifications: the issuing board or body, primary and usually free. Publications, patents, and deals: the publisher, the patent office (USPTO, primary and free), and the counterparty's filings — a claimed role in a public deal that no filing mentions grades unverified at best.

## Adverse media — grade: lead

News archives (wire services, national and trade press, local papers near the subject's anchors) and broadcast transcripts. Media's grade is lead: it finds the story, and the record modules decide what the story was worth. The repetition rule: syndication and aggregation mean one original report can appear as dozens of hits; count sources by their original reporting, not by their headlines. Local coverage near an anchor city routinely carries what national search misses — court reporting, business journals, licensing-board write-ups.

## Vendor and on-chain sources — mixed grades

Vendor cyber checks read three kinds of source. Certification registers are primary for the certificate: ISO certificates are verifiable against the certification body's own register (a certificate number that no register carries is a contradicted claim); SOC 2 reports are not public documents at all — the claim to hold one is unverified until the user obtains the report as legitimately held material. Breach history is primary where a regulator or a state breach-notification register published it; breach-exposure services of the Have I Been Pwned kind are graded lead-to-mirror for the narrow question "was this domain's service breached, and when" — and are never a route to anyone's credentials, which are not public data under any framing. External posture (DMARC, SPF, DKIM records, TLS configuration, a security.txt) is read from public DNS and the vendor's own site: primary, because it is the vendor's own published configuration.

On-chain, the chain itself is the primary source: an address's history and a contract's control state are read from the ledger at a noted block. Explorers (Etherscan and its equivalents per chain) are official mirrors — the working interface to the ledger, graded as mirrors, with material readings confirmable against a second explorer or a node. The sanctions lists carry crypto addresses directly (OFAC's SDN entries include them), so address screening is primary-list work, not heuristic work. Everything beyond the ledger — clustering, "this wallet belongs to that exchange/person" labels, analytics-platform attributions — is inference produced by a vendor's model, graded lead at best, and labeled as inference in the fact sheet wherever it appears.

## Aggregators and people-search sites — grade: lead, lowest

Data brokers and people-search sites assemble public records with inference and present both in the same typeface. Their legitimate use in a sweep is exactly one: as a lead generator for namesakes, addresses, and associated entities to confirm or exclude at primary sources. Nothing from an aggregator is ever cited as a finding, and their credit, debt, and criminal-history claims are the least reliable text on the internet relative to the confidence with which they are printed.
