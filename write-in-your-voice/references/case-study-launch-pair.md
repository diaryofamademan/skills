# Case study — one launch, written two ways

A product launch (an open-source skill for decisions where other people get a move), November 2026. Same content, same day, two constructions. This pair is the evidence base for the drafting procedure and the shape gate.

## v1 — model-composed from the brief (rejected in full)

"New open-source skill: game theory for decisions. Most bad moves are not bad analysis. They are moves picked without solving for the other side's response. Eight steps to map the players, the payoffs, and the likely replies before you commit."

The person's verdict: "What the hell is this? Are you following the skills? None of this has any hook."

What is wrong, move by move:

- The opening is an announcement. "New open-source skill:" is a press-release line. Nothing in it is a reaction, a receipt, a question, or a verdict.
- The second sentence is a composed aphorism with mirrored structure ("not bad analysis / moves picked without solving"). Balanced pairs are a lecturer's device; they are not how this person talks on tape.
- No first person anywhere. Nobody is doing anything in the text.
- Not one sentence traces to anything the person has ever said. The draft was generated from the product description and then checked for voice afterward.

## The dictation

Offered the choice, the person dictated the explanation himself, answering a prompt of the founder-friend shape ("what's the moment this is for, what's the dumbest way people play it, what does the thing make you do instead"). His words, lightly trimmed: "Have you ever been in a high stakes situation, business wise, with multiple parties involved, and you're trying to optimize for the best outcome for you and your business, while also trying to understand all the parties involved and what outcomes they're optimizing for? Well, this is a skill that's designed for that. Game theory is basically the math of social interactions, the math of life. I built a skill to take the obtuse academic math and make it practical for my AI agents, to break down a standoff with the next best action and all the moves on the chessboard, because if life is chess, you want to make sure you're playing the best strategy."

## v2 — assembled from the dictation (approved)

Post 1: "Ever been in a high-stakes deal where every party at the table is optimizing for something different, and you have to work out what they all want before you can pick your own move?" Post 2: "That is game theory, the math of social interactions. I built a skill that makes the academic version practical: my AI agents break down any standoff, every player, every payoff, the next best action. If life is chess, play the best strategy."

Why it passes: the opening is his question, lightly tightened. Every load-bearing phrase ("math of social interactions," "next best action," "if life is chess, play the best strategy") is his. The structure (hook, interest, payoff, action) was applied to his sentences; it did not generate them. First person arrives exactly where he put it.

## The gate scores — why words are the wrong layer

The operation runs a mechanical vocabulary checker (the person's attested spoken and written corpus; unattested words are flagged). Scores on the two drafts:

| Draft | Words checked | Vocab flags | Person's verdict |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| v1, composed | 41 | 2 | Rejected in full |
| v2, from dictation | 77 | 3 | Approved |

The word gate scores the failure cleaner than the success. Both drafts also passed the operation's style description, which already banned aphorism cadence: the ban existed as prose and did not fire, because nothing in the pipeline checked shape. That is the whole argument for Procedure B (compose from sources, so shape is inherited) and for the shape gate (check the shape explicitly when a draft is reviewed). A voice system that only checks words will keep shipping plausible text its person rejects on sight.
