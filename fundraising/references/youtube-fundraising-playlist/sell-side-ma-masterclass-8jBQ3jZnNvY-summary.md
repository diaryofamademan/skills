# Sell-Side M&A Masterclass | Structuring a Formal Sale Process for Maximum Value

- **Speaker:** Paul Giannamore (investment banker)

## Summary (paraphrased)

Giannamore frames selling a business as a **negotiation process**, not a fixed-price transaction. Value is created at the bargaining table; the seller who runs a disciplined process and controls timing generally captures better price and terms than one who negotiates ad hoc.

**Leverage** is multidimensional: deadlines that bind buyers but not the seller, **optionality and competition** (more qualified bidders raise outcomes), and hidden factors such as necessity or desire. Sellers should **conceal** forced-sale pressure while learning whether a buyer faces defensive urgency, executive incentives, or synergy math that increases their willingness to pay. Leverage is often **perceptual**—both sides shape how strong they appear.

**Information** flows matter: listen in management meetings, research before outreach, and avoid tipping desperation. **Judgment and emotional detachment** keep owners from being their own worst negotiators; advisors set process while founders stay credible in sessions.

**Credibility** requires building real competition from day one, not inventing a last-minute auction. A **formal sell-side process** (typical middle-market **modified auction**) means proactively hunting a curated buyer universe rather than passively listing the company. The advisor publishes a **process letter**: NDAs, a consistent **CIM/teaser**, parallel **IOI** deadlines, pay-to-play rounds for management access, batched diligence Q&A, and revised **LOIs**. Serial one-off exploding offers let the buyer control pace and create **disparity of desire**—bad for sellers.

**Management meetings** are for rapport, damage control, and intelligence gathering—not hard selling. Sellers should not fall in love with one bidder before comparing economics. **LOI and exclusivity** shift leverage to the buyer; escrow, reps, caps, and no-shop terms should be negotiated **before** exclusivity. After LOI, sellers can use **acceptance time**: diligence spend and finite exclusivity work in the seller’s favor if they avoid panic on re-trades.

**One-buyer traps** and weak price discovery are recurring failure modes; parallel serious parties mirror the seed-fundraising lesson of racing for a second term sheet. The talk aligns with competitive capital processes: process control, verified momentum, and sprinting to signed documents and wired funds.
