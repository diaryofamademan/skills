# Mimetic desire: the market read

The lens Step 3 uses. Girard's theory is large; this file carries only the part the procedure needs — why desire converges, what convergence does to returns, and how a run checks the reading against facts.

## The model in brief

Desire, on Girard's account, is not a straight line from a person to a thing. It is a triangle: the subject wants the object because a **model** — a person, a firm, a crowd whose wanting is visible — wants it, or is seen to have it. We learn what to want by watching what others want. This is mimesis: imitation, not of actions, but of desire itself.

Two consequences matter for markets.

**Convergence.** Because models are shared — the same celebrated founders, the same visible winners, the same press — desire converges. Whole industries come to want the same market, the same feature, the same hire, at the same time, each entrant's conviction borrowed from the others' presence. The crowd then reads its own size as evidence: everyone is here, so here must be valuable. On the mimetic account that inference is exactly backwards as often as it is right: the crowd's presence proves the desire is imitated, and says nothing yet about whether the object can pay all the desirers.

**Rivalry.** When subjects converge on an object that cannot be shared — one market's profit pool, one position, one title — models become rivals. Rivals imitate each other ever more closely as they compete (same prices, same features, same claims), which is why competition tends to erase the differences it was fought over, and the returns with them. Thiel's "competition is for losers" is, on this account, not a slogan but a mechanism: mimetic rivalry is the engine that competes profit away.

## Running the read

Step 3's mimetic read answers three questions, briefly, from the market's facts:

1. **Whose desire is being copied here?** Name the models: the visible winner everyone cites, the category the press anointed, the adjacent success being transposed. If the venture's own plan names its model ("the X for Y"), the record notes it — a plan stated as an imitation has announced its end state.

2. **What is the crowd doing to the object?** Entry rate, margin direction, the sameness of the offers. Rising entry with falling margins and converging products is the mimetic signature. Rising entry with holding margins may be genuine demand growth instead — the read must say which pattern the facts show, and the limits file's warning stands: a lens that reads every crowd as imitation explains too much.

3. **Where is desire not looking?** The constructive half of the read. If desire is copied, then the un-copied want — the need nobody's model has blessed yet — is where a secret about people usually lives. The read names one or two unglamorous objects adjacent to the crowded one, as candidates for Step 1's secret or Step 5's beachhead.

## What the read is not

It is not a reading of any individual's soul. A rival founder's motives are their own; the read describes patterns in markets — convergence, rivalry, margin behavior — and cites market facts for them. It is not a claim that all imitation is folly: copying a proven desire is how most commerce works, and the copying verdict in Step 0 routes such ventures to the execution skills with respect, not contempt. And it is not a measurement: it grades as a lens in the evidence file, and a run that writes a mimetic conclusion without the market facts beside it has misused it.
