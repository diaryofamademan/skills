# The evidence

What this skill rests on, source by source, with grades. The rule of the house: practitioner and philosophical frames are labeled as what they are, industry data is graded as industry data, and the criticisms are stated at full strength, not in a weakened form the procedure can survive.

## The frame: Zero to One

**Source.** Peter Thiel with Blake Masters, *Zero to One: Notes on Startups, or How to Build the Future* (2014), developed from Thiel's Stanford course (CS183) lectures, which Masters's class notes first carried.

**Grade.** Practitioner and philosophical work. Thiel's standing is a record, not a method section: co-founder of PayPal and Palantir, the first outside investor in Facebook. The book argues from that record and from a philosophy of history — the four quadrants of optimism and definiteness — not from controlled study. Nothing in it is peer-reviewed, and the skill never cites it as evidence about a venture in front of a run. It supplies the questions: creation or copying, the secret, definite or indefinite, monopoly or competition, the seven questions, the beachhead, the power law.

**What the skill takes.** The procedure's skeleton, almost whole: the 0 to 1 test, the contrarian question, secrets about nature and about people, the definite plan, the four sources of durability, the seven questions, starting with a small market, and the power law as the closing read.

## The lens: mimetic desire

**Source.** René Girard, *Things Hidden Since the Foundation of the World* (French 1978; English 1987), and the body of work around it. The accessible modern secondary is Luke Burgis, *Wanting* (2021), which applies the theory to business and names its models and rivalries plainly.

**The claim, in one paragraph.** Desire is triangular: a subject wants an object because a model — a person, a crowd, a market — is seen to want it. Desire is therefore imitated, and imitation converges rivals onto the same objects, where they compete until the object's value, and the profit in it, is destroyed. Girard's wider system (the scapegoat mechanism, the founding of culture on mimetic crisis) is outside this skill's use and is not relied on.

**Grade.** Philosophical anthropology. Contested in anthropology and psychology, not falsifiable in the way a market claim is, and used here accordingly: as a noticing instrument for one pattern — imitation crowding a market — that a run then checks against the market's own facts (entry rates, margin direction, how rivals describe their reasons). The lens proposes; the evidence disposes.

## The power law's shape

**Source.** Industry analyses of venture returns, most commonly Horsley Bridge's long-running fund data as reported in the venture literature, with Correlation Ventures' dataset pointing the same way.

**Grade.** Industry data, not peer-reviewed, and era-dependent. The consistent finding across datasets is the shape: returns are heavily concentrated, with a small share of investments producing the majority of total returns, and the median venture investment returning little. The skill relies on the shape only. Any precise split quoted in a run must carry its dataset and its dates, or be struck.

## The criticisms, at full strength

- **Survivorship bias.** The frame is generalized from a handful of extraordinary winners, several of them the author's own. The population of ventures that held secrets, planned definitely, and sought monopolies — and failed anyway — is not sampled. A method built on winners can mistake the winners' decorations for their causes. The skill's structural answer (the frame asks, the venture's evidence answers) reduces the exposure; it does not remove it, and this paragraph stays in the package so no reader has to take the reduction on faith.

- **Definite plans fail definitely.** The case for optionality is real: markets that shift under a plan punish commitment, and some durable companies were built by fast following and kept options. The definite/indefinite read is the frame's most ideological step, and a run records it as the frame's demand, not as a finding of fact about the venture.

- **Monopoly as a goal invites its abuses.** Durable advantage earned by being better is one thing; durability bought by acquisition of rivals, regulatory capture, or lock-in of people who cannot leave is another, and the book's frame does not police the boundary. This skill does: the monopoly read must name the source of durability plainly, and lock-in of the exit-less is recorded as what it is.

- **Mimetic theory explains too much.** A lens that can read any crowd as imitation can also read a genuinely growing market as imitation. The check is in the market's facts: if margins hold as entry rises, the crowd may be demand, not mimesis, and the record must say so.

## What no source here can do

None of these sources can certify a venture. The book is one man's argument, the lens is one philosopher's system, the data describe other people's portfolios in other decades. A run's verdict stands on the venture's own evidence or it does not stand — which is why the output contract shows the evidence beside every claim.
