# The seven questions

Step 4 of the procedure, worked slowly. Thiel's claim is that every great venture can answer all seven, and that a venture which fails one it cannot repair will usually fail outright. The run's discipline is plainer: each question is answered from evidence or marked unanswered, and the unanswered ones are findings, not embarrassments.

## 1. Engineering

**The question.** Can the venture create breakthrough technology — roughly an order of magnitude better on the dimension that matters — instead of an incremental improvement?

**What a real answer looks like.** A named dimension, a measured multiple, and the reason the multiple is possible now (the secret, usually a nature secret, does the work). "Ten times cheaper per transaction, because the cost that dominated the old way fell out of the system" is an answer. "A better experience" is not.

**Failure shape.** The improvement answer: twenty percent better on a dimension buyers already compare, inside a market that will compete the twenty percent away within a cycle.

## 2. Timing

**The question.** Why is now exactly the right time — what changed?

**What a real answer looks like.** A dated change in the world: a cost curve crossing, a regulation landing or lifting, a behavior reaching a threshold, an incumbent's commitment becoming a trap. The answer names what was false five years ago that is true now, or what will be false in five years that is true now — the window, and its edges.

**Failure shape.** "The market is growing." Growth is a climate, not a timing answer; it was true for every failed entrant in the same market too.

## 3. Monopoly

**The question.** Does the plan start with a large share of a small market?

**What a real answer looks like.** A first market small enough to take whole (Step 5 names it), a reason the venture takes it rather than shares it, and the durability sources from Step 3 already visible in embryo. Thiel's warning rides along: a venture that defines its market hugely to look small in it ("one percent of a trillion") is hiding from this question, and one that defines its market tiny to look dominant is doing the same trick in reverse. The record states the market both ways when the definition is doing work.

**Failure shape.** The large-market opening: a plan whose first move needs a share of a big market it has no mechanism to take.

## 4. People

**The question.** Does the venture have the right team — the specific people, on the specific work?

**What a real answer looks like.** Names against the venture's two or three hardest jobs (usually: build the breakthrough, sell the first market), evidence those people have done the adjacent thing, and the gaps named as gaps with a plan or a bet recorded. Thiel's own marker is worth keeping: a team that is right can usually say why each member is irreplaceable at their job; a team described in interchangeable adjectives is a warning.

**Failure shape.** The résumé answer: impressive histories that do not touch the venture's actual hardest jobs.

## 5. Distribution

**The question.** Does the venture have a way to deliver its offer to its people — not just to build it?

**What a real answer looks like.** A named channel with its economics: who is reached, at what cost, by what motion the venture has run or credibly can run, and why the channel fits the beachhead. Distribution answers are where definite planning shows: a channel is a commitment, and "virality" is the indefinite answer wearing a growth costume.

**Failure shape.** The product-will-spread answer. Superior products that nobody could reach fill the record of every era; this question exists because of them.

## 6. Durability

**The question.** Will the venture's position still stand in ten to twenty years?

**What a real answer looks like.** Step 3's durability sources projected: which of proprietary technology, network effects, scale, and branding will be stronger in a decade, what the strongest rival's best move does to them, and the honest sentence about what could end the position — a platform shift, the secret diffusing, the founders' edge expiring. The answer is a projection and is labeled one.

**Failure shape.** The moat adjective: "strong brand," "sticky product," with no mechanism by which time makes the position stronger rather than weaker.

## 7. Secret

**The question.** Has the venture identified a unique opportunity that others have not seen?

**What a real answer looks like.** Step 1's output, carried in whole: the one-sentence secret, its kind, its evidence, its disprover. This question is last because it judges the other six: an engineering answer without a secret is usually an improvement; a beachhead without a secret is usually a hope.

**Failure shape.** The opportunity everyone sees. If the chance is on the cover of the industry press, the run asks what the venture knows that the cover story got wrong — and if the answer is nothing, the question is marked accordingly.
