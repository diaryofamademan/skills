# Worked example — a fox explains game theory

One public series taken through the whole procedure: a launch carousel for a business-concepts explainer, drawn as a newspaper-style comic strip.

The lead is a fox. The counterparty is a bear. The series teaches one idea per page: how to read what the other side of a deal actually wants.

**Flags for this job: `headline` on, `caption` on, `signature` on.** The carousel is a deck format, and the series' deck work runs with all three text layers on. The images were still generated text-free; every word on the shipped pages was set as type at assembly.

## The bible, in summary

**Series rules.** The cast rule: animals and robots only, never humans; the other side of any negotiation is cast as an animal. The style block, carried word for word into every prompt, fixes the register: a minimalist mid-century newspaper comic strip — simple confident ink lines, sparse backgrounds, flat limited colours, gentle deadpan humour. The frame spec: wide landscape panels, re-mounted after generation on one cream paper at a fixed padding, with one near-black border weight for the whole set.

**The fox (the lead).** A fox with a warm orange coat, cream chest and muzzle, pointed ears, and a full tail with a cream tip. Slender build; he sits and stands upright and thinks with one paw at his chin. His eyes are the identity trait: small, plain black dots under a single short brow line, which is where his deadpan lives. Role: the point of view — the one who reads the game.

**The bear (the standing counterparty).** A large brown bear with a tan muzzle and small round ears, in a grey business suit with a white shirt and dark tie. He stands at about twice the fox's size in frame. His eyes sit closed to dashes: he gives nothing away. Role: the buyer, the other side — whoever sits across the table in any piece this series makes.

**The supporting cast.** Entered in the bible before they appeared: an owl (the watcher), a tortoise (the slow strategy), a squirrel (the hoarder), a parrot and a raccoon (the sponsor's people), and three small round robots (the fox's agents, one in professor's glasses). Each got an entry and an anchor from its first approved panel.

## Three panels

**Panel one — the table.** A long plain negotiation table in a sparse room. The fox leans forward on one side, one paw raised, asking. Across from him the bear sits with arms folded, eyes shut, giving nothing away. Headline: "What do they really want?" Caption: "Not what they say. What they really want." The panel's point: the stated want is not the real one.

**Panel three — the buyer.** An office desk. The fox presents a paper to the bear, who is not looking at him at all — the bear gazes past him at a trophy and a medal on a shelf. The fox notices, mid-gesture, eyebrows up. Headline: "He didn't want efficiency." Caption: "He wanted a promotion." The panel's point: the counterparty's real prize is personal, and it is visible if you look where he looks.

**Panel five — the chessboard.** The fox alone at a chessboard, paw on chin, eyes on the far side of the board. Headline: "Business is chess." Caption: "Know the moves. Think several steps ahead." The panel's point: the game is read several moves deep. This panel later became one of the fox's standing anchors — a clean, solo, full-body drawing of the lead.

## The chain, in practice

The eight panels were generated in sequence, not in parallel. Each approved panel joined the working reference set before the next was drawn.

The clearest case is the bear. He appears at the table in panel one, behind the desk in panel three, and shaking paws over a signed document in panel six. Panel three was generated with panel one in its reference set, and panel six with both — so the suit, the tan muzzle, the folded-arm bulk, and his scale against the fox hold across all three cuts, and the desk in panel six is recognisably the desk from panel three.

## One drift failure

The closing panel — the fox reading a large book under a tree, the owl on a branch, the tortoise passing — came back wrong in the eyes. The fox's eyes were large and glossy, with white highlights and pupils, and the owl had drifted the same way. In a deadpan strip, that is a different fox: the panel failed the drift check on the eyes block, outright.

The panel was regenerated with the anchor set and a strengthened style block that names the trait — small plain black dot eyes, no highlights. The shipped panel carries the standard dot eyes on both animals. The failure and the wording that fixed it went into the fox's drift notes, and the eyes block of the drift check carries its own section in this skill because of panels like this one.

## Assembly

The approved panels were cropped to their drawn borders and re-mounted on the one cream paper: same padding, same redrawn border weight, same page size for all seven pages. With the flags on, each page then took its headline (one line, centred, the type size flexing to fit the line), its caption (italic, directly under the headline), and the signature block in the footer — the series' signature block: its three public handles and the page number, fixed once in the bible, identical on every page.

The run closed by promoting two panels into the standing anchors — the chessboard panel for the fox, and a crop of the negotiation panel for the bear — so the next piece in the series starts from shipped, approved drawings of both leads.
