# The dossier: format and worked example

## The format

Two layers. The card is read in 30 seconds; the long read sits under it for the night before. Fixed sections, in this order. Anything the evidence cannot fill is written as unknown, never padded. Every dimension score states which pile carried it — performed, chosen, or both — and a score carried only by performed evidence is flagged on its face; where a trait splits into facets that score differently, the facets are written separately, never averaged into one line.

**Header** — the person's name, the decision this dossier serves, the date, the evidence window (the dates of the sources), and a stale flag on any score whose evidence predates a known formation event or the window the decision needs.

**The 30-second card** — five lines, no more: what they want most; the lever that moves them; the move that kills it; how they decide; their tell. If a line cannot be compressed from the scored evidence below, it does not go on the card.

**The person in their own words** — three short quotes, each with its source and date, chosen because they carry the read: a want, a decision rule, a tell.

**What they want** — motives in order, values from the record, each line with its evidence and confidence.

**Household and life structure** — partner, children, living setup, and how time outside work is spent, each scored from the record; an absence in a dense record written as a finding, an absence in a thin record written as unknown. Never silently omitted.

**Formation and values** — the upbringing and turning points the record supports, the story the person repeats about how they were made, and the values their trade-offs price, ranked by what each one beat. Scores carry their tag — temperament, formation, or choice — where the tag does work.

**How they decide** — complexity level, speed, what counts as proof, whose opinion gets a vote, and the disagreement rule applied where words and record part ways.

**How they talk** — directness, typical length, data or story first, signature phrases and topics they return to, topics they avoid.

**Pressure points** — what they fear losing, their known fears and aversions, the tender spots, the over-investments, the blind spots, and the gap between the self they present and the record. Each with evidence and confidence.

**Persuasion levers** — for each lever: the move, the dimension it answers, and how it backfires if detected or overused.

**What to say** — the angles, the proof, and the framing that fit this person, each tied to the dimension it answers.

**What to avoid** — the framings, words, and moves their record says will fail, with the evidence.

**Situation playbooks** — one short playbook per arena actually in play: cold first contact, a pitch or investor meeting, a negotiation, a social setting, a recruiting conversation, a conflict or repair moment. Each playbook: their likely posture there, your opening move, the proof that lands in that arena, and the one thing never to do in that room.

**Predictions** — two or three, specific and checkable, dated, written before contact, each carrying its base-rate line — what the obvious call is, and why the profile's read differs — and recorded in the prediction ledger; once ten ledger predictions have been scored, the section also states the profiler's running record.

**Gaps** — dimensions not scored and questions the evidence cannot answer.

## Worked example — a composite from published research

This example is assembled only from findings published in the research literature, to show the format filled without inventing a subject. The subject is a composite political leader drawn from the published at-a-distance record; every scored claim below traces to a published source named in `the-evidence.md`.

**Header** — Subject: a composite head of government. Decision served: preparing a head of government for a first summit meeting. Evidence window: the subject's speeches and interviews across the decade before the meeting, as scored in the published literature.

**The person in their own words** — In a real dossier this section carries three verbatim quotes with sources. In this composite it is left as the template it is, because a composite has no words of its own. On a real subject, never skip it: the quotes are the section the reader remembers, and the check that the profile is built from the person and not from the profiler.

**What they want** — Motive scoring of this leader type in Winter's published presidential work shows power imagery leading, achievement second, affiliation trailing (moderate confidence for the type; the published scores behind this ordering include Kennedy highest on power, Carter highest on achievement, George H. W. Bush highest on affiliation among US presidents scored from inaugural addresses). A power-led leader hears every proposal as a question about standing: who gains, who is seen to gain.

**How they decide** — Hermann's published trait work on leaders of this profile shows high belief in own control and low-to-middle conceptual complexity: events are things the leader makes happen, and situations are read in fairly black-and-white terms until experience complicates them. Proof, for this leader, is a demonstration of resolve by the other side, not a paper argument.

**How they talk** — Direct, declarative, low hedging in public; complexity rises in interview answers and drops in rally speech, so the interview record is the scoring source, per Hermann's rule that spontaneous material outranks prepared text.

**What to say** — Frame proposals as moves this leader visibly leads. Give them a win that can be described in one sentence to their own audience. Put the trade-off on the table early and plainly; a low-complexity read hears a hidden trade-off discovered later as a trick.

**What to avoid** — Do not frame anything as the leader following another's plan. Do not rely on affiliation appeals — shared history, personal warmth — as the load-bearing argument; the motive ordering says they are decoration for this leader, not structure. Do not present a many-sided analysis in the room; send it in writing beforehand if it must exist.

**Predictions** — (1) The leader will open by testing resolve: an early, small demand made firmly, to see whether it moves. (2) Concessions framed as the leader's own initiative will be accepted at a higher rate than identical concessions framed as compliance. Both predictions are stated so a summit's record could score them.

**Gaps** — Emotional stability unscored: the public record of a head of government is too managed to show bad days honestly. Affiliation read is type-level, not individual. Both gaps are named rather than filled, which is the format working as intended.

## The historical note behind the format

The one-page, decision-first shape is deliberate. The CIA center's profiles were written for a president preparing to sit across from the person profiled — Carter's Camp David use is the documented case — and a profile that cannot be read in the car on the way to the meeting fails its only job. Length is a defect in a dossier, not a virtue.
