# The evidence, graded

Every method this skill uses, with what stands behind it and how far it can be trusted. Grades: **strong** (replicated or field-tested at scale), **moderate** (real method, real checks, narrower base), **tradition** (used by serious practitioners, never validated like a test), **weak** (checked and found wanting).

## The CIA tradition — grade: tradition, with moderate parts

Jerrold Post, a psychiatrist, founded the CIA's Center for the Analysis of Personality and Political Behavior in the early 1970s (a pilot effort began in the mid-1960s) and directed it for 21 years. The center built psychological profiles of foreign leaders for presidents, to prepare for summits and crises. Its best-known product: the profiles of Egypt's Anwar Sadat and Israel's Menachem Begin prepared for President Jimmy Carter at the 1978 Camp David talks, which Carter credited in his memoirs as a real help in the negotiations.

Post's 2003 edited volume, *The Psychological Assessment of Political Leaders*, published the center's approach in the open: an interdisciplinary read of a leader located in their political, historical, and cultural context, built from speeches, interviews, memoirs, and the record. That structure — context first, words second, behavior throughout — is the spine of this skill's Steps 1 and 2.

The honest limit: the center's case-study core was never validated the way a psychometric test is validated. Its successes are documented cases, not measured accuracy. This skill borrows its structure and its seriousness, and states its confidence accordingly.

The tradition's early exhibit is also its warning. The CIA's psychiatric study of Fidel Castro, written about two years after he took power, reads with total confidence in clinical language — neurotic, narcissistic, unstable — with no evidence shown and no way to check a word of it. Whatever its value to its readers, as method it is exactly what this skill refuses to produce: labels without receipts.

## Leadership Trait Analysis (Hermann) — grade: moderate

Margaret Hermann's system scores seven traits from a leader's spontaneous verbal material: belief in their own ability to control events, need for power, conceptual complexity, self-confidence, task orientation, distrust of others, and in-group bias. Each trait has defined verbal markers — conceptual complexity, for example, is scored from words that mark shades of gray (sometimes, perhaps, possibly) against words that mark black-and-white thinking (always, absolutely, forever).

The method has been applied to more than 500 heads of state and other leaders, published across four decades, and is used to train analysts in the intelligence and defense communities. Two of its design choices matter for this skill: it requires *spontaneous* material (interviews, press conferences) over prepared speeches, because prepared text may be staff-written; and it produces scores that can be compared across people and checked against what they later did.

## Motive scoring (Winter) — grade: moderate

David Winter adapted the Thematic Apperception Test's motive imagery scoring to running text: any speech, interview, or letter can be scored for three motives — achievement (concern with meeting a standard of excellence), power (concern with having impact on others), and affiliation (concern with warm relationships). His 1991 integrated system for scoring motives in running text reported interscorer reliability, temporal stability, and convergence with the original test-based scores.

Applied to leaders, the scores have predicted behavior in if/then ways: high power imagery in leaders' speech has tracked with conflict escalation and decisions for war; among US presidents scored from their inaugural addresses, the highest achievement score was Jimmy Carter's, the highest affiliation score George H. W. Bush's, and the highest power score John F. Kennedy's. Winter's own summary of the field is the honest one: motives can be measured at a distance, reliably — and they predict behavior only in contingent, if/then ways, never as certainties. This skill's prediction step is built to that standard.

## Integrative complexity (Suedfeld) — grade: moderate

Peter Suedfeld's scoring measures how a person structures their thinking: whether they see one side or several, and whether they connect the sides into a whole. It is scored from paragraphs of natural speech or writing with published coding rules and reliability. Its best-known finding: leaders' complexity drops measurably in the run-up to surprise attacks and crises, which makes it a state signal as much as a trait — useful in a dossier both as a thinking-style read and as a stress indicator when a person's recent words score lower than their baseline.

## The Big Five — grade: strong (as a map)

The five-factor model — openness, conscientiousness, extraversion, agreeableness, neuroticism — is the most replicated structure in personality science, validated across languages and decades, and the only personality framework this skill scores. The older direction stands: MBTI's four scales correlate with four of the Big Five traits (McCrae and Costa, 1989) but its type categories and dichotomies lack support, and it has no measure of neuroticism; it survives here only as rough communication vocabulary. Systems built on Jungian function stacks have no comparable validation and are excluded.

## Reading personality from records and language — grade: moderate, with a known ceiling

Kosinski, Stillwell and Graepel (PNAS, 2013) showed that digital records — in their study, Facebook Likes from about 58,000 volunteers — predict personality traits and other attributes well above chance. Youyou, Kosinski and Stillwell (PNAS, 2015), with about 86,000 volunteers, compared computer judgments against human ones: the computer's Big Five judgments from Likes correlated with people's self-reports at r = 0.56, against r = 0.49 for judgments by the person's own friends; about 100 Likes sufficed to beat an average human judge, and about 300 to rival a spouse.

Two honest caveats shape how this skill uses that research. First, Likes are behavior, not language; reads from *text alone* land lower, roughly r = 0.3 to 0.4 per trait in the language-based literature. Second, these are group-level accuracies: they say a read is better than a guess, not that any single read is right. Hence the skill's rules: traits as ranges, confidence in words, behavior ranked above language, and predictions scored after contact.

## Matching the message to the person — grade: strong in ads, unproven in outreach

Matz, Kosinski, Nave and Stillwell (PNAS, 2017) ran three field experiments reaching more than 3.5 million people: ad appeals matched to the audience's extraversion or openness produced up to 40 percent more clicks and 50 percent more purchases than mismatched or unpersonalized appeals. Hirsh, Kang and Bodenhausen (2012) found the same direction under controlled conditions: personality-matched messages were rated more persuasive.

That is the strongest evidence that profiling a person improves what you say to them — and it is advertising evidence, at audience scale, on two traits. Whether matching moves replies in one-to-one business outreach is a hypothesis. This skill treats it as one: Step 5 applies matching, Step 4's predictions and Step 6's scoring are how a user finds out whether it works in their own batches.

## Formation, values, and the nature–nurture split — grade: moderate

Twin studies put the heritability of Big Five traits at roughly 40 to 60 percent (Jang, Livesley and Vernon, 1996; the Minnesota twin work associated with Bouchard). Temperament is real, and this skill treats it as the floor a profile starts from. The same studies carry the surprise that shapes this skill's formation layer: the shared family environment — the upbringing siblings have in common — accounts for almost none of the adult trait variance. Upbringing's measurable fingerprint is not on the traits. It runs through values, narratives, skills, and wounds, which is why formation is scored as its own layer and never used to explain a trait score after the fact.

Traits also move. The maturity principle (Roberts and colleagues, from longitudinal studies across the life span): conscientiousness and emotional stability rise with age and with adult roles — work, partnership, responsibility. A dossier is therefore dated evidence about a person at a time, and old scores decay.

The formation layer's positive method is narrative identity (McAdams): the life story a person constructs and repeats — its turning points, its heroes and villains, what the suffering meant — predicts well-being and generativity beyond trait scores. For a profiler the repeated self-narrative is doubly useful: it is evidence about formation, and it is the story the person's current choices are organized to keep true, which makes it predictive in its own right.

Values are scored on Schwartz's map: ten basic values (self-direction, stimulation, hedonism, achievement, power, security, tradition, conformity, benevolence, universalism), validated across more than 80 countries, which predict behavior specifically in trade-off situations — when two values collide and one must be paid for. That is the only place a value's rank is observable, so this skill scores values from costly choices and marks the rest as claimed.

## Reading personality from the visual record — grade: mixed, by method

**Environments and their residue — grade: moderate.** Gosling, Ko, Mannarelli and Morris, "A Room With a Cue" (*Journal of Personality and Social Psychology*, 2002): observers who never met the occupants rated Big Five traits from offices and bedrooms alone, and their ratings showed both consensus and real accuracy against self- and peer-reports — strongest for openness and conscientiousness, moderate for extraversion, weakest for agreeableness and emotional stability. The cues observers used were partly the valid ones: neatness and order tracked conscientiousness; distinctive decoration and a variety of books and media tracked openness. Private spaces carried more signal than offices. This is the strongest visual method available to a profiler, and it works because an environment is accumulated choice — the record, in physical form.

**Self-presentation in photographs — grade: modest.** Zero-acquaintance research on full-body photographs (Naumann, Vazire, Rentfrow and Gosling, 2009, and the literature around it) finds observers judging extraversion, conscientiousness, and openness from photographs at real but small accuracy, with extraversion read best — largely from presentation: posture, expression, clothing, and how social the photograph is. The signal is what the person chose to show, which is why this skill files photographs as behavior, at the tier their provenance earns.

**Facial structure — grade: weak.** The modern physiognomy is the facial width-to-height ratio (Carré and McCormick, 2008, linking it to aggression in men). The 2015 meta-analysis (Geniole and colleagues) pooled the literature at r ≈ 0.16 for threat behavior in men, with signs of publication bias; later large-sample work found the ratio's basic sex difference to be within measurement noise, and a worldwide sample of nearly 5,000 people found no association with aggression. The criminality-from-faces classifier (Wu and Zhang, 2016) was never peer-reviewed, and its critics showed the model had learned which dataset a photograph came from. Facial structure is excluded from this skill's scoring.

**AI inference from faces — grade: contested, and not individual evidence.** Wang and Kosinski (2018) reported a neural network distinguishing sexual orientation from dating-site photographs well above human judges; the published rebuttal (Agüera y Arcas, Mitchell and Todorov) showed that a short checklist of grooming, eyewear, facial hair, and camera-angle differences matched the network's accuracy — the model was reading self-presentation, not faces. Kosinski's political-orientation work (2021) drew the same critique; a 2024 follow-up using standardized photographs, with presentation and expression controlled, still found a small association (r ≈ 0.2), which the field continues to dispute, including over head-tilt artifacts. Whatever survives that dispute is a group-level statistic. It is not evidence about a person, and this skill does not score from it. The part of the finding that is solid — presentation is readable — is already covered by the self-presentation method above.

**Health conditions from lifestyle imagery — grade: no validated method.** No published, validated method infers a health condition — addiction, illness — from photographs of a person's social life. A feed can document behavior (a nightlife-centered social life, drinking in frame) with the same standing as any other behavior in the record; the step from behavior to condition is a diagnosis, barred by the Goldwater boundary below as well as by the absence of evidence. Conditions enter a dossier only in the subject's own stated words.

## The public legal record — grade: strong as documents, judgment as evidence

A docket is a primary document: that a case was filed, by whom, and how it ended are matters of record, not reporting. Federal filings sit in PACER (public, paywalled by the page) with a large free mirror in CourtListener's RECAP archive; state coverage varies from full free portals to courthouse-only. Regulator and licensing actions are published by the agencies themselves. As documents, this layer is the hardest evidence the gather touches.

The inference from the documents is where judgment enters, and three cautions govern it. **Base rates:** litigation is ordinary in business life — a founder or executive with one suit across a career sits near the reference class, not outside it. **Selection:** cases that settle quietly leave thin records, so the visible residue over-represents disputes that could not be settled; a docket shows the fights, not the temperament that avoided the others. **Allegation is not finding:** a complaint states what one side claims; only outcomes — judgment, dismissal, sanction, settlement — record what a process concluded, which is why the skill weights outcomes and treats being sued as context. Two boundary facts complete the grading: family-court records are sealed or restricted in many jurisdictions, and credit reports are consumer reports under federal law with permitted purposes — they are not public records, and no lawful public source carries a person's credit or debts. Bankruptcy filings, judgments, and liens are public, and they are the financial layer's honest extent.

## Attribution pivots — grade: strong as mechanics, judgment as tradecraft

Reverse image search and username pivoting are matching engines, not inference: a search that returns the same photograph on two services has established that the photograph is on both — nothing more, and nothing less. The judgment sits in what the match means. A subject reusing their own headshot across their own accounts is the common case, and the intended find; it is stronger attribution than a shared username, because people share names and handles by coincidence, while a placed headshot is a claim of identity. The failure modes are known: employers and event pages reuse a speaker's headshot, scraper and impersonator accounts copy photographs wholesale, and stock or generated avatars match strangers. Hence the skill's rule: a match opens an account for inspection, and a second, independent tie — employer, city, cross-link — is required before the account's material is scored as the subject's own. What never changes: the matched image is a pivot, not evidence about the person. Its content is read only under the visual-record methods above.

## Criminal profiling — grade: weak

The FBI's offender profiling, the kind television made famous, was built from interviews with 36 incarcerated offenders and typologies (organized versus disorganized) that were never documented well enough for outsiders to replicate. Snook, Cullen and colleagues' 2008 review, "The Criminal Profiling Illusion," examined the performance studies and found self-described profilers barely outperforming comparison groups, including non-experts, and concluded criminal profiling lacked the theoretical grounding and empirical support to be used as an investigative tool. A UK survey of 184 cases (Copson and Holloway, 1997) found profiling directly identified the offender in under 3 percent of cases.

This skill takes one thing from that world: profilers write their read down before the outcome is known, which is the only reason anyone can score them at all. The inference habits — confident narrative built on intuition and typology — are what the evidence file exists to keep out.

## Analytic discipline — grade: strong as practice

Two published standards govern how this skill handles its own uncertainty. Richards Heuer's *Psychology of Intelligence Analysis* (CIA Center for the Study of Intelligence, 1999) documents the cognitive traps of this work — seeing patterns early, then defending them — and the structured counter, Analysis of Competing Hypotheses: list the rival explanations, and grade evidence by how well it separates them, not by how well it fits your favorite. The US intelligence community's ICD 203 analytic standards require the same habits in finished work: sourcing shown, uncertainty expressed, alternatives considered. Every dossier claim carrying its evidence, confidence, and disconfirmer is those standards, applied to one person. The scoring reference's base-rate check belongs to the same tradition: a pattern that merely matches its reference class is background, not a finding.

## The ethics boundary — the Goldwater rule

In 1964, a magazine published psychiatrists' armchair diagnoses of presidential candidate Barry Goldwater. The American Psychiatric Association's response became the Goldwater rule: a psychiatrist may not offer a professional opinion about a public figure they have not examined. The rule exists because at-a-distance clinical labels carry authority they have not earned and cannot be checked. This skill's version is broader and simpler: no clinical labels at all, for anyone — behavior, words, and choices, each with its evidence shown.
