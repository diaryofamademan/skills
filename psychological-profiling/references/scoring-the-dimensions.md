# Scoring the dimensions

How to turn a person's words and record into scores the dossier can use. Every score is stated the same way: the read, the evidence for it, a confidence level, and the disconfirmer. If you cannot name a disconfirmer, you do not have a read — you have an impression. Leave the dimension unscored.

## Confidence, in words

- **High** — several independent sources, including spontaneous speech or behavior, point the same way, and nothing in the record contradicts them.
- **Moderate** — the sources point one way but are few, or all of one kind (all prepared text, all second-hand).
- **Low** — one source, or sources that conflict. A low-confidence read may still be worth stating, labeled, when the decision turns on it.

Never convert confidence to a percentage. The method cannot support one, and a number would claim a precision the evidence does not have.

## Base rates — check the reference class

Before a score is called a finding, ask: against what reference class is this unusual? Most founders score high on achievement imagery. Most executives show high belief in their own control. Most public figures' records over-sample both warmth and conflict. A score that merely matches its reference class is background, not signal — say so in the dossier and move on. The clearest field errors in this skill's testing were base-rate errors: a performed surface read as a trait, and a person's worst week read as a stability score. The check takes one sentence per dimension and prevents both.

## Performed versus chosen evidence

Before anything is scored, file every piece of evidence in one of two piles.

- **Performed** — what the person's role demands they show: stage talks, panels they were invited to, launch and announcement posts, the persona their job requires. Performed evidence proves capability. It does not prove preference.
- **Chosen** — what the person does when the format and the structure are theirs to pick: the formats they create for themselves, the organizations and routines they design, the projects they run with no audience, what they keep doing after the spotlight moves on. Chosen evidence is where traits live.

Rules:

1. A trait score is carried by chosen evidence. Performed evidence may support it at low weight, and the dossier says when it did.
2. When the two piles point opposite ways, report both and score from the chosen pile. The split is itself a finding — it usually marks the gap between the person and their persona.
3. When a trait splits into facets that score differently, score the facets separately. An average that hides a split is a wrong answer with good manners.
4. Absence is evidence when the record is dense. A person who documents everything and never shows a social life has told you something about their social life. Absence in a thin record tells you nothing.
5. The discount caps confidence; it does not move the score. When both piles point the same way, score the agreement at full strength and let confidence rise with the agreement. Pulling an agreed score toward the middle to be safe is an error this skill has made in the field: a subject's warmth was discounted because it was also his job, even though his chosen life agreed, and a genuinely extraverted man came out middle-high.

### The extraversion sub-protocol

Extraversion is the trait performed evidence corrupts worst, because public-facing jobs are extraversion performances. Score it from these, in this order, and never from audience size or stage count:

1. **Self-description in unscripted speech.** Decisive when it exists. Search for it; do not assume it exists.
2. **Chosen formats.** Solo or one-on-one formats the person built for themselves — a lone livestream, a long conversation show, writing — weighed against group formats they merely accepted, such as panels, tours, and stages. Broadcasting is not socializing: a person alone in a room, talking at an audience, is in a solo format.
3. **Built structure.** The working life the person designed when they could design it: team size, meeting load, how much of the day is people. People build the social dosage they can stand.
4. **Absence.** In a dense record, missing social content — no entourage, no party surface, no group socializing — counts toward the introverted end.

### Score the facets, and let them split by context

Extraversion is three scores wearing one coat: **sociability** (seeking company), **assertiveness** (dominance — taking charge of rooms and decisions), and **energy** (pace and activity level). They split in real people, and they split by setting: a person can be dominant in every group room and shy in a courtship, relentless at work and quiet at home. Score each facet from the sub-protocol's sources, score by context where the record shows a split, and never average a dominant-but-shy subject into a meaningless middle. The same applies, with less ceremony, to any trait the record shows splitting by arena.

### Sampling warnings

A public record over-samples conflict, novelty, and crisis, and under-samples routine, decency, and private life. Three consequences. Agreeableness scored from public material is capped at low confidence unless chosen evidence carries it. Emotional stability is scored from duration and recovery, never from the single worst episode. And any trait whose evidence is mostly coverage rather than the person's own output is flagged in the dossier as coverage-shaped.

## The visual record, scored

- **Environment.** Read rooms and backgrounds the way Gosling's observers did: neatness, order, and condition for conscientiousness; distinctive decoration and variety of books, media, and objects for openness; social density — seating for groups, shared spaces — as a moderate extraversion cue. Private spaces outrank staged ones, for the same reason chosen outranks performed.
- **Self-presentation.** Clothing, grooming, displayed objects, who appears in frame and how often: chosen behavior, scored like the record. In photographs, how social the images are — people present, group settings — is the most reliable visual extraversion cue, because it counts chosen company rather than performed presence.
- **On-camera behavior.** Energy, posture, and pace in unscripted, self-chosen formats count as behavior. In formats the role imposed, they are performed evidence and capped.
- **The personal feed is required sampling.** Read the subject's own social accounts, not only the professional surface: who recurs in frame, which settings recur — nightlife, family tables, solo work — and how the social life is actually spent. In the cold field test the profiler read the subject's business website and never sampled his personal feed, and underweighted the party-centered social life that organized his evenings.
- **Never scored:** facial structure, facial proportions, features, or body type. The research behind those reads is graded weak in the evidence file, and no dossier line may rest on them.
- **Never inferred:** any health condition. Photographs can evidence a nightlife-centered social life; they cannot evidence addiction or illness. A condition enters a dossier only in the subject's own stated words.

## The Big Five, from words and record

State each trait as a range — low, lower-middle, middle, upper-middle, high — with the spread the evidence allows. Score from behavior first, language second.

**Openness.** High: seeks new ideas and fields, changes positions in public, uses analogy across domains, funds or builds unfamiliar things. Low: stays inside one frame, treats novelty as risk, repeats a settled doctrine. Language markers: breadth of vocabulary and topics, comfort with hypotheticals. Disconfirmer shape: a high-openness read dies if the person's record shows a decade of declining every unfamiliar opportunity. Split ideas from experience when the evidence does: a person can be ravenous for new ideas and indifferent to new sensations, and coverage over-samples novelty because novelty is what gets covered.

**Conscientiousness.** High: ships on stated deadlines, keeps systems and records, follows up in writing, preparation visible in their work. Low: chronic lateness, dropped threads, improvisation as a pattern rather than an exception. This trait is scored from the record more than from words — everyone claims to be disciplined. Score the facets separately when they split: design discipline (systems built, preparation, records) and upkeep discipline (maintenance chores, follow-through on boring warnings, basic hygiene) routinely sit at opposite ends in the same person, and the average describes neither.

**Extraversion.** Score it by the sub-protocol above — self-description, chosen formats, built structure, absence — never from audience size, stage count, or speaking volume, which measure the role. Low: writes rather than meets, small deliberate circles, energy spent in depth rather than breadth. Note the arena effect: a person can be quiet in public and relentless in small rooms. Score the arena the decision happens in.

**Agreeableness.** High: credits others unprompted, repairs conflict, frames outcomes as shared, disagrees with ideas while protecting the person. Low: public criticism of named people, zero-sum framing, credit kept close. Watch behavior under provocation — agreeableness shows most when the person has been attacked. Public records over-sample conflict, because fights are news and decency is not: agreeableness from public material is capped at low confidence unless chosen evidence — unprompted repair, credit given where no audience required it — carries it. Score the facets separately when they split: **compassion** (care for people's welfare, generosity with time and help) and **deference** (softening opinions, yielding the floor, keeping the peace). They diverge in real people — the cold field test's subject was compassion-high and deference-low: a man who gives his patients hours and tells rooms they are wrong, kindly, at length. A single averaged score described neither half.

**Emotional stability** (the low end of neuroticism). High stability: even response to setbacks and criticism, humor under pressure, no grudges carried in public. Low stability: grievances repeated across years, threat sensitivity, mood visibly steering decisions. Score stability from duration and recovery: how long the person kept functioning under sustained pressure, and how fast they returned to baseline and repaired after a spike. The worst episode measures volatility, a separate facet — score it separately. A profile of a person's worst week is a profile of a week, not a trait. Add a third facet: **worry** — the steady background anxiety a person carries between events. Endurance and volatility describe what happens when things go wrong; worry describes the quiet weeks. They split in real people: the cold field test's subject endures publicly and does not erupt, and runs anxious underneath, with control-seeking as the anxiety's management strategy. A stability score that averages the three describes none of them.

## Motives, from speech imagery

Following Winter's system, score the imagery in spontaneous speech. Count what the person's stories and arguments are *about*, underneath their topic:

- **Achievement imagery** — beating a standard, doing something better or first, pride in measurable improvement, frustration with "good enough."
- **Power imagery** — moving people, winning standing, impact on an audience or a market, concern with reputation and who is up or down.
- **Affiliation imagery** — friendship, belonging, loyalty, warmth, fear of exclusion or of a relationship breaking.

Report the ordering (which motive leads, which trails) rather than raw counts; the ordering is what predicts choices. A power-led person hears a pitch as a question about standing. An achievement-led person hears it as a question about the standard. An affiliation-led person hears it as a question about the relationship.

Two motive splits, scored when the imagery shows them. Power divides into **standing** (being seen to matter — audience, rank, reputation) and **control** (steering decisions and outcomes, whether or not anyone watches). A standing-led person hears a pitch as a question about visibility; a control-led person hears it as a question about who decides. Achievement divides into **mastery** (beating a standard — the work done better than it was done before) and **winning** (beating the field — rivals named, rankings watched). A mastery-led person asks whether it is excellent; a winning-led person asks whether it beats them. The halves take different levers, so an unsplit score can point the approach at the wrong one.

Apply the performed-versus-chosen separation to motives too. Standing that is spent — an audience built and then walked away from, to go build something — is instrumental, not terminal: score power as control before scoring it as fame. Community language in a person's brand is performed affiliation; affiliation with no chosen evidence behind it is reported as unmeasured, not moderate.

## Conceptual complexity

Score from how the person handles opponents, trade-offs, and uncertainty in spontaneous speech:

- **Low** — one side is right, the other is corrupt or stupid; outcomes are certain; disagreement is bad faith. Messages to this person: one clear claim, one clear enemy or obstacle, no hedging.
- **Middle** — several factors acknowledged, then collapsed into a single rule of thumb. Messages: a clear lead claim, with the main trade-off named and answered.
- **High** — multiple perspectives held at once, probabilities instead of certainties, positions updated in public. Messages: nuance is respected; a one-sided pitch reads as an insult.

Complexity also splits by domain: the same person can be nuanced on their craft and binary on their enemies. Score it per domain when the record shows the split, and write the message to the domain the decision lives in.

Compare recent speech against the person's own baseline. A drop from their normal level is a stress signal, and it belongs in the dossier when the decision is near-term.

## Leadership traits (Hermann's set)

Score only what the words show:

- **Belief in own control** — "we made it happen" versus "the market decided." High scorers initiate; low scorers react and wait for conditions.
- **Need for power** — overlaps the power motive; score it here when the speech is about control over decisions and people, not just standing.
- **Self-confidence** — certainty of judgment stated without qualification, few appeals to others' authority.
- **Task versus relationship focus** — whether problems or people lead their sentences when something goes wrong.
- **Distrust** — default suspicion of others' motives, verification demanded, betrayal expected.
- **In-group bias** — the world split into us and them, with us holding the virtues.

## Values and incentives, from the record

List what the person's choices optimize for, in the order the record supports: growth, money, status, craft, freedom, loyalty, safety, impact. Use costly choices only — what they paid for, declined, or walked away from. A value that never cost anything is a slogan, and the dossier marks it as claimed, not shown.

## Household and life structure

Score the shape of the life alongside the traits: partner or single, children and their ages, who the person lives with, and how time outside work is actually allocated. The sources are the record and the visual record — who is in the frame across years, whose events anchor the calendar, what the evenings and weekends visibly contain. Household status is scored, never assumed and never skipped: in a dense record, no partner and no children across years of documentation is a finding with confidence attached, because it shapes the person's time economy, social life, and appetite for risk. In a thin record it is marked unknown. What the section must never do is fill the silence with a guess about why — the structure is the evidence; the reasons are the person's.

## Formation and values

**Formation.** Gather the layer underneath the traits: family position, migration or displacement, the scarcity or abundance of the early years, the home's religion and culture, the first money story, and the turning-point events. The strongest formation evidence is the subject's own repeated narrative — the story they tell, in version after version, about how they were made. Score the story as the mechanism: it is what their choices are organized to keep true. Score the facts in it like everything else, with confidence stated, and mark what is not known (the inside of the home usually is not public, and the dossier says so rather than filling it in).

**Values.** Schwartz's ten are the map: self-direction, stimulation, hedonism, achievement, power, security, tradition, conformity, benevolence, universalism. Do not score them from statements. A value is ranked by what it beat: find the collisions — the moments two values pulled opposite ways and the person paid for one of them — and rank by the price paid. A value that never cost anything stays marked claimed, per the values section above. Values decide trade-offs; traits only set the style of the deciding, which is why values often predict the choice itself better than the Big Five do.

**The three tags.** Every score the tag does work on carries one: **temperament** — early-appearing, stable across decades, what stress reveals; **formation** — installed by upbringing and events, explains values, wounds, and the self-narrative; **choice** — a current strategy that changes when incentives change. Two discipline rules follow. Never explain by temperament what formation explains: a sensitivity installed by a specific event is not a trait, and treating it as one makes it sound permanent when it is a scar. And never file a strategy as a trait: a person performing a role their incentives pay for will stop when the pay stops, and a dossier that scored the performance as temperament will be surprised. Under stress, predict from temperament and values; in calm, expect choice to be running.

## Pressure points — where the person is exposed

A profile that only lists strengths prepares you for the person's best day. Score the exposed side with the same discipline: evidence, confidence, disconfirmer.

- **What they fear losing.** Standing, control, money, a key relationship, a self-image ("the person who builds," "the one who called it early"). Fear shows in what they defend first and hardest when challenged, and in the topics where their complexity suddenly drops.
- **Known fears and aversions.** Specific fears the subject's own words or record state — heights, flying, hospitals, public speaking — recorded never from type or guesswork. They earn their line for a practical reason: they change real choices in a playbook, from the venue to the activity to the travel plan. A fear the profiler merely suspects does not ship; an unstated fear is a gap, not a finding.
- **Tender spots.** The achievement they feel is under-credited. The comparison that stings — a rival, a peer group, a earlier version of themselves. The criticism they answer at length, every time, instead of letting it pass.
- **Over-investments.** The position, project, or relationship they keep funding past the evidence — sunk cost they cannot walk away from because leaving would rewrite the story.
- **Blind spots.** The feedback that never reaches them (everyone around them knows; they do not). The risk they systematically underweight. The flattery they cannot detect because it matches their self-image.
- **The self-image gap.** Where the presented self and the record part ways. The gap is the sorest point and usually the strongest lever: an approach that lets the person close the gap themselves — act as the person they believe they are — moves them more than any argument.

Pressure points decide the levers, and the levers are stated in the dossier as: the move, the dimension it answers, and the backfire. A lever backfires when the person detects it as handling — most people forgive a direct approach and punish a detected script — or when it is used twice. Never build a lever on a genuine crisis in the person's life; that is out of bounds, and the dossier does not record those even when they are public.

## The disagreement rule

When words and record disagree, the dossier reports both, scores from the record, and names the gap. The gap itself is often the most useful line in the profile: it tells you which of the person's self-descriptions not to build your message on.
