# The five focusing steps, in depth

Goldratt's method, from *The Goal* (1984) and *Theory of Constraints* (1990). The main skill's seven steps operationalize these five: its steps 1–2 build the measurement the five steps assume, and its steps 3–7 are the five steps with a repeat-and-audit discipline made explicit.

## 1. Identify the system's constraint

What it requires: find the one stage that limits the whole system's output, from evidence. The signs are physical: work accumulates in front of the constraint, and stages behind it wait for work. The constraint's effective capacity, after its own losses, is the lowest in the chain.

How it is botched: naming the stage people already dislike, or the stage with the loudest queue of complaints rather than the longest queue of work. A constraint named by opinion produces a year of confident work on the wrong stage.

Knowledge-work translation: the constraint is as often a policy as a resource — an approval, a hold rule, a routing decision. Policies leave no physical pile, so the queue has to be made visible in the tooling before the constraint can be seen at all.

## 2. Exploit the constraint

What it requires: get the maximum out of the constraint as it stands, before spending anything. Keep it fed with ready work, keep defective work away from it, take every task off it that another stage can do, and shield it from interruptions.

How it is botched: skipped entirely, in favor of buying capacity. This step is nearly free and is frequently sufficient on its own; skipping it pays money for output that was already available.

Knowledge-work translation: protect the constraint's attention. If the constraint is a reviewer, a lane, or a person, unplanned work arriving at random times is the largest single leak, because it pays the interruption cost at the system's most expensive stage.

## 3. Subordinate everything else to the constraint

What it requires: every other stage works at the constraint's pace and in its service. Non-constraints produce only what the constraint can consume. Their local efficiency targets are suspended where they conflict.

How it is botched: refused, because it asks visibly busy people and machines to slow down. Local utilization metrics survive, work in progress piles up in front of the constraint, cycle times lengthen, and the method gets blamed for the pile it predicted. This is the step where the method either works or dies, and it dies politically, not technically.

Knowledge-work translation: throttle intake and cap work in progress. A team or fleet that starts everything finishes nothing faster; it just holds more inventory.

## 4. Elevate the constraint

What it requires: only after steps 2 and 3 are genuinely done, invest in the constraint's capacity: more of it, a faster version, a redesign.

How it is botched: done first. Elevation before exploitation buys capacity to compensate for waste, and the waste survives inside the larger system.

Knowledge-work translation: this is where redesign methods belong — Musk's Algorithm in its order (question the requirement, delete, simplify, accelerate, automate last) and first-principles decomposition when the stage's design is itself the limit.

## 5. Repeat, and do not let inertia become the constraint

What it requires: once the constraint is elevated, it moves. Return to step 1. Goldratt's warning is explicit and usually dropped from summaries: the rules and policies written to protect the old constraint become candidates for the new one.

How it is botched: the improvement program declares victory at the first gain and defends its own rules forever. The system ends up constrained by the cure.

## The one-line summary

An hour lost at the constraint is an hour lost for the entire system. An hour saved at a non-constraint is a mirage.
