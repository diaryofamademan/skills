# Worked example — a data-licensing batch to AI labs

A complete run of the procedure on one batch, in the data and product licensing lane. The structure, gates, and verdicts are real, drawn from the author's buyer outreach; names, companies, and identifying details are changed, and proof lines are shown as slots because the lane's approved lines are the operator's to fill. Word counts are the counts of the drafts as written.

## Step 0 — the offer test

The offer, in one plain sentence: "We license the operating data our platform collects — what real users ask, click, and pay for — to labs training and evaluating models." Pass. A stranger can repeat it back. Lane: data and product licensing.

## Step 1 — the batch

Segment: US-based AI labs with a public product and a growing data team. Trigger: each lab in the batch had, within the previous month, either announced a new model or posted data-team roles. Batch size: 12 companies, one named recipient each — the person who owns data or evaluations, not a general inbox.

## Step 2 — warm path, then history gate

Warm path: 2 of the 12 had an existing connection to the sender on LinkedIn. Those two left the cold batch and were routed to connection messages first. History gate: a mailbox search found a prior thread with 1 of the remaining 10 — an old support exchange under the same company domain. That recipient was removed and the message routed as a warm follow-up. **Cold batch: 9.**

## Step 3 — the sender's surface

A search of the sender showed: the professional profile leading with the platform the data comes from, the book, and the company — matching the email's claim. One fix was made before writing: a stale bio line naming an old role was updated, because the email introduces the sender by the current one.

## Steps 4–5 — one recipient's research note

Observation: the lab announced a new model release three weeks ago and has two open roles for data partnerships and evaluations staff. Source: the company's own announcement post and its public careers page, checked the day of writing.

Hypothesis: a team hiring for data partnerships right after a launch is actively sourcing outside data for the next training and evaluation round — which is the moment a licensing conversation costs them nothing to have.

## Step 6 — the first touch

Subject: "your data sourcing" (3 words, internal-sounding)

> Saw the [model] launch last month, and the two data roles you have open under it.
>
> Looks like you are sourcing outside data for the next round of training and evals. We license the operating data from [the platform] — real user questions, clicks, and payments, across [the domain].
>
> Is outside data part of the plan for this round, or is it all in-house?

Word count: 68. Checklist: observation with source ✓ · hypothesis hedged ("looks like") ✓ · one ask, an interest question ✓ · no pricing, link, or image ✓ · no proof line — the lane's proof is held for the follow-ups ✓.

## Step 7 — the follow-ups

First follow-up (carries a new fact — the dataset's shape):

> One detail I left out: the data runs to about [size] across [number] source systems, and it is the full history, not a sample.
>
> Still the same question — is any of this round's data coming from outside?

Word count: 41.

Final follow-up (carries the strongest proof, and the free exit):

> Closing the loop on this. [Approved proof line: the lane's strongest permitted proof — for example, that buyers are already under agreement — goes here, in one line.]
>
> If the answer is no, just say so and I will close it out — no hard feelings, and no more emails. And if it is someone else on your side who owns this, a name is enough.

Word count: 62, plus the proof line. Checklist: proof spent once, strongest last ✓ · "no" made free in plain words ✓ · one referral ask, allowed in this lane ✓ · no manufactured deadline ✓.

## Step 8 — the verdict pattern

The batch returned for approval as: the offer test result, the batch definition, the gate results (2 routed warm, 1 removed for history, 9 cold), the surface fix noted, then the nine recipients each with research note, hypothesis, three emails, word counts, and verdicts. Two drafts in the batch failed their first checklist pass — one observation could not be verified against a current source and was rewritten from a checkable fact; one first touch ran 91 words and was cut to the ceiling. Both fixes are the normal shape of a run: the checklist exists to catch them before the approver does.
