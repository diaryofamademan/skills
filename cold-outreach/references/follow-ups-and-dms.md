# Follow-ups and direct messages

Step 7's depth material: how follow-ups are built, how proof is sequenced, what the honest benchmarks are, and what is actually known about cold direct messages.

## Why follow-ups carry the sequence

In Belkins' study of 7,530,489 emails (2025), the first email produced 41.4% of all replies and steps 2–6 produced 58.6%; steps 3–5 produced 53.5% of email-sourced meetings, with step 3 alone the single largest meeting source. Instantly and Woodpecker report the mirror split (58% of replies from the first email) on different denominators; the agreed fact across all of them is that follow-ups add roughly 40–59% of replies. A sequence that stops after one follow-up forfeits the stage where most of the return sits. The evidence-supported total length is three to five touches; returns diminish sharply after the fifth in every dataset. This skill writes a first touch plus two follow-ups — inside the supported range — and the sending process owns whether a lane runs longer.

## Building a follow-up

Each follow-up carries exactly one new thing. The permitted kinds:

- **A new fact** the first touch left out — the dataset's size, the offer's scope, a relevant date. New information is a reason to read; repetition is not.
- **A new piece of proof**, sequenced as below.
- **A shorter form of the same ask.** If the first question needed context, the follow-up can ask it bare. Shorter follow-ups are not ruder; they respect that the thread above them carries the context.
- **The exit** (final follow-up only): the free "no", and where the lane allows, one referral ask.

Never a bare bump ("just floating this to the top"), never a guilt line ("third time writing"), never a fake reply thread or a manufactured deadline. Each of those spends the sender's name for one open.

## Proof sequencing

Proof is the sequence's scarce currency, and it obeys one rule: **a proof point earns attention once, when it is new.** Shown in the first email, it cannot be new again in the third; a follow-up repeating it reads as decoration, and the follow-up's only job is to give the prospect a new reason to answer.

So the sequence holds proof in reserve and spends it in rising order:

1. **First touch:** at most one short proof line, where the lane allows any. The first touch's real proof is the observation itself — the visible research is what separates the sender from spray.
2. **First follow-up:** a further fact or a second proof point — the dataset's shape, the offer's scope, a mid-weight result.
3. **Final follow-up:** the strongest proof the lane permits, in one line, paired with the free exit. The prospect who has ignored two emails gets the best reason and the cheapest way out in the same message.

This ordering is also where the audience is: since follow-ups produce the majority of replies and meetings in the largest dataset, most prospects who ever weigh the proof will meet it in a follow-up. Spending the strongest proof in the first email puts it in front of the smallest audience the sequence will ever have.

Momentum claims follow the same discipline as facts: true and verified, or not written. "Two buyers signed this quarter" is a proof point when it is true and a liability when it is not — in a small market, it will be checked.

## Spacing — honestly labeled

No dataset publishes a controlled comparison of follow-up spacing. Vendor guidance clusters at two to four days for a first follow-up, widening later; the author's sending process runs five business days between touches. Both are practice. This skill takes no position: cadence belongs to the sending process, and anyone claiming data for a spacing rule is selling something.

Small, targeted, well-researched batches — this skill's whole design — sit at the favorable end of every one of these ranges, because list quality is the largest term in all of them.

## Cold direct messages

The DM evidence is thinner than the email evidence, and this section is labeled accordingly.

**LinkedIn** has one large independent dataset: Belkins × Expandi (15.1 million contacts, 2025). Overall reply rate across LinkedIn outreach: 7.2% of messages. Messages to existing connections replied at 12.22% — the highest rate measured, and the empirical reason this skill's step 2 works the warm path first. Open InMail replied at 7.5%, on a small subsample (about 13,600 contacts), so treat it as directional. Connection requests were accepted about 26% of the time; a personalized note slightly *lowered* acceptance (25.3% against 27.6% with no note) but *raised* replies after connecting (8.2% against 5.3%). About 1.3% of connected prospects booked a meeting. LinkedIn's own published InMail figures (10–25%, sometimes 18–25%) come with no methodology, mix recruiting with sales, and are not used in this skill.

Practical shape, labeled practice: connect first where a connection is plausible, message second; keep the message to the same structure as the email — observation, hypothesis, one interest question — at email length or shorter; and count the funnel honestly (invites → connections → replies → meetings), because the connection gate, not the copy, is the binding constraint.

**X/Twitter direct messages** have no credible dataset. The best located evidence is one documented campaign of 1,344 cold DMs producing 15 positive responses (1.1%, grade C); vendor ranges of 3–15% publish no data (grade D). This skill offers no X DM rules beyond the email rules applied plainly, and says so rather than inventing a playbook the evidence cannot carry.
