# The evidence

What the research actually supports, graded. This file is the skill's memory: every rule in `../SKILL.md` traces to a line here, and every number carries its source, size, and year.

**The framing fact:** no peer-reviewed experiment on cold B2B outreach exists. The evidence is (a) academic studies of opt-in marketing email and lab persuasion, and (b) large observational datasets published by the platforms that process cold email — no confidence intervals, and the vendor usually sells the conclusion its data supports. Direction is often consistent across datasets; precise percentages are not portable.

**Grades:** A = peer-reviewed or academic. B = large-sample vendor or platform dataset, named with its size and year. C = small practitioner test. D = opinion or unsourced claim. Open-rate findings are degraded since Apple Mail Privacy Protection (2021) auto-loads tracking pixels; reply-based findings are preferred throughout.

## Length

Short first emails beat long ones, but the optimum is contested. Gong's 85-million-email index (2025, grade B) peaks at 51–100 words (2.6% reply, against 1.6% at 151–200 words), and its 28-million-email analysis puts the ceiling at about 100 words. Smartlead's 850-million-email dataset (grade B) ranks under 50 words best (1.47%). Belkins' 16.5-million-email study (grade B) ranks 101–200 words and 6–8 sentences best — the opposite order, on a different denominator. Boomerang's widely quoted "50–125 words" finding is general email, not cold outreach (5.3 million emails analyzed in 2016, grade B for general email only). **The skill's rule:** a first-touch ceiling near 75 words, labeled contested. Anyone quoting a single optimal word count is quoting one vendor.

## Plain language

Simpler language wins where it has been measured. Boomerang (2016, general email, grade B) found third-grade reading level responses about 36% higher than college level. Lavender's claim of +67% replies at a 3rd–5th grade level (2023, grade B−) states no sample size and comes from a vendor whose product scores reading level. No grade-A study tests reading level in email. **The skill's rule:** plain words, short sentences — the grade-level number is a proxy, not the rule.

## Personalization

The ranking is consistent across datasets: relevance beats tokens. In Gong's 85-million-email index (2025, grade B), personalization by type indexed: activity and trigger-based 24, company facts 9, individual details 6, against a baseline of 2. Salesloft's 15-million-email data (2020, grade B) found emails about 15% personalized replied at 2.75 times the rate of unpersonalized ones — with the caveat that heavy personalizers also target better. Backlinko's 12 million outreach emails (2019, PR outreach, grade B) found personalization worth about +30% on responses. The only randomized trial in the field (Sahni, Wheeler and Chintagunta, *Marketing Science* 2018, grade A) found a first name in a marketing email's subject line raised opens about 20% and leads about 31% — and a 2023 replication found first-name personalization slightly negative. Much of the observational lift is a targeting effect: Hunter's data (grade B) shows campaigns to 50 or fewer recipients replying at 5.8% against 2.1% at 1,000 or more. **The skill's rule:** personalization means a verified observation tied to a trigger. Mail-merge tokens are not personalization. The "+142% from personalization" figure that circulates in cold email content has no primary source and is excluded (grade D).

## Subject lines

Short is the most replicated finding: two to four words across Gong's 85-million index, Belkins' 5.5-million-email subject study (46% opens in that bracket, 2024 data), and Boomerang. Beyond length, the datasets contradict each other. Case: Gong found lowercase +11% on opens; Belkins found caps and lowercase effectively tied. Questions: Gong found −3%; Belkins found question subjects tied for best; Lavender's secondary summaries claim −56%. Personalization: Belkins found personalized subjects replying at 7% against 3% generic; GetResponse's aggregate found personalized subjects opening *less* than generic. An empty subject raised opens 30% and cut replies 12% in Gong's index — the cleanest proof in the literature that opens are not replies. Social proof in the subject line: −4% (Gong). **The skill's rule:** one to three words, internal-sounding, held as practice. No case, question, or personalization rule is stated, because the evidence does not support one.

## The ask

The strongest actionable finding in the research. Gong's analysis of 304,174 emails (grade B) found an interest call to action — a question about interest — more than twice as likely to book a meeting as a specific-time ask on cold email; the same study found the specific-time ask winning once a deal was in motion (its share of successful asks rising from 15% cold to 37% in-deal). Gong's 85-million index adds: an offer in the email raised replies about 28%, and a meeting ask cut them about 44%. Boomerang (general email, grade B) found one to three questions raised response likelihood about 50% against none. Third-party numbers attributed to the Gong study ("12% versus 7%", "68% positive") do not appear in Gong's text and are excluded as invented. **The skill's rule:** the first touch asks one clear interest question; the meeting ask comes after the reply.

## Follow-ups

Follow-ups carry the sequence. Belkins' study of 7,530,489 emails (2025, grade B) found steps 2–6 produced 58.6% of all replies, steps 3–5 produced 53.5% of meetings, and a three-to-five-step total as the practical sweet spot. Instantly and Woodpecker report a 58/42 split favoring the first email — a denominator difference (per-contact against per-send), flagged rather than resolved; the agreed fact is that follow-ups add roughly 40–59% of replies. Backlinko (2019, grade B) found a single follow-up worth +65.8% responses in PR outreach. **Spacing has no dataset behind any recommendation.** The common vendor ranges (first follow-up at 2–4 days) are practitioner convention, grade C/D; this skill takes no position and leaves cadence to the sending process. The famous "80% of sales need 5–12 touches" traces to a 1942 survey of fewer than 40 people and is excluded.

## Timing

A second-order lever. Smartlead's 850 million emails (grade B) put reply rates between 0.82% and 0.91% across all days of the week — a near-null effect — with a modest peak around 7am recipient time (1.34%). Belkins found Wednesday and Thursday marginally ahead (0.48%) and 8am–12pm the best window (0.54%). **The skill's rule:** send in the recipient's morning if it costs nothing; do not treat the day as a lever.

## Format

No cold-specific controlled test of plain text against HTML exists. HubSpot's marketing A/B tests over more than 500 million emails (grade B) found simpler creative always won clicks — a plain version beat a GIF version by 42%, and an HTML-plus-images version lost 51% of clicks — while properly coded HTML delivered exactly as well as plain text. The circulated claim that HTML bounces "674% more" has no primary source and is excluded (grade D). Salesloft data (grade B−) found video in cadence emails raised replies 26%, against the plain-text dogma. **The skill's rule:** no links or images in a first touch, as a simplicity rule — never stated as a deliverability law.

## Framing

Loss framing is excluded as a rule. The grade-A meta-analysis (O'Keefe and Jensen, 165 effects, N=50,780) finds loss frames are not generally more persuasive than gain frames; gain frames win prevention-type behaviors by a small margin (r = .03–.083). The loss-aversion coefficient itself is contested: Brown, Imai, Vieider and Camerer (2024, 607 estimates) put it at 1.955; Walasek, Mullett and Stewart (2024) at 1.31; Yechiam and Zeif (2025, symmetric designs) at about 1.07 and not significant. The famous 2.25 is a 1992 median model fit, and 2.5 comes from a mug experiment's willingness-to-accept ratio — neither is a persuasion effect. No cold-outreach dataset isolates framing at all. **The skill's rule:** framing is an untested copy choice; write the outcome plainly, in gain or neutral terms, and do not cite prospect theory for it.

## Social proof

One dataset, used with care. Gong's 85-million index (grade B) found social proof in the body worth +41% replies, and in the subject line −4%. Within Gong's own research there is a partial contradiction: ROI language cut success about 15% (132,000 emails). No dataset isolates a named client against generic proof. **The skill's rule:** relevant proof, in the body, stated as a fact ("worked with" a peer the recipient recognizes), never as an ROI multiple, and only where the lane's approved lines allow any proof at all.

## Sender identity

No comparative dataset exists. Nothing located in any major vendor's published research compares founder-sent against salesperson-sent outreach with list and copy held constant. Every "send as the founder" claim is grade D opinion, and recipient-side data (founders as recipients reply most, Belkins; C-level recipients reply less, Gong) is routinely mis-cited as sender evidence. **The skill's rule:** no founder-sent claim. A lane sends as the founder when the offer is the founder's, which is a fact about the offer, not a data claim.

## Benchmarks

The denominator is the story. Belkins' 2026 report on 2025 data (7.53 million sends, grade B): 0.45% replies per send, about one meeting per 6,275 sends. Smartlead (850 million, grade B): 0.74% median replies per contact. Instantly (billions of interactions, 2025, grade B): 3.43% mean replies per delivered email, top decile at 10.7%. Hunter (grade B): 4.1% per campaign (11 million emails, 2024); 3.0% for sales outreach specifically (31 million, 2025). Gong: 344 emails per meeting for the average rep. Positive replies run about 13–23% of all replies where measured. Rates are declining in every longitudinal series (Belkins 6.8% to 5.8% year over year on its old denominator, and 0.54% to 0.35% within 2025 on the new one; Instantly 5.1% to 3.43%). **The skill's rule:** judge a batch against the operator's own previous batches in the same lane. Vendor headline rates describe other people's denominators.

## Claims the evidence kills

Recorded so the build — and future editors — never import them:

- "80% of sales need 5–12 touches" — a 1942 survey of fewer than 40 people.
- "Losses loom 2.5× larger, so frame for loss" — a mug experiment's ratio, misapplied; the persuasion meta-analysis disagrees.
- "+142% replies from personalization" — circulates with no primary source.
- Boomerang described as "40 million cold emails" — it analyzed 5.3 million general emails.
- "HTML emails bounce 674% more" — no primary source located.
- "Interest CTAs get 12% replies and 68% positive (Gong)" — invented by third-party blogs; not in Gong's text.
- "The average cold email reply rate is 8.5%" — Backlinko 2019, link-building outreach, pre-2024 inbox rules.
- "1–3% of cold emails book meetings" as a typical benchmark — transparent datasets put meetings per send between about 0.016% and 0.29%.

## What could not be verified

Hunter's "+52% deep personalization" figure (secondary citations only). Instantly's gated report detail. LinkedIn's primary InMail data (none is published). Any X/Twitter DM benchmark. Any controlled comparison of follow-up spacing. Any dataset isolating open against closed question types, or named-client against generic proof.
