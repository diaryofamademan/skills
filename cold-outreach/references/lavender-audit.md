# The practitioner audits

Two practitioner sources were audited line by line against the evidence base and the author's own sent record, instead of adopted whole. Verdicts: **adopt** (the rule enters the skill as stated), **adapt** (the direction is right; the form changes), **reject** (the rule does not enter, with the reason recorded).

## Lavender's 20 cold email tips

Lavender is an AI email-coaching vendor. Its tips circulate as a screenshot list; its statistics come from its own dataset, are labeled as Lavender's claims wherever they appear, and are never stated in this skill as measured facts. Each tip below is paraphrased in a few words; the verdict and the reason are the substance.

**Adopted as written (8)**

- **Tip 4 — Paragraphs of one to two sentences.** Adopted. Matches the skill's formatting rule and the plain-language evidence.
- **Tip 5 — Open with an observation about the recipient.** Adopted. It is the skill's step 4, and the evidence-backed form of personalization.
- **Tip 7 — Verify the research before sending.** Adopted, and hardened: every observation carries its source in the research note. A wrong fact about the recipient kills the email and spends the sender's name.
- **Tip 8 — Write about their problem, not your product.** Adopted. Gong's data found product-first pitching cut replies by up to 57%.
- **Tip 11 — Make the question easy to answer.** Adopted. It is half of the interest-question rule.
- **Tip 14 — One ask per email.** Adopted.
- **Tip 18 — Cut the clichés.** Adopted. Stock openers and sign-offs mark the email as a template.
- **Tip 19 — No images or links in a first touch.** Adopted as a simplicity rule (see the evidence file, Format). Not adopted as a deliverability claim, which the data does not support.

**Adapted (10)**

- **Tips 1–2 — Very short emails (25–50 words; under 75).** Adapted to a ceiling near 75 words with the context included. Lavender's 25-word end is thinner than the author's own record supports: his direction on his buyer outreach was that cold emails need a little more context and a clearer ask. The evidence is contested in any case (Gong peaks at 51–100 words, Smartlead under 50, Belkins at 101–200), so the skill holds a ceiling, not a target.
- **Tip 3 — Write at a 3rd–5th grade reading level.** Adapted: plain words, yes; the grade number, no. The direction is supported (Boomerang, general email); the specific +67% figure is Lavender's, with no stated sample. A grade level is a proxy for short sentences and common words, and the proxy is not the rule.
- **Tip 6 — Turn research into a hypothesis.** Adopted in shape: it is the skill's step 5, the observation tied to the problem the offer solves.
- **Tip 9 — Use a relevant customer story.** Adapted hard: only proof the lane has approved for external use, never another client's terms, never an invented story. Proof lines are gated in the author's lanes (in one lane, exactly one external proof line is permitted), so "use a customer story" becomes "use the approved proof line, where the lane allows one."
- **Tip 12 — Use hedge words.** Adapted by target: hedge the hypothesis ("seems like", "might"), never the ask. A hedged request reads as doubt about the offer.
- **Tip 15 — Lead with outcomes for executives.** Adopted in direction: revenue, cost, risk. With the evidence caveat: outcomes are not ROI multiples — Gong found ROI language cut cold success about 15%.
- **Tips 16–17 — Subject lines of 1–3 words that sound internal.** Adopted as the default, held as practice rather than law. Short is the most replicated subject finding in the evidence; the internal-sounding detail (no verbs performing, no numbers, no questions, no first names) is Lavender's craft rule, kept because it agrees with writing as a person rather than as a campaign.
- **Tip 20 — Cap daily volume per inbox and space sends out.** Adapted out of the skill's scope: volume and spacing belong to the sending process, which already governs them. The writing-side note that survives: copy is written for spaced, low-volume sends to verified lists, never for spray.

**Overridden (2)**

- **Tip 10 — Never ask for the meeting; the only goal is a reply.** Overridden as written, refined by evidence. The author's rule is one clear, easy ask — a reply is a means, not the goal. Gong's 304,174-email analysis then settled the form: an interest question booked more than twice the meetings of a time ask on cold email, and the time ask wins after a reply. So the first touch asks the interest question, and the meeting ask is not banned — it is sequenced. Lavender's instinct (do not open with a calendar grab) survives; its absolute does not.
- **Tip 13 — Drop statistics and "did you know" openers.** Adopted for cold email, with a fence: an informative tone is not a reply hook, and a stat the recipient did not ask for reads as a pitch. The fence: this verdict governs cold email only. It does not touch content writing, where a well-chosen stat that shows a change is a core tool under its own rules.

## Dimitar Angelov's outbound article

"Every Cold Outreach Principle I Know in One Article" (X Article, 2026-04-05). A whole outbound-system piece — volume, infrastructure, multi-channel, scaling — from a practitioner who runs B2B outbound systems and sells implementation. No external source is cited for any statistic in it; every number is the author's own claim from his own sending (he describes five million sends, unverified). Same labeling rule as Lavender: author claims, never facts.

**Where it agrees with this skill:** text-only emails with no images; fix the offer before the copy; precise targeting and verified list data over list size; track the funnel and change one thing at a time; any reply, even an objection, is progress.

**Adopted from it (3)**

- **The offer test.** State the offer in one plain sentence; if it does not appeal stated plainly, no copy fixes it. Became step 0.
- **The funnel diagnostic.** Low replies point at message or audience; a low positive share points at the offer; low booking points at the ask. Adopted for results runs, with his benchmark ranges recorded as author claims and used as sanity ranges only.
- **Batch by segment.** Prospects grouped by segment and pain, one template per batch with per-recipient observations. Adopted as step 1's shape.

**Rejected for this skill's lanes (4)**

- **Volume before copy.** His model is an agency outbound machine: a thousand sends at a low rate beat a hundred at a higher one. The author's lanes have no thousand-send version — high-value prospects, a data market of about a hundred buyers, shared daily caps, and an approver reading every draft. Research depth beats send count, and volume spends the sender's name in markets where everyone talks.
- **Follow-ups at 24–48 hours, four to five touches.** Conflicts with the sending process's cadence. Cadence is the sending process's call in any case, and the evidence file notes that no dataset supports any spacing claim — his included.
- **Pattern-interrupt subject lines.** Directly contradicts the adopted Lavender default. From a named founder's real inbox, a pattern interrupt reads as marketing; in a small repeated game it is taxed across future prospects.
- **The assumptive close — specific times or a calendar link in the first touch.** Rejected on the evidence (Gong: the interest ask books twice the meetings cold) and on the author's own process, where in one lane pricing goes by email to approved applicants before any call link exists.

**His figures, recorded as claims:** multi-touch beating single emails 3–5 times over; 70–80% of meetings coming from follow-ups (the Belkins dataset supports the direction at 53.5% of meetings from steps 3–5, and the skill uses Belkins' number); loss aversion at "2.5×" (excluded — see the evidence file, Framing); funnel benchmarks (opens 40–60%, replies 2–5%, positive 40–50% of replies, booking 30–50% of positive replies) — the positive-share figure in particular runs well above the 13–23% the measured datasets report, and none of his ranges are used as targets.
