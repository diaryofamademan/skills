# Lane cards

A lane is a kind of offer with its own ask and its own proof rules. Confirm the lane at step 0; a batch never mixes lanes. These four cards describe the author's lanes in general terms; an operator adopting this skill should rewrite each card with their own approved proof lines before the first run.

## Data and product licensing

**The offer:** a dataset, a feed, or a product capability licensed to a company that builds with it. Buyers are few, named, and worth five to six figures each; in the author's market the whole buyer universe is about a hundred companies.

**The ask:** one interest question about fit — whether the buyer's team evaluates outside data or product sources for the thing the trigger names. Pricing never appears in a first touch; it is discussed only after interest, and only as fixed, signable terms.

**Proof rules:** proof is scarce and gated. Use only the lines approved for external use. Never disclose another buyer's terms, price, or structure. A signed buyer's existence may be stated only where the lane's rules allow it; their terms, never.

**Shape notes:** research depth beats everything — one buyer justifies an hour of research. Triggers are product launches, model releases, data-team hiring, and public statements about data needs. The warm path matters most here: this market is small enough that a mutual connection usually exists within two hops.

**Failure mode:** treating it as a volume lane. There is no thousand-send version of a hundred-buyer market; a sprayed batch burns the whole universe in a week.

## Advisory and services

**The offer:** the founder's own judgment as a service — an audit, an engagement, a retainer. The sender is the product, so these emails go out under the founder's name and in the founder's voice; the voice rules sit above this skill's structure.

**The ask:** one interest question about the problem the audit or engagement solves. Where the lane's process qualifies applicants first and sends pricing by email before any call, the first touch carries neither price nor a booking link — the ask is interest, and the process does the rest.

**Proof rules:** the founder's own record, stated plainly and verifiably — what they built, what it did, in numbers they can defend. No client results without the client's standing permission. No borrowed credibility.

**Shape notes:** the sender-surface check (step 3) matters most in this lane: the prospect is buying the person, and the search results are the audition. Batches are small by design; the lane's capacity is the founder's calendar, and the batch size should never exceed the engagements the founder could actually take.

**Failure mode:** writing in a company's voice for a person-shaped offer. If the email could have come from anyone at any firm, it fails the lane.

## Sponsorship

**The offer:** access to an audience — a newsletter, a show, an event, a community — sold to a sponsor. Prospects are companies with marketing budgets and a fit with the audience.

**The ask:** one interest question about the sponsor's current push — the launch, the segment, the quarter's goal the trigger names. No media kit, deck, or rate card in a first touch; materials follow interest.

**Proof rules:** proof is the audience, stated in verified numbers — size, composition, past sponsor outcomes where the lane's rules allow naming them. In the author's practice, exactly one completed-campaign proof line is approved for external use in this lane's first touches; everything else waits for the reply.

**Shape notes:** triggers are funding announcements, product launches, and hiring in growth roles. This is the highest-volume of the four lanes and still numbers in the dozens per batch, not the thousands.

**Failure mode:** leading with the deck. A first touch carrying a media kit announces that the sender's goal is to send decks; the interest-first gate exists because the reply is worth more.

## Partnership

**The offer:** a working relationship — distribution, integration, co-marketing, reselling — where both sides give and get. The counterparty is a peer, not a buyer.

**The ask:** one interest question about the shared opportunity the trigger names, framed as a trade: what the sender brings, what the recipient brings, in one line each.

**Proof rules:** proof is the sender's own asset — audience, product, distribution — stated verifiably. Never imply an existing partner's endorsement without permission.

**Shape notes:** the warm path check does the most work in this lane; partnerships that start cold are the exception. Where a real relationship already exists, this skill does not apply at all — that is a conversation, not a cold touch.

**Failure mode:** writing a partnership email like a sales email. Pressure tactics against a future peer cost more than one declined batch; they cost the relationship the lane exists to build.
