## 0.6.0 — 2026-11-25

The rename, the research module, and the distribution modules — three changes the owner directed in one sitting, each because the skill had outgrown the shape its name and stages described.

**The rename: Channel Operator is now Funnel Operator.** The owner's reason, recorded as he gave it: the skill does more than operate a channel — it is the funnel: the channels, the website, the conversion endpoint, and everything around them. The name now says what the skill operates — attract, convert, measure, research — and this version's two additions complete the shape the name claims. The folder, the frontmatter name, and every internal reference carry the new name; the README keeps a "formerly Channel Operator" note so existing links and habits resolve. References outside this package are swept separately by the maintainer.

**The research module** (`references/research-module.md`), wired into setup's backlog seeding, stage 1, stage 9, and the scoreboard module's weekly review. The owner's words for the gap: the channel should do market and content research on what is performing in its niche, industry, and market, "so it's not just creating stuff blind." The module names the failure it prevents — scoring topics on judgment alone — and replaces the judgment with four passes run on a rhythm: what is performing on the video platform in the niche (search results read directly, competitor channels, the formats and hooks pulling), search demand in the audience's own phrasing (the platform's suggestions outrank any tool's estimate), what is performing on the channel's other platforms, and buyer demand signals, the channel's own deal flow and market sources read before any public reporting. Findings land as dated research notes in the channel folder; every topic score's reason cites the finding behind it, and a score with no finding is marked a judgment score until research replaces it. The weekly read re-ranks the backlog against the refreshed findings and checks the refill rule — unused topics against the next two weeks of cadence — so a dry backlog is caught two weeks early instead of at stage 1. Guardrail 7 is untouched and binds the module: research widens the well's evidence, it never imports a topic the well cannot support, and funnel fit stays a veto over everything the market is rewarding.

**The distribution modules** — one reference per platform (`references/linkedin-module.md`, `references/x-module.md`, `references/instagram-module.md`), a Distribution block in the config template, a stage 8 post-publish line, and a section in SKILL.md binding the three together. Each module states what gets published (LinkedIn: posts and document carousels; X: a post, a thread, or a long-form post, one shape per episode; Instagram: feed posts, reels from the episode's Shorts cuts, and carousels), the posting routes as they honestly are (LinkedIn's member-posting API is partner-gated, so the browser route through the signed-in profile or Page is the default and the API runs only where a channel legitimately holds approved access; X posts through its API on a paid tier via the account's own app, or through the browser; Instagram posts through Meta's Content Publishing API for Business and Creator accounts connected to a Page, or through the browser, and a personal account cannot use the publishing API at all), the owner gate (the exact copy and exact media — every post of a thread, every page of a carousel — approved in the episode's gate package before anything posts; nothing publishes silently), and verification (the live post read back, its URL recorded on the episode's line in the published log). The config block records which platforms are active, the account and identity on each, and the route each uses, so the modules run for any channel whose owner fills the block in — nothing in them is fitted to one channel's accounts.

The four-skill pass ran on the update. First-principles: the funnel was decomposed to its actual steps — a channel that researches nothing is guessing at the first step, and a channel that publishes on one platform is abandoning the last one; the two modules close the two open ends of the same chain, and the rename simply stops the name from understating the chain. Game theory: the platforms are actors with their own gates — LinkedIn rations its posting API to partners, Meta ties publishing to account type and Page connection, X sells the posting tier — so the modules state each platform's real gate as a constraint to route through honestly, never as an obstacle to work around; and competitors' published work is the market's revealed strategy, which is why the research module reads it directly instead of modelling it. Comms: every module states its gate the way the owner states his — exact copy, exact media, his word, then posting — in words a run can be audited against at a glance. Fundraising: no terms are negotiated in this version, but distribution is where the funnel's reach is priced by sponsors and buyers, and the modules' read-back verification is the evidence standard a buyer's diligence would apply to the channel's claimed presence.

Every run carries the playbook pass — first principles decomposing the funnel into measured steps and naming the binding constraint from the ratios, game theory where a platform's or competitor's choices gate the outcome, positioning when the message is the constraint, comms on any public copy proposed — and a briefing with numbers and no pass is defined as a failed run. Authority inside the loop follows the channel's gates exactly: records and preparation are applied in the run; titles, thumbnails, final cuts, public copy, and publishing are prepared to the exact decision for the owner's word. The first channel's two jobs were rebuilt to the module the same day (beacon traffic source added, the pass and the loop written into both bodies).

The four-skill pass ran on the update. First-principles: a channel is a chain of ratios, and a read that reports levels without ratios cannot find its own bottleneck — the module makes the decomposition the run's spine rather than its garnish. Game theory: the platform is an actor with its own incentives, and a scoreboard that never asks what the recommender is rewarding will optimize the channel for a game it is not playing; the pass names that step explicitly. Comms: the failed-run rule is stated the way the owner stated his — plainly enough that a run can be audited against it at a glance. Fundraising's gate did not trigger: no terms are negotiated in a numbers read, though the loop's output feeds the funnel the business sells through, and its evidence standard is the one a buyer would apply.

## 0.3.0 — 2026-11-24

The Shorts stage, approved by the owner on the day the pilot episode published, before any Short was cut. Shorts join the pipeline as an optional stage that hangs off the episode's own pass the way the podcast module does: a channel runs it when its config carries a Shorts block, and the nine stages do not change for a channel that does not. The design starts from purpose. A Short is discovery only — it puts one sharp claim in front of a viewer who would never click the long video and routes him to the full episode — and it is judged on that movement: related-video clicks into the episode, profile visits, funnel starts downstream. Its own view count is context, never the verdict, and stage 9 now reads Shorts on those terms. Selection happens at the script stage: two or three cuts per episode, never a batch, each passing a stand-alone test — one claim, one turn, no setup from earlier in the story — and named in the production notes before production starts; a moment that needs the story before it to land stays in the long video. The build is in the same production pass: a native vertical (9:16) rebuild from the same stills and the same narration segment, never a centre-crop or letterbox of the horizontal picture; thirty to sixty seconds; the claim spoken inside the first two seconds, with no title card, no logo sting, and no warm-up line; the picture on the channel's pacing standard; a shortened spoken call to action at the end; and a sidecar SRT for the segment, uploaded as the subtitle track, never burned in. The Shorts ride the episode's publish gate — they are listed in the same approval package the owner reviews, so there is no second gate and nothing uploads before his word — and release is staggered between episodes, never all on one day, never before the long episode is live. In YouTube Studio each Short's related-video link is set to the long episode; that link is the funnel, and setting it is a required step, not an option. Over the whole stage stands a new guardrail, guardrail 13: mass auto-clipping is banned. The precedent is the owner's own channel history — 193 auto-derived Shorts deleted in a Partner Program cleanup, because the platform reads bulk derivative clipping as low-effort, repetitive content — and anything beyond the two or three hand-picked cuts needs his explicit word for that episode. The full stage lives in `references/shorts-module.md`; SKILL.md gains the stage section between the publish gate and the numbers read, the script-stage selection step, the gate-package and release-schedule lines in stage 8, a Shorts block in the config fields, an output-contract line, and a checklist item covering the vertical frame check, the related-video link, and the subtitle track.

## 0.2.0 — 2026-11-24

The picture standard, settled by watching. The pilot's first episode was finished twice: once with motion — slow drift on the stills, two generated motion clips, animated chapter chips, a card that revealed line by line — and once without, after the owner judged the motion plainly: it doesn't look good. Four short transition demos (hard cuts, a dip to dark at a chapter turn, cross-dissolves, match cuts) were built from the same twenty-five seconds so the replacement could be chosen on evidence, and the ruling came back for stills and speed: a new image roughly every five seconds, hard cuts only. The rebuilt episode runs 111 beats over 555 seconds, and the owner approved it as the episode. Stage 3 now says a scene is a narration unit, not a picture hold; Stage 4 carries the pacing standard — mid-scene stills at sentence turns on any scene longer than a beat, generated and checked like any other asset — and Stage 6 makes hard cuts the only transition, with drift, motion clips, animated chips, and staged reveals named as out. In the same sitting the owner was offered more literal scenes and chose to keep the channel's abstract symbolic register; Stage 4 records that a style confirmation, once made against an offered alternative, is written into the config with its date and stops being a per-episode question.

Two verification lessons join the sound and delivery rules, both paid for on this episode. The first completes the masking work of 0.1.9: the final mix's bed measured present on broadband meters and sat at digital silence in the phone fold-down's pauses, because its energy lived below what a phone speaker can reproduce — and four placed effects were band-dark the same way. No fader level fixes a sound with no energy in the band the listener's speaker plays. Stage 6 now judges presence in the phone band on the fold-down render: the bed must register in-band in the pauses, every effect must peak in-band at its moment, and a sound that fails is re-voiced into the band or cut, never merely raised. The owner's ear overruled the meters three times on this mix and was right each time; the skill says so, and says what to do when it happens again. The second lesson is about eyes, not ears: one of the transition demos was delivered black from end to end because its verification read the file's metadata — duration, resolution, streams — and never looked at a frame. The checklist now requires every delivered video file to be verified by extracting and viewing frames across its runtime. Metadata describes a file; only frames show a picture.

## 0.1.9 — 2026-11-23

The masking rule. The pilot's second mix was built to the standard 0.1.8 wrote — every stem measured, the bed set by a stated broadband ratio, the swells ducked — and the owner, listening on his phone, still could barely hear the words. The diagnosis pass that followed measured why, and the numbers were more interesting than the theory: inside the speech band the bed was no closer than broadband said, but it bloomed in the narration's pauses (a third of the runtime, at a level above its under-speech level), it closed to within about 24 dB of the voice at the story's biggest beats, and it was pitched piano in the voice's own register, competing for the ear out of proportion to its level. The bed was replaced with a design that lives below the speech band, and Stage 6 now carries the lesson as standing method. The mix standard names the industry's settled narration practice instead of leaving each episode to derive its own: ducking by sidechain compression keyed on the narration, not gain automation alone; frequency slotting, the bed high-passed and carved out of the speech band so it occupies different territory from the voice; the voice chain checked the standard way and processed only where the check shows a need, with the finding recorded either way; loudness mastered to the platform's targets; and a small-speaker fold-down produced and measured as the reference listening condition. Above the techniques sits the rule they serve: a bed is judged by masking — its measured level inside the speech band against the voice, in bed windows and at swells — not by broadband ratio alone, and a bed whose energy lives in the speech register is carved out of the band or replaced with one that lives below it. The verification checklist carries the speech-band figure, the voice-chain finding, and the fold-down.

## 0.1.8 — 2026-11-23

The sound pass. The pilot's first mix was built the way mixes get built when no stage owns them: the bed set by a fixed offset under the narration, the swells by another fixed offset, one effect placed, and a single loudness reading of the finished file standing in for mastering. The owner heard the result — music competing with the words — and asked whether any of it had been measured. It had not. Three steps now own the sound. Stage 5 gains verification by ear: the rendered narration is transcribed with a speech-to-text model and diffed against the script before assembly, because the misreads that matter are audible, not visible; artefacts (a number written two ways, a homophone spelled the other way) are classified and set aside, and a substantive mismatch is a re-render decision for the owner, never a silent patch. Stage 6 gains the per-scene sound pass: every scene in the storyboard gets a recorded sound decision — a designed effect, a texture or bed change, a swell, or deliberately nothing — tied to what the scene is doing, with its reason in the production notes; a motivated moment the story plainly has (a message arriving, an approval, a deletion, a cup set down) is placed, and restraint becomes a set of decisions instead of a default that skips them. Stage 6 also gains the mix-and-mastering standard: every stem measured before mixing, the bed set by a stated ratio to the dialogue, swells ducked under speech so they bloom in the pauses, and the master held to the channel's integrated loudness and a true-peak ceiling — with the per-stem figures, the ratios, and the master's numbers recorded in the production notes. The verification checklist carries items for all three.

## 0.1.0 — 2026-11-23
