# The four formats

The full templates. `SKILL.md` carries the pipeline every format runs through; this file carries what makes each format itself: its promise, its structure, its chapter rhythm, its word budget, its music rule, and its title formulas.

Every format here was reverse-engineered from a reference video that proved the shape in public. The references are named per format. Their view counts are the counts at the time they were studied; they are evidence the shape can work, not a promise it will.

Shared measures, all formats:

- Narration runs at about one hundred and forty-five words a minute. Word budgets below are built on that rate; a channel whose locked voice runs faster or slower re-prices its budgets once, at setup, from its pilot episodes.

- One scene covers ten to twenty seconds of narration. Scenes change on hard cuts. No format below uses camera movement as a crutch; where movement appears, it is the footage itself moving, not an effect laid over a still.

- Captions are burned in, in the channel's caption treatment, in every format.

- The call to action appears once mid-video and once at the end, in the channel config's exact words, in every format.

## Story A — second person, one narrator

**Reference:** "Your Life as a Ruthless Hedge Fund CEO" — Hypothetically. About eight minutes, more than half a million views at study time.

**Narrative structure:** governed by the storytelling skill's templates when that skill is present; this file carries the production shape — length, rhythm, voice, music, visuals.

**The promise:** for the length of the video, the viewer is the character. Not watching a life — living it, in second person, present tense.

**Structure:**

- Cold open inside the character's day, at a specific moment with a specific detail. No preamble, no "welcome back." The first thirty seconds establish who the viewer now is and what their world costs and pays.

- The day, or the arc, proceeds in chapters. Each chapter raises the stakes: a bigger number, a bolder move, a nearer consequence.

- A reversal lands about every ninety seconds — the win that turns, the loss that teaches, the luck that arrives dressed as skill. The escalation loop is the engine: attempt, outcome, refrain, bigger attempt.

- The ending is a turn, not a summary. The last chapter reframes the first: the viewer leaves seeing the character's world — and, quietly, their own — differently.

**Chapter rhythm:** a chapter every ninety seconds to two and a half minutes. In the reference, five chapters carry about eight minutes.

**Word budget:** about one thousand one hundred and sixty to one thousand seven hundred and forty words — eight to twelve minutes.

**Voice:** one narrator, dry and even, brisk pace. The delivery does not sell the drama; the specifics do. Exaggerated emotion breaks this format.

**Music rule:** swells at story beats only. The reference uses about six music cues in eight minutes, each under a turn or a reveal. Between beats, the narration carries alone or over the faintest bed. One sound effect at a story moment is allowed; a soundscape is not.

**Visuals:** flat, simply drawn scenes on plain grounds, in the channel's palette. Static shots, hard cuts. Occasional full-screen title cards mark time jumps ("SIX MONTHS LATER"). Large emphasis words may appear, set as type, at a refrain. The script is the product; the visuals are the cheapest honest carrier, and the reference's numbers prove the carrier is enough.

**Script engine:** specificity. A number in nearly every beat — prices, hours, sizes, counts — because specific detail is what makes a second-person life believable. Short declarative sentences. A repeated refrain the viewer can predict by the third chapter is a feature: prediction is participation.

**Title formulas:**

- "Your Life as a [role the audience wants, fears, or wonders about]"

- "Your Life as a [role] With [the striking specific]"

**Failure modes:** a narrator who acts; stakes that stay flat for two chapters; an ending that summarises instead of turning; specifics that turn out to be factual claims about the real world with no source (guardrail 2 applies to claims, not to the character's colour — and the production notes must keep the two separate).

## Story B — dialogue, several voices

**Reference:** "From broke to Wall Street trader" — Zero Budget Stories. About sixteen minutes, more than two million views at study time.

**Narrative structure:** governed by the storytelling skill's templates when that skill is present; this file carries the production shape — length, rhythm, voice, music, visuals.

**The promise:** a small character the viewer roots for, played in scenes, climbing from nothing to the top of a world — and the climb costs him something by the end.

**Structure:**

- Open at the bottom: the character's lack is concrete — an amount owed, a notice on the door, a shift about to start.

- Scenes alternate between narration bridges and played dialogue: the character against a boss, a sceptic, a mentor, a rival. Each scene changes the character's position — a first small win, a public win, an offer, a bigger table.

- The middle is the rise, compressed: montage stretches where music and captions carry the scene and dialogue goes sparse.

- The final act is the biggest stake the story can hold, and the ending is a fall or a price paid, not a clean win. The reference ends on the character's face, in close-up, crying. The fall is why the video gets shared.

**Chapter rhythm:** chapters of about two to five minutes, each named for the stage of the climb ("Escaping wage cage", "Hired by Wall Street", "The billion dollar trade").

**Word budget:** about one thousand seven hundred and forty to two thousand six hundred and ten words of spoken text — twelve to eighteen minutes. Dialogue is sparse by design; a large share of the runtime is music over scenes, so the spoken budget sits below what the runtime alone would suggest.

**Voices:** several distinct character voices — the lead, and one voice per recurring counter-player (boss, anchor, rival). Delivery is exaggerated and fast compared to Story A; emotion is the carrier here. Voices must stay cast: the same character keeps the same voice across scenes and across episodes.

**Music rule:** a near-constant emotional bed. The reference carries more than forty music cues in sixteen minutes; tracks are credited in the description. Music is this format's second narrator.

**Visuals:** one simply drawn recurring character, composited over photographic or painted backgrounds. Images are reused across the video without apology — the same location shot serves every scene set there. Static shots, hard cuts, bold captions, a small channel watermark. Crude is the register; polish would break it. A premium brand cannot carry this register, which is why most channels hold Story B in reserve.

**Title formulas:**

- "From [the low point] to [the high point]"

- "[Character] vs [the world that underestimates him]" — for series episodes once the character is known.

**Failure modes:** voices that drift between scenes; a rise with no price at the end; dialogue written like narration (people in scenes do not explain the plot to each other); production polish creeping in until the format's cheapness — its honesty — is gone.

## Explainer — reframe, mechanism, walk, application

**Reference:** "The Real Reason Fasting Attracts God (What the Bible Actually Reveals)" — BEHIND THE SCRIPTURE. About twenty-four minutes, more than five hundred and eighty thousand views at study time, on a channel of about nine thousand subscribers. The page labels the video as made with AI.

**Narrative structure:** governed by the storytelling skill's templates when that skill is present; this file carries the production shape — length, rhythm, voice, music, visuals.

**The promise:** the viewer has heard the topic explained before and come away with the wrong idea. This video gives them the real one — and proves it step by step.

**Structure:**

- The hook names the gap: a thing everyone treats as simple, optional, or settled — "here is what the usual teaching misses."

- The reframe: three or four "it is not this" statements clear the wrong idea away, each one short enough to remember.

- The mechanism: one clear sentence the whole video hangs on — the reason the thing works the way it does. Everything after this is evidence for that sentence.

- The walk: the evidence, step by step, in the order the source material itself presents it. Each step is a chapter. Quote cards and fact cards carry the load-bearing lines as type on screen, so the viewer can check the words rather than trust the narrator.

- The application: "what this means for you" — the mechanism turned back on the viewer's own case, with the smallest honest first step.

- The call to action sits mid-video, softly, right after the strongest evidence chapter, and again at the end.

**Chapter rhythm:** chapters of about two to three minutes. In the reference, nine chapters carry about twenty-four minutes. The description's chapter list and the spoken material must agree — the reference itself has a panel drift where headings and content sit one chapter apart, and viewers forgave it; do not rely on forgiveness.

**Word budget:** about two thousand one hundred and seventy-five to three thousand six hundred and twenty-five words — fifteen to twenty-five minutes.

**Voice:** one narrator, calm and measured, mid-range, even pace. Reverent where the subject asks for it, plain everywhere. The voice sounds like a person who has read the material and is not performing it.

**Music rule:** a soft ambient bed under the entire video, low enough to forget. No sound effects. The bed is the room tone of the channel's world.

**Visuals:** illustrations in one consistent style and palette, changing every ten to twenty seconds, plus quote cards for the lines that matter. Fully static; no camera movement. Images may repeat across a long video — the reference reuses its key quote card — but a card must match the narration under it when it appears.

**Title formulas:**

- "The Real Reason [topic] [does the surprising thing] (What [the source] Actually Reveals)"

- "Why [familiar thing] Is Not What You Think"

**Failure modes:** a reframe with no mechanism under it (a hot take, not an explainer); a walk that wanders off the source's own order; application that promises more than the mechanism supports; a second topic smuggled into the back half.

## Retelling — the complete story

**Reference:** "The Book of Enoch: The Entire Story From the First Page to the Last" — The Buried Library. About fifty-nine minutes, more than one point seven million views at study time, on a channel of about twenty thousand subscribers.

**Narrative structure:** governed by the storytelling skill's templates when that skill is present; this file carries the production shape — length, rhythm, voice, music, visuals.

**The promise:** completeness. The whole subject, from its first page to its last, in one sitting, told well enough that the viewer never needs the summary version again. Completeness is the hook and the title says so.

**Structure:**

- A cold open of pure mood: in the reference, about fifty seconds of music and imagery before a single word. The subject's weight is established before its facts.

- The hook is a mystery or a gap: a thing quoted but never explained, a book lost for a thousand years, a record everyone cites and nobody reads. Two to three minutes that make the next hour feel necessary.

- The story itself, in chapters inherited from the subject's own structure — its books, its eras, its acts. The narrator does not reorganise the subject; the subject's own order is the spine, and the chapters are named for it.

- Inside each chapter, the same micro-rhythm: the events, then the one line that says why they matter, then on. Interpretation is seasoning, never the meal.

- The close is legacy: what the subject changed, where its fingerprints still are, and the questions it leaves open. The final line returns to the cold open's image.

**Chapter rhythm:** chapters of about three to six minutes. In the reference, fourteen chapters carry about fifty-nine minutes.

**Word budget:** about four thousand three hundred and fifty to eight thousand seven hundred words — thirty to sixty minutes.

**Voice:** one narrator, deeper and slower than the Explainer's — a documentary register, measured, unhurried. No dialogue, no characters voiced. The authority comes from steadiness over an hour.

**Music rule:** a cinematic ambient bed, near-continuous, low under narration. The opening may run music alone for up to a minute. No sound effects beyond what the imagery implies.

**Visuals:** mixed media held in one register — painted scenes, archival images, engravings, documents — plus numbered fact cards in one template (in the reference, gold on black: "10,000 YEARS", "10 · GATES OF THE WINDS") that mark the story's load-bearing facts. Some filler will be loosely matched to the narration; the register, not the match, is what the viewer reads. Sources are listed in the description, as credibility the viewer can check.

**Title formulas:**

- "[Subject]: The Entire Story From the First Page to the Last"

- "The Entire Story of [subject]"

**Failure modes:** completeness promised and not delivered (a summary wearing the title); the narrator's opinions crowding out the subject; chapters reorganised into the teller's pet order; a flagship attempted before the channel's shorter formats have proved the voice and the look — the Retelling is a quarterly flagship, not an opener.

## Choosing the mix

A channel's menu, set in its config, usually looks like this: one lead format that carries the cadence, one second format for range, the Retelling once a quarter as the flagship search keeps finding, and Story B in reserve unless the brand's register can carry a meme character. Judge each format only after five published episodes (guardrail 5), and let the numbers read — funnel actions first, views second — move the mix.
