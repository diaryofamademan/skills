# Worked example — one episode through all nine stages

A public worked example of the funnel-operator pipeline, at summary depth. The channel is Hidden Worth, a firm that gets software companies paid for the data they already produce. The episode is its pilot's first: a Story A, "Your life as a founder sitting on a goldmine." The channel's owned concept is that the data you already have is income, and every episode drives to one action: a five-question pre-screen that returns a Data Income Report — a defensible price range for a company's records, labelled a screening estimate.

Nothing below is a script. It is the shape of the work, stage by stage, so a new channel can see what each stage takes and what it hands on.

## Stage 1 — Topic engine

The topic came from the channel's well: the question founders ask the firm most, in the firm's own inbox and pre-screen — some version of "we have years of records; is any of it worth anything?" It sat in the backlog with its three scores:

- **Search demand: medium.** Founders search the plain phrasings ("sell company data", "what is my data worth") in modest volume, and the volume grows each time AI training data makes the news.

- **Story strength: high.** The topic carries a full day-in-the-life arc — a founder, a growing pile of records, a buyer's world he has never seen — with escalation built in.

- **Funnel fit: high.** The story ends exactly where the channel's endpoint begins: the character finally asks what his records are worth, which is the pre-screen's first question.

The topic passed the veto (funnel fit) and led the backlog, so it became pilot episode one and the channel's Story A proof.

## Stage 2 — Script

Written from the Story A template: second person, present tense, one dry narrator, a target of ten minutes — about one thousand four hundred and fifty words at the channel's narration rate.

Sources named at the top of the script file: the firm's public positioning record (for the proof line and the promise), the firm's published description of what buyers purchase (for the buyer's-world chapters), and the channel folder's screening-range notes (for the ranges the character hears, stated in the video as ranges, never as a promise). The character's own figures — his team's size, his record volumes — are story colour, marked as colour in the production notes, because they describe a fictional founder, not a claim about the world.

Chapter outline:

- **0:00 — You wake up owning a goldmine and a payroll problem.** The hook: the viewer is a founder, sixty people, a good product, and a bank balance that does not care. Under the business, unnoticed, sits the asset.

- **1:30 — The records pile up.** Support conversations, sales calls, internal decisions — years of them, growing every day, treated as exhaust. Specifics do the work: how many, how far back, what they cost to store and ignore.

- **3:15 — Somewhere else, people pay for this.** The buyer's world, seen for the first time: AI labs buying records of real work and real judgment, because models learn from what people actually did. The character's exhaust is someone else's raw material.

- **4:45 — The broker's offer.** A broker arrives, friendly and vague, offering to "take the data off your hands" for a single quiet number. The turn: the character almost signs, because nobody has ever shown him a price.

- **6:15 — What the labs actually buy.** The mechanism chapter: not files, but judgment — decisions, exceptions, fixes, outcomes, recorded over time. The character replays one support thread and hears, for the first time, what he owns. Mid-video call to action, in the config's exact words.

- **7:45 — The number you never saw.** The character gets his records priced properly — a range, with the reasoning visible — and the range is not the broker's number. No client story is told; this is the character's arc, and the firm's proof line is carried in the description, not dramatised.

- **9:15 — The turn.** The close: the mine was never under the company. It was the company, keeping its own records, all along. Final call to action.

## Stage 3 — Storyboard

About thirty-five scenes at ten to twenty seconds each, every prompt built as the style block first, then the scene. Three scenes, as examples of the range:

- **Scene 1 (0:00–0:18).** A founder's desk before dawn, drawn flat in the channel's slate and gold: laptop glow, a payroll spreadsheet on screen, a coffee going cold. The narration's first lines land here. One idea: the weight of an ordinary morning.

- **Scene 14 (about 3:30).** The records pile, visualised: a wall of conversation threads, call recordings, and decision notes stacked like archive boxes in a dark room, one gold thread running through the stack. One idea: volume, unnoticed.

- **Scene 22 (about 6:20).** A fact card, built from the style bible's card template — gold type on deep slate, the channel's diamond mark at the top: the line the mechanism chapter hangs on, set as type so the viewer can read it twice. One idea: the definition, held still.

## Stage 4 — Assets

Scenes generated in rounds of six or fewer clips and stills. Two scenes failed the style check on the first pass — one drifted photographic, one invented text inside the image — and were regenerated with the style block unchanged. The card was built from the template, not generated. Cash cost and round count went into the production notes for the published log.

## Stage 5 — Voice and music

The channel's locked narrator voice — one male voice, dry, measured, a touch of deadpan — read the script at the standing pace. Music followed Story A's rule: no bed under the ordinary chapters, swells at the broker's arrival and at the turn. Captions burned in, white, lower third, checked line by line against the script.

## Stage 6 — Assembly

Hard cuts throughout. Chapter markers set from the outline. Loudness normalised to the channel's standing level. The assembled file — ten minutes and a few seconds — became the final cut candidate, untouched by anything after this stage except the gate.

## Stage 7 — Packaging

Packaging ran the stop-the-scroll round: ten candidate titles from the format's formulas, each scored against the sea-of-100 and stand-alone tests. The winner and its top runners-up:

- "Your Life as a Founder Sitting on a Goldmine" (winner — the format's own formula and the channel's owned concept in story form; it stands alone for a stranger, and "goldmine" stops the scroll)

- "Your Life as a Founder Whose Data Is Worth Six Figures" (runner-up — specific and strong, but it states a figure the video itself gives as a range)

- "The Founder Who Sat on a Goldmine for Six Years" (runner-up — a real story shape, but third person in a second-person format)

The subtitle round picked the description's opening line the same way, from ten candidates: "You already produce the records. Here is what they are worth — and how a founder finds out."

The thumbnail-words round picked from ten candidates too. The thumbnail, from the style bible: one image — the archive-room scene, the gold thread bright — the winning words, "SITTING ON A GOLDMINE", the Double Diamond in the corner, readable at phone size.

The description: two plain lines on what the video is; the chapter list with timestamps; the call to action with the pre-screen link; the sources the script used; and the firm's proof line in spoken form — built by the founder of Token Metrics, a data business with more than eighteen million dollars of lifetime revenue, now licensing its own company's dataset the same way.

## Stage 8 — The publish gate

The final cut went up unlisted, disclosure set. The gate package to the channel owner was one message: the unlisted link, the winning title with its runners-up and the line on why it won, the thumbnail, the description. The owner approved the exact cut and the winning title. Only then was the episode scheduled public, on the channel's cadence slot.

Once public, the run finished its last steps. The pinned comment went up immediately from the channel account — the pre-screen call to action in the config's exact words, with its link — and was pinned. The end screen was set to the funnel endpoint. The first-hour check confirmed the pin, the end screen, the captions, and the disclosure on the public copy, and the check was logged in the production notes.

## Stage 9 — The numbers read

At the weekly read, this episode's line in the performance ledger carries: views, click-through rate, average view duration, subscribers gained, pre-screen starts, pre-screen completions. What the read is watching for, written down in advance: whether the broker chapter holds viewers or loses them (the mid-video dip is where Story A lives or dies), and whether completions — not views — justify making the second Story A in the same shape. As pilot episode one of its format, it cannot be judged yet: the format gets five episodes (guardrail 5). Its job in the ledger for now is to set the channel's baseline and its real cost per finished episode.
