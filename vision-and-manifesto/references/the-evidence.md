# The evidence

Every load-bearing claim in `SKILL.md`, with its source, its standing, and its limits. Grades: **(a)** measured research, peer-reviewed or large-sample; **(b)** practitioner framework or retrospective case work, used as structure or vocabulary, never as causal evidence.

## What the procedure stands on

**Image-based language — grade (a).** Andrew M. Carton, Chad Murphy, and Jonathan R. Clark, "A (Blurry) Vision of the Future" (*Academy of Management Journal*, 2014), and Carton and Brian J. Lucas, "How Can Visionary Leaders Overcome the Illusion of Invincibility?" (2018). Vision statements built on concrete, image-based language outperform those built on abstract value words, because an image gives followers something to simulate and act on; value words do not. The same program names the default failure "blurry vision bias": in the samples studied, fewer than one in ten leaders used strong imagery. The four-value limit comes from this program too: the benefit of naming values holds when the set is small — four or fewer — and washes out as the list grows. **Limits:** much of the evidence is experimental and short-horizon; it measures the language's effect on followers' understanding and effort, not decades of firm performance. The skill uses it for what it measures: how vision language should be written.

**The structure — grade (a), archival.** Carton's NASA study (2018), on the Kennedy-era record: the vision worked through a structure, not a slogan. One aspiration (a leading spacefaring nation, in the archive's terms) was narrowed to a concrete, quantifiable objective (a man on the Moon, within the decade) and broken into milestones, in language employees could map to their daily work — the engineer and the janitor could both say where their job touched the objective. **Limits:** a single, extraordinary case, state-funded, with a wartime-grade mandate; the skill takes the structure, not the scale of the resourcing behind it.

**Content, attributes, and communication — grade (a).** J. Robert Baum, Edwin A. Locke, and Shelley A. Kirkpatrick, "A Longitudinal Study of the Relation of Vision and Vision Communication to Venture Growth in Entrepreneurial Firms" (*Journal of Applied Psychology*, 1998). Vision attributes and content were related to venture growth, and the path ran through vision communication: what the vision said mattered, and its being said — repeatedly, in the organization — carried the effect. **Limits:** entrepreneurial firms in one industry sample; growth is a noisy outcome. The skill takes two things: content is not decoration (what the vision contains matters, not only that one exists), and an uncommunicated vision does not compound.

**The decision gate — grade (a).** Christopher K. Bart, Nick Bontis, and Simon Taggar, "A Model of the Impact of Mission Statements on Firm Performance" (*Management Decision*, 2001): mission statements related to performance only where they changed behavior and alignment — the document's effect, where it existed, ran through what people did differently. Claudine Gartenberg, Andrea Prat, and George Serafeim, "Corporate Purpose and Financial Performance" (*Organization Science*, 2019), in a sample of roughly half a million employee responses: purpose alone showed no relation to performance; purpose paired with clarity — middle managers and frontline staff able to state what the purpose meant for their work — did. **Limits:** both are correlational at the firm level; the skill uses them as a design rule (test for changed decisions and for clarity), not as a promise that passing the gate causes performance.

## Weighed and labeled

**Collins and Porras, *Built to Last* (1994) — grade (b), retrospective case lore.** Contributes the field's working vocabulary: core ideology, and the BHAG, the big audacious goal. The method selected companies that had already succeeded and read their traits backward as causes; Phil Rosenzweig's *The Halo Effect* (2007) is the standing critique of exactly that inference. The skill uses the vocabulary where it is handy and rests no procedure on the causal claim.

**The mission-statement meta-analyses — the caution that shapes the design.** Desmidt, Prinzie, and Decramer (2011) and Alegre, Berbegal-Mirabent, Guerrero, and Mas-Machuca (2018) find the link between having mission statements and performing to be weak and inconsistent across studies. The skill reads this correctly: the artifact alone is not an intervention. Hence the gate, the mapping test, and the decision log — the parts of this skill that are not writing.

## Excluded, by name

**Simon Sinek, the Golden Circle ("Start With Why," 2009).** Excluded for three reasons, stated so a user who asks gets them plainly: the frame has no peer-reviewed test; the brain biology offered as its mechanism (the "limbic brain" account of decision-making) is discredited neuroscience; and its evidence is selected success stories, the Halo pattern in its purest form. Popularity is not a grade. If a user wants the why-how-what ordering as a drafting convenience after hearing this, that is their call — it enters a run, if at all, as the user's own preference, never as this skill's method.
