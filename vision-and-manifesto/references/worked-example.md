# Worked example — SpaceX, read through the method

A public case, worked with public material only: SpaceX's stated aim to "make life multiplanetary." It is the cleanest widely known specimen of the pattern this skill teaches — and of the costs that come with it, which this example carries rather than hides. Nothing here is a claim that the method caused the outcomes; it is a reading of the language against the method's tests.

## The scene (Step 1)

The future SpaceX describes is not an abstraction like "advancing spaceflight." It is a place: a self-sustaining city on Mars — people living there permanently, growing food, making fuel, raising children who were born on another planet. A stranger can picture it without help: domes or habitats, a sky a different color, a settlement that no longer needs Earth to survive. That picturability is the first thing the method looks for, and it is present in full. Compare the industry-standard alternative — "to be the leading provider of space transportation services" — which pictures nothing and could be said by any firm in the sector.

## The vision (Step 2)

The pattern the NASA study found is visible almost piece for piece:

- **One aspiration.** Humanity as a multiplanetary species — stated in one breath, carrying the scene's central image (life, on more than one planet) rather than a synonym for ambition.

- **A concrete objective.** Not "go to Mars someday": a self-sustaining city — a population threshold with a survival test built in. The city either sustains itself or it does not; a stranger can say what done looks like.

- **Milestones, ordered and checkable.** A privately built rocket to orbit (Falcon 1, 2008). Routine reusability, the economic hinge of the whole argument (first-stage recovery 2015, reflight 2017). Crewed flight (2020). A fully reusable heavy vehicle (the Starship program) as the milestone the city's arithmetic depends on. Each is a fact the world can verify, in the order the path requires.

- **Values, few and behavioral.** The company names almost no values in the abstract. What it repeats instead are working rules a stranger can recognize as behaviors: make it reusable, delete the part, question every requirement. Whether or not one admires them, they pass the method's test — stated as things people do, not nouns the company admires.

## The mission (Step 3)

The present-tense work, as the company states it in its own materials, is the design, manufacture, and launch of advanced rockets and spacecraft — true today, specific enough that a satellite firm or an airline could not swap in its mission unnoticed, and plainly the engine of the vision's first milestones. The fit test the skill applies — mission as the vision's first milestone, stated as today's job — holds.

## The gate (Step 4)

Public reporting supplies decision-test material in all three domains:

- **Money.** Reusability was funded as the central program through years when expendable rockets were the proven revenue path — the objective overruling the old default, at real cost.

- **Hiring.** The company's recruiting is famous for selecting on the mission itself: the vision is used as a filter, and the manifesto-like public material around it does the self-selection work the module describes — people join *for* the city, and people who find the aim absurd stay away, which is the filter working in both directions.

- **Standards and refusal.** Vertical integration — building in-house what the industry buys — is the anti-belief in action: a refusal of the sector's default supply chain, paid for continuously.

The clarity mapping test also passes in public view: employees at every level of the company are routinely quoted mapping their specific work — a valve, a weld schedule — to the Mars objective, in the form the NASA study described.

## The costs, carried (Guardrail 7)

The method requires the record to price specificity, and this case is the standing exhibit of the price:

- **Deadline slippage, in public.** The company's timelines are measurably, famously optimistic — Mars mission dates announced for the early 2020s have moved repeatedly, and vehicle milestones have slipped by years. A quantifiable vision makes misses countable. The company pays for its specificity in credibility on every slipped date, and keeps paying, because the alternative — vagueness — would forfeit the coordination the specificity buys. The skill's position: that trade can be right, but it must be made knowingly, and the record must state it before the number is adopted, as this example now does for the case.

- **The "scale talk" critique.** Yang (2026) names a rhetorical risk in language of this kind: scale itself — millions of people, a species-level claim — can do persuasive work that outruns the engineering and the ethics underneath it, recruiting belief by magnitude rather than by evidence. The method's guard against it is already in the procedure: the objective must be verifiable by a stranger and the milestones checkable one by one, so the scale claim is continuously audited against countable waypoints. Where a vision's scale cannot be decomposed that way, the critique should be read as a warning the run has failed Step 2.

## What the example teaches

Three things. First, the pattern is portable: aspiration, countable objective, ordered milestones, image intact — the same structure the archive study found at NASA appears here in a private company's public language, which is why the skill teaches structure rather than taste. Second, the manifesto module fired legitimately: recruiting self-selection is a named job, the beliefs genuinely filter, and the public long form earns its cost. Third, specificity is a loan, not a gift: it buys coordination and belief at an interest rate set by every public deadline. Companies that cannot service the loan should borrow less — a nearer objective, honestly stated — rather than default in public.
