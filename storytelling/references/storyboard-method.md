# The storyboard method: scene cards

A scene card is the smallest unit a story can be handed off in.

The storytelling skill designs the story.

The cards carry the design to whoever produces it — an editor, a designer, a voice artist, or a channel operator skill — without the story being re-decided on the way.

## What a scene card holds

Each card holds six fields.

**Scene.** A number and a short name that says what happens: "Scene 4 — The invoice on the whiteboard."

**Function.** One of three words: advance, reveal, or reverse.

Advance moves the story toward its end.

Reveal gives the audience something it did not know.

Reverse turns the story's direction.

A scene with no function word does not get a card; it gets cut or merged at design stage.

**Turn.** The value the scene turns, written as a before and an after: "Control: assumed → measured."

The value is something that matters to the central figure or the audience — safety, trust, wealth, control, status, knowledge.

If the before and the after are the same state, the scene is footage, not story.

**Visual.** What is on screen or on the page while the scene plays: the shot, the frame, the graphic, the document.

One idea per scene.

The visual serves the turn; it never decorates past it.

**Line or beat.** The narration lines the scene carries, or the beat in outline for a scene played without words.

This is where the hook's promise, the re-engagement beats, and the closing payoff are pinned to exact scenes.

**Length.** The scene's share of the whole, in seconds or in words, so the card set adds up to the piece's planned length.

## The function test, applied card by card

Before a card set is handed off, every card is read against two questions.

First: does this scene advance, reveal, or reverse?

Second: does a value end the scene in a different state from where it began?

A card that fails the first question is cut or merged into the card before or after it.

A card that passes the first question and fails the second is rewritten until the turn is real — usually by moving the moment of change inside the scene instead of between scenes.

Two neighbouring cards with the same function and the same turn are one scene wearing two costumes; merge them.

A card whose visual cannot be produced with the tools the producer actually has goes back to design, not to production with a hope attached.

## How cards become a storyboard

The card set, in order, is the storyboard.

Read top to bottom, the function column tells the story's logic: a healthy set mixes advances with reveals and lands at least one reverse before the final third.

The turn column, read the same way, is the arc drawn in values: the states should move, overall, along the shape the design chose — down then up, up then down, and so on.

The visual column is the production list: every frame, graphic, and document the producer must make or find appears exactly once, attached to the scene that needs it.

The line column is the script's skeleton: narration written against the cards inherits their order and their turns, so the script cannot drift into scenes the design never approved.

In a channel workflow, the channel operator skill takes the card set as the input to its storyboard and asset stages, and production questions go back against the cards — never against memory of a conversation.

## Example card set

Three cards from the worked example in `references/worked-example.md` — a second-person story video, "Your life as a founder sitting on a goldmine."

| Field | Card |
|---|---|
| Scene | Scene 1 — The 9 a.m. inbox |
| Function | Advance |
| Turn | Control: comfortable → questioned |
| Visual | A founder's support inbox rendered as a simple product frame; the message count climbs as the narration runs |
| Line or beat | The hook: "By nine this morning, forty-one people have written to your company. By tonight, every one of those messages will be worth nothing. That is not true of almost anything else you own." |
| Length | About 30 seconds |

| Field | Card |
|---|---|
| Scene | Scene 5 — The competitor's announcement |
| Function | Reverse |
| Turn | Knowledge: "our data is exhaust" → "our data is an asset class" |
| Visual | A plain news-style card: a rival company has licensed its own records; the founder's inbox frame from scene 1 returns beside it, unchanged |
| Line or beat | "A company your size, in your market, just got paid for the same kind of records sitting in your archive. They did not build anything new. They sold what they already had." |
| Length | About 45 seconds |

| Field | Card |
|---|---|
| Scene | Scene 9 — The same inbox, repriced |
| Function | Advance (the payoff) |
| Turn | Control: questioned → owned |
| Visual | The scene 1 inbox frame again, the same messages, now each carrying a small price marker; the frame holds, then cuts |
| Line or beat | "Nothing about your company changed this week. The inbox is the same inbox. The only thing that changed is that you stopped giving it away." Then the channel's closing call to action, in the channel's own words. |
| Length | About 40 seconds |
