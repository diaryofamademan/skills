# Worked example: "Your life as a founder sitting on a goldmine"

One public story taken through the whole procedure in `SKILL.md`.

The story is the pilot episode of a business channel run by a firm that gets software companies paid for data they already own.

The format is a second-person story: one narrator walks the viewer through a single day as a founder, and the day keeps escalating.

Everything below is public shape: the firm's public offer, its public proof line, and a story pattern any founder in its audience would recognise as their own week.

No client is named, because the story is a pattern, not a case.

## Step 1: The job and the audience

The job: hold a founder's attention for about ten minutes, change one belief — "our records are a cost" into "our records are an asset" — and end at one action, the firm's free five-question pre-screen.

The audience: founders of business software companies with roughly fifty to two hundred people.

They arrive holding a model in which their support inbox, their sales calls, and their usage records are exhaust — the cost of doing business, deleted or ignored.

## Step 2: The arc

Chosen shape: fall then rise — the man in a hole.

The day starts comfortable.

It falls as the founder discovers, piece by piece, that the asset has been leaking value for years and that a competitor has already sold theirs.

It rises when the founder prices the records, runs the screening, and ends the day owning something they started the day giving away.

The shape fits the material because the belief change is a fall and a climb: comfort, loss, recovery on new terms.

Lessons used: the six measured arcs [measured · Reagan et al., 2016]; man in a hole as the shape audiences never tire of [framing · Vonnegut's story-shapes lecture].

## Step 3: The structure

Ten minutes, at roughly a hundred and forty-five spoken words a minute, is about fourteen hundred and fifty words of narration.

Nine scenes carry it.

Scenes 1 to 3: the comfortable morning, and the first crack — the archive the company pays to store and never opens.

Scenes 4 to 6: the fall — what the records actually contain, the competitor's announcement as the reversal, and the price of the years already deleted.

Scene 7: the re-engagement beat at the story's low point — the founder asks what the records would fetch, and the answer is a range, not a shrug.

Scenes 8 to 9: the rise — the screening, the report, and the same inbox repriced.

The escalation is a staircase: each scene's discovery is larger than the last, and the largest lands last.

Lessons used: escalate in a staircase [practitioner · MrBeast production practice]; re-engagement beats planted where attention fades [practitioner · MrBeast production handbook].

## Step 4: The hook

The first thirty seconds carry the whole episode, so the promise is stated in the first lines, with the day already moving:

"By nine this morning, forty-one people have written to your company. By tonight, every one of those messages will be worth nothing. That is not true of almost anything else you own."

The promise: by the end of this day, you will see the inbox you curse as the asset you have been giving away.

The tension: a gap the viewer can see — messages that are somehow worth something and somehow worth nothing — and cannot yet fill.

No greeting, no channel name, no warm-up.

Lessons used: open on the promise in the first seconds [practitioner · MrBeast production handbook]; the intro must match what the title promised [platform-declared · YouTube Help]; curiosity as a visible gap [measured · Loewenstein, 1994].

## Step 5 and 7: Three scene cards

The full design runs to nine cards; three are shown here, in the format `references/storyboard-method.md` defines.

| Field | Card |
|---|---|
| Scene | Scene 1 — The 9 a.m. inbox |
| Function | Advance |
| Turn | Control: comfortable → questioned |
| Visual | The founder's support inbox as a simple product frame; the message count climbs as the narration runs |
| Line or beat | The hook lines above |
| Length | About 30 seconds |

| Field | Card |
|---|---|
| Scene | Scene 5 — The competitor's announcement |
| Function | Reverse |
| Turn | Knowledge: "our data is exhaust" → "our data is an asset class" |
| Visual | A plain news-style card: a rival company has licensed its own records; the scene 1 inbox frame returns beside it, unchanged |
| Line or beat | "A company your size, in your market, just got paid for the same kind of records sitting in your archive. They did not build anything new. They sold what they already had." |
| Length | About 45 seconds |

| Field | Card |
|---|---|
| Scene | Scene 9 — The same inbox, repriced |
| Function | Advance (the payoff) |
| Turn | Control: questioned → owned |
| Visual | The scene 1 inbox frame again, the same messages, now each carrying a small price marker; the frame holds, then cuts |
| Line or beat | "Nothing about your company changed this week. The inbox is the same inbox. The only thing that changed is that you stopped giving it away." Then the call to action: the five-question pre-screen, in the channel's own words |
| Length | About 40 seconds |

Every scene in the full set passes the same test these three pass: a named function, and a value that ends in a different state from where it began.

Lessons used: every scene turns a value [practitioner · McKee, Story]; the scene-function test [practitioner · this skill's scene test].

## Step 6: The ending

The ending answers the beginning.

The hook said the morning's messages would be worth nothing by tonight.

The final scene returns to the same inbox, the same messages, repriced — the promise paid in the opening's own image, not in a summary.

The last narration line lands, the call to action follows in the channel's words, and the video cuts.

There is no outro, because the payoff is the end.

The firm's public proof line sits in the description, in spoken form: built by the founder of a data company with more than eighteen million dollars in lifetime revenue and more than a hundred thousand users, licensing his own company's dataset right now.

Lessons used: end on the payoff [practitioner · MrBeast production practice]; the ending answers the beginning [practitioner · this skill's operating rule].

## Step 8: What the feedback loop will read

When this episode publishes, its retention curve gets read against this design.

The intro score tests the hook's promise against the title.

The dip map tests the staircase: any dip before scene 7 means an escalation beat is missing or late.

The final-third retention tests the ending: if viewers leave before scene 9, the payoff was visible too early or priced too low.

Any lesson those numbers contradict gets rewritten in `references/lessons.md` with this channel's own data as the source.
