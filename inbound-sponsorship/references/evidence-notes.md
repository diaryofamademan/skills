# Evidence notes

The studies and doctrine behind the rules in [`../SKILL.md`](../SKILL.md). Each note states what the source found, what it did not find, and which rule it carries. Labels match the skill: `studied` means peer-reviewed research or a documented dataset; `borrowed` means adjacent-field evidence or doctrine applied to publishers by inference; `practitioner` means working consensus among sellers, with no study behind it.

All sources were read on 2026-11-19 for the research pass behind version 0.1.0. Content is paraphrased; no source text is reproduced beyond short figures.

## Anchoring and the first offer

**Petrowsky et al. (2025), *Organizational Behavior and Human Decision Processes* 191.** A preregistered meta-analysis of 374 effects from 90 studies (N = 16,334), plus two preregistered experiments (N = 2,121). Findings: a general first-mover advantage, g = 0.42; a positive correlation between first-offer magnitude and agreement value, r = 0.62; an advantage of ambitious over moderate first offers, g = 1.14. Costs: ambitious first offers produce more impasses (g = −0.42) and worse subjective value for recipients (g = −0.40). Anchoring drove counteroffers; anger drove impasses. Both the benefits and the costs shrink as the number of issues in the negotiation grows.

Carries: step 3, rules 1 and 2. This is the headline evidence for the whole skill. Use r = 0.62 as the defensible figure for the first-offer effect, not the r = .85 from the single early lab study below.

Source: https://ideas.repec.org/a/eee/jobhdp/v191y2025ics0749597825000603.html

**Galinsky & Mussweiler (2001), *Journal of Personality and Social Psychology* 81(4).** The foundational first-offer experiments. In one experiment the first offer correlated with the settlement at r = .85, and Harvard's Program on Negotiation summarizes the literature as the first offer explaining 50–85% of the variation in outcomes. Treat r = .85 as a single small lab result, cited for history, not as the expected field effect. Anchoring itself replicated robustly in the Many Labs project (Klein et al. 2014; 36 labs, about 6,300 participants).

Source: https://www.pon.harvard.edu/daily/negotiation-skills-daily/negotiation-research-you-can-use-make-stronger-first-offers-in-multi-issue-negotiations-nb/

**The information condition — Gunia et al. (2013), as summarized in the negotiation chapter of the *Handbook of Social Psychology* (6th ed.).** Negotiators can make the first offer when they have a good sense of the bargaining zone: their own reservation price and a sense of the counterpart's. Otherwise, delay offers and exchange information first. This qualification is what turns "move first" from a slogan into the lane policy.

Carries: step 2 (the lane test) and step 3, rule 1.

Source: https://guscooney.com/wp-content/uploads/2025/05/handbook_negotiations.pdf

**Maaravi & Levy (2017), "When your anchor sinks your boat," *Judgment and Decision Making* 12(5):420–429.** Documents a first-offer *disadvantage* under information asymmetry: when the counterpart knows the market or their own valuation and the offerer does not, the offerer's anchor tends to land too low, leaving money on the table and revealing priorities. Directly relevant to a publisher facing a professional media buyer on a placement type it has never sold.

Carries: step 3, rule 1 (the "let them open" half).

## Precision

**Janiszewski & Uy (2008), *Psychological Science* 19(2):121–127.** Precise anchors produce smaller adjustments. In their task, a precise anchor of $799,800 produced estimates of $784,671 against $751,867 for a round $800,000 anchor. An analysis of 12,581 home sales found precisely listed houses sold closer to list.

**Mason, Lee, Wiley & Ames (2013).** Precise first offers draw more conciliatory counteroffers and signal knowledge.

**Lee, Loschelder, Schweinsberg, Mason & Galinsky (2018), "Too precise to pursue," *OBHDP* 148:87–100.** The counterweight: across six studies (N = 1,580) and archival real-estate data (N = 11,203), precise offers reduce the chance a counterpart enters the negotiation at all, because precision reads as inflexibility. A related line of work finds a too-much-precision effect with experts.

Together these carry step 3, rule 4: round published prices that must invite inquiry; precise, justified figures once a negotiation is running.

Sources: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Precision_bias · https://business.columbia.edu/faculty/research/too-precise-pursue-how-precise-offers-discourage-negotiation-entry

## Packages (MESOs)

**Leonardelli, Gu, McRuer, Medvec & Galinsky (2019), *OBHDP* 152:64–83.** Multiple equivalent simultaneous offers: two or three packages of equal value to the offerer, differing across issues such as price, term, scope, and deliverables. Across six experiments, MESOs produced stronger anchors and better outcomes for the offerer, greater joint outcomes, and a more cooperative climate, because recipients read the packages as a sincere attempt to agree and countered less aggressively. Two packages performed about as well as three.

Carries: step 3, rule 3, and the package rows in the media-kit checklist.

What it does not support: any fixed percentage lift in average ticket from three-tier pricing. Figures like "+15–30%" circulate in vendor content with no methodology and are not taught in this skill.

Sources: https://papers.ssrn.com/sol3/papers.cfm?abstract_id=732665 · https://www.pon.harvard.edu/daily/negotiation-skills-daily/managing-the-negotiators-dilemma-nb/

## The budget question

**No experiment exists** that randomizes asking budget before versus after quoting, in media sales or (as found) in any sales setting. The rule in step 3 is built from doctrine plus anchoring theory and is labeled `borrowed`.

**MEDDIC Academy, "Why you should never ask about a customer's budget."** Three arguments: asking for a budget asks for an anchor, and once pronounced, any number above it reads as too expensive; the word "budget" itself frames a ceiling; and the question shifts the conversation from value to price before the economic case exists. The same source concedes the transactional exception: budget qualification (BANT) remains standard for short-cycle, low-value, inbound sales — which is exactly the card lane.

**The Futur, price bracketing (practitioner method).** State a range beginning with the high number ("packages typically run $X to $Y"), then ask whether that works for the buyer's budget. The reaction reveals fit without letting the buyer's uninformed number become the anchor. A travel-industry triage guide makes the same move in form design: budget as a dropdown of brackets, not a blank box, placed after the logistics questions.

Carries: step 3, rule 5, and the budget line in step 1's qualification pass.

Sources: https://meddic.academy/why-you-should-never-ask-about-a-customers-budget/ · https://thefutur.com/content/how-to-talk-about-price-or-budget

## Fixed versus negotiated pricing (borrowed evidence)

No study randomizes publishers to published versus negotiated sponsorship pricing. The lane policy rests on three borrowed legs, and the skill says so wherever the policy appears:

- **Buyer-preference and transparency data from B2B SaaS** (secondary compilations; primary reports not all opened): buyers expect pricing visibility (Gartner, 72%); transparent-pricing companies converted demos better and closed faster (OpenView); hiding price raised raw form fills while lowering pipeline conversion (HockeyStack, 31M visitors). Directional, not publisher data.

- **Pricing-institution economics:** a posted price saves bargaining time and commits the seller; bargaining enables price discrimination but hands surplus to the stronger bargainer. Against professional media buyers and agencies — strong bargainers — a posted price protects the publisher.

- **Practitioner split by ticket:** media-kit guides warn that kits without rates create friction and invite lowballs; creator coach Justin Moore advises the opposite for custom deals — no pre-set pricing, ask goals first, scope D.U.E. before quoting. The two camps describe different lanes, and the skill's policy is the reconciliation: publish where the deliverable is fully specified, negotiate where it varies, with a rough boundary around $15,000–$25,000 borrowed from the transparency literature and the BANT-to-MEDDIC switch point.

Sources: https://successknocks.com/when-to-hide-pricing-on-a-b2b-saas-website/ · https://creativesonfirepodcast.com/brand-partnerships-insider-tips-with-justin-moore/ · https://www.castfox.net/blog/how-to-write-podcast-media-kit-that-gets-sponsors · https://sponsorgap.com/blog/how-to-write-newsletter-media-kit

## Qualification frameworks

**BANT** (Budget, Authority, Need, Timeline; IBM) is a fast intake filter built for transactional sales. **MEDDIC / MEDDPICC** (Metrics, Economic buyer, Decision criteria, Decision process, Identify pain, Champion; PTC, 1990s) is deal inspection for complex, multi-stakeholder sales. Practitioner consensus (Offbook): use BANT as the intake filter and switch to MEDDIC at a contract-value or stakeholder gate, around $15,000–$25,000. No head-to-head trial of the frameworks exists; adoption is consensus, labeled `borrowed`. Alternatives worth knowing: CHAMP (leads with the buyer's challenge, not the budget), SPICED (adds the critical event — the dated thing that creates urgency, often a product launch in sponsorship), ANUM (authority first).

Carries: step 1's qualification pass and step 2's threshold.

Source: https://www.offbook.pro/blog/bant-vs-meddic

## Response speed — and the myth

**Oldroyd / InsideSales Lead Response Management Study (2007).** Three years of data, six companies, more than 15,000 leads and 100,000 call attempts. Calling within 5 minutes rather than 30 made contact about 100 times more likely and qualification about 21 times more likely; odds fell more than tenfold inside the first hour. The study measured contact and qualification, not closed revenue — the skill states this limit wherever the figures appear.

**Oldroyd, McElheran & Elkington (2011), "The Short Life of Online Sales Leads," *Harvard Business Review*.** An audit of 2,241 US companies: firms responding within an hour were nearly 7 times as likely to qualify the lead as firms that waited an hour longer; 23% never responded at all; average response time was 42 hours.

**The myth.** "78% of buyers buy from the first responder" has no primary source. A source audit traced its oldest appearance to an unsourced line in a 2008 vendor white paper. The closest real measurement is 6sense's 2025 survey of about 4,000 B2B buyers, where the vendor the buyer contacted first won about 80% of the time — a measure of which vendor the buyer already preferred, not of reply speed. The skill names this figure only to forbid it.

Carries: step 6, including the honest-evidence wording in the output contract.

Sources: https://signalscaler.com/blog/lead-response-speed-automation · https://ainora.lt/blog/78-percent-buy-from-first-responder-study-2026

## Disclosure

**FTC Endorsement Guides, 16 CFR Part 255 (revised 2023).** A material connection between endorser and advertiser must be disclosed clearly and conspicuously: difficult to miss and easily understood. FTC native-advertising guidance accepts labels such as "Ad," "Advertisement," and "Sponsored Advertising Content," and treats "Promoted" as ambiguous. Under Google's link policy (2019), paid links carry rel="sponsored" or rel="nofollow". A playbook that teaches selling unlabeled or guaranteed-dofollow placements as a standard product is teaching a liability.

Carries: guardrail 4 and gate 3 in step 1.

Source: https://www.getdigitize.com/blog/sponsored-content-vs-earned-media/
