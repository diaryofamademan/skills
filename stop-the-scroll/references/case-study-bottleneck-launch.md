# Case study: one launch, fourteen versions

The session this skill was distilled from: the launch of an open-source agent skill (a bottleneck-fixing procedure, itself published in the same repository as this skill), across X and LinkedIn, in a single evening in November 2026. Fourteen versions of the launch were drafted. Each rejection added a principle; the thirteen in the main skill are the session's complete harvest. This file tells the story in the order the lessons landed and prints the final copy.

## How the versions died

**Version 1 died of no hook.** It opened on the receipt — the fact that the thing had been built — and walked the material in order. The author's verdict was that it had no AIDA at all: no attention stage, just an announcement. Principles 1 and 3 are the fix: the reader's problem first, built to stop a scroll.

**The middle versions died of insider language.** Drafts named the author's systems by their internal labels and quoted the operating numbers bare — a rate per hour and a target rate per hour, presented as self-evidently dramatic. To anyone outside the author's operation they were neither. Principles 4, 5, and 6 are the fix: universal framing, every number carrying who waits and what it costs, and things named as what they are (the operation became "the software factory I am building").

**Four consecutive versions died of a leak.** While directing the work, the author made a side comment about what the thing should be called. The drafter forced that comment into the copy, version after version, as if an instruction about the piece were a line of the piece. Principle 13 is the fix, and it is the session's most expensive lesson per word: direction is not copy.

**The late versions died at the link.** The call to action pointed at the code repository, landed abruptly after a bare label, and the link's preview card showed the author's photo rather than the work's mark. Principles 9, 10, and 11 are the fix: the call to action as its own complete post, the link eased into the piece's motion, and the destination moved to the author's own site, where a reader can leave an email.

**The formatting died last.** A draft set one sentence per line throughout, reading as a staircase of fragments. Principle 12 is the fix: paragraphs of one to two sentences, sentences flowing together inside them.

## The final copy

The hook that survived, both platforms: "What is the one thing in your business that everything else waits on? Most people never find it. They are too busy fixing everything else." A direct question about the reader's own problem (principle 1), framed as a universal problem (principle 2), complete alone (principle 3). The body's second beat is the confession: "I was doing exactly that."

The body carries exactly two numbers, each with its why: the software factory "finishes 3 pieces of work an hour. The goal is 10 an hour, every hour. Customer fixes wait in line. Everything new waits behind them." The numbers are the impact because the waiting is spelled out (principle 5).

The call to action that survived, as its own post in the thread: "Every system has one step everything else waits on. Fix that one first, or nothing else you fix matters. That is the whole job of fix-the-bottleneck, a skill my agents run. You can download it for free here: ianbalina.com/skills/fix-the-bottleneck" — the maxim, the name, and the link to the author's site (principles 8, 9, 11), with the link preview carrying the skill's own mark.

## What the session proves, and what it does not

It proves the principles are load-bearing for this author and this kind of launch: remove any one of them and a specific, named version of the launch dies. It does not prove they are complete. They are one evening's doctrine, earned on one piece of copy, and the skill's contributing note stands: a new principle needs a dead draft behind it.
