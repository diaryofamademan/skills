# Hook and CTA patterns

Construction detail for steps 1 and 3 of the main procedure: the shapes that recur in ends that pass the tests, and how the two tests are actually run.

## Running the two tests

**The sea-of-100 test.** Put the line, alone, in a list with the last twenty hooks (or calls to action) you can remember from your own feed. Read down the list at scroll speed. The line passes if it is the one you stop on, and if you can say in five words why: it asks something you feel, it claims something you doubt, or it names something you recognize. "It is well written" is not a why.

**The stand-alone test.** Screenshot the line alone, with no thread, no author bio, no surrounding posts. Show it to the coldest reader available (or simulate one honestly). The line passes if that reader can say what the piece is about, why it matters, and — for a call to action — what to do next, without asking a question.

## Hook shapes that recur

**The reader's-problem question.** "What is the one thing in your business that everything else waits on?" The question names the reader's problem in the reader's vocabulary and lets the reader supply the personal details. This is the default shape; reach for it first.

**The confession as second beat.** The hook asks; the line after it confesses: "I was doing exactly that." The confession converts the question from an accusation into a shared problem and earns the author the right to teach. It belongs at the top of the body, not inside the hook itself.

**The universal reframe.** When the topic is technical, the hook names the universal shape of the problem (everything waits on one step; the queue nobody sees; the number that lies) and lets the body supply the technical instance. The reframe is a framing act, not a summary: it chooses which universal problem this piece is *about*.

## CTA shapes that recur

**The maxim plus the name.** The piece's sharpest earned sentence, stated as a general truth, followed by the name of the thing and the link: "Every system has one step everything else waits on. Fix that one first, or nothing else you fix matters. That is the whole job of [the work]." The maxim is the jab; the name makes it actionable; the stand-alone test passes because all three elements are present.

**The closing restatement (long-form).** On platforms where the piece is read whole (a LinkedIn post, a newsletter), the call to action can end with a one-line restatement of the hook's answer after the link: the link is eased into mid-flow, and the final line returns the reader to the idea. The stand-alone requirement still applies to the block that carries the link.

## Thread architecture

A thread's beats, in order: **hook → story → my example → stat → big win → call to action**. Between each pair of beats sits a transition, and the transition is a bridge line inside a post ("My version of that line is..."), never a fact the reader is expected to remember from earlier in the thread.

Three of the posts are load-bearing and must each stand alone with full context: the hook (principle 3), the story, and the call to action (principle 9). The story is the one most often failed: told as an analogy or an anecdote, it is tempting to open it with a lean on the surrounding thread ("The book tells it as a hike"). That opening borrows its setup from context the screenshot will not carry. The fix is mechanical: rewrite the story post with its setup inside it, the way a story that begins from its own first line needs nothing before it.

The beat before the call to action is the **big win**: the end result, the conclusion the piece has been walking toward. The call to action then converts a reader who has just seen the outcome, not one who has only seen the problem.

## The results post: in vs out, and the actors who missed it

When a thread reports what a system or a test actually did, two moves carry it.

**Lead with in vs out.** If the system loses most of what enters it, open there: "26 tasks went in. Only 3 shipped." The gap is the story. The stages (the leaks, the rates) are the explanation and come after the gap is felt.

**Show smart actors missing the whole picture.** When the piece's claim is "this sees what others miss," reporting the failure flatly ("errors had no names") proves nothing. Show the others missing it, each locally right and globally blind: "Every agent blamed a different bottleneck. Each one fixed what was in front of it. Nobody was looking at the whole hose, so the kink stayed hidden." Note the callback doing double duty: the whole-metaphor image is what the actors were not looking at.

The founding launch's final results post, all moves together: "26 tasks went in. Only 3 shipped. / The leaks: only 1 in 4 tasks got done, and only 1 in 2 of those shipped. / Every agent blamed a different bottleneck. Each one fixed what was in front of it. / Nobody was looking at the whole hose, so the kink stayed hidden." In vs out first, the source's own numbers as the leaks, the blind actors, and the callback to close.

## Draft the load-bearing post in full, three or four ways

A drafting practice, not a principle: when one post carries the argument, draft it three or four **complete** ways and let the author pick. In the founding launch's later rounds, every decision came in one tap once full versions were in front of the author; half-edits and explanations of options stalled. Complete versions make the differences visible and the choice cheap. This applies to the post that carries the argument, not to every post: drafting everything four ways spends the author's attention where it buys nothing.

## The callback

When the story sets a strong visual metaphor, the reader carries that picture for the rest of the thread. The later beats must use it. The rule: **the method line, the big win, and the call to action each call back to the metaphor at least once, in the story's vocabulary, rather than switching back to the abstract term.** The callback deepens the metaphor each time it lands, and the piece ends inside the story it opened instead of stepping out of it to summarize.

The metaphor is the whole image, not a single word from it. The founding launch's story is a hose with a kink — but the metaphor is the tap, the water, the hose, and the kink together: the system is the hose, and the bottleneck is its kink. Callbacks that repeat only the one striking word flatten the picture back into a slogan. Draw on the full world, and at least once, state the mapping plainly, out loud.

From that launch: the method line reads "Every system is a hose. Find the kink, the step everything waits on, and fix it first" — the mapping stated in the open. The win line ends "...the skill found the kink in the hose in one night," its first half carrying the tap ("Weeks of turning the tap up never found it" — the author's weeks of fixing everything else, in the story's terms). And the call to action opens "Every system is a hose with a kink in it." The abstract word (bottleneck) still appears where precision needs it; the metaphor carries the beats the reader remembers.

## Making the story punchy

The story post faces the sea-of-100 test too: it must stand out in a sea of 100 stories. A flat telling of a good story still fails. The treatment, worked out on the hike story from *The Goal* (flat version: one slow kid in the line, the line can only move as fast as he does, they move him to the front):

1. **Name the character.** The kid in *The Goal* is Herbie. A named person sticks in the reader's head where "one slow kid" slides off.
2. **One concrete detail.** Herbie's pack is stuffed with cans. One picture does the work of a paragraph of explanation about why he is slow.
3. **Give the stuck beat its own short line.** "Nobody can pass him." The moment the whole story turns on gets a line to itself, at full stop.
4. **Lean on the backwards turn, and end on an image.** The fix is the opposite of instinct: the slowest kid goes in front, and the fastest kids carry his extra weight. End the post on the picture of the result — the troop walks into camp together — not on a summary of what it means. The meaning is the next post's job.
5. **Give the story stakes.** Something that can be lost: a deadline, a cost, or something at risk, stated in the setup. The corrected Herbie was better but still not punchy enough; what closed the gap was a clock — "The troop has to make camp by dark," and at the end, "and they make camp in time." The facts did not change; the tension did. A story where nothing can be lost is a story the reader can safely leave.

Then run the **logic pass**, the third story test, earned by the first punchy draft of this very story: it placed Herbie at the back of the line and then said nobody could pass him. If he is at the back, they already have. In the book, Herbie walks in the middle of the line: the kids behind him pile up, gaps open ahead of him, and the troop arrives when Herbie arrives — which is also the truer picture of a bottleneck in a business, where the slow step is rarely at the end. The rule: every physical claim in the story must hold together under a plain reading. Punch is no license to break the story's own mechanics, because a reader who stops to question how the story works has stopped scrolling for the wrong reason.

## What kills ends, collected

- Opening on the receipt ("I built X") instead of the reader's problem.
- A hook that needs the thread to make sense.
- Numbers presented as self-evidently dramatic.
- A call to action that asks generically ("check it out", "link below") instead of landing.
- A link that lands on a repository when the author's site exists, or whose preview shows the author's face instead of the work's mark.
- The author's margin notes, pasted in as if they were copy.
