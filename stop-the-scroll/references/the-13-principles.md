# The thirteen principles, in full

Each principle states the rule, why it exists, the failure it prevents, and the quick test that checks it. The principles are grouped as they apply: hook (1–3), body (4–7), call to action (8–11), formatting (12), and drafting discipline (13). They were distilled from one launch session; the case study in this folder is the record.

## The hook

**1. The hook is a direct, personal question about the reader's own problem.** Talk to the reader, not at them. Why: a question about the reader's own situation is answered involuntarily; the reader is inside the piece before deciding anything. Failure prevented: the announcement opening ("I built a thing"), which asks the reader to care about the author first. Test: is the reader the subject of the first line?

**2. The problem is framed as a universal problem.** Even when the topic is engineering, the framing is the wait, the waste, the dependency — things every reader has lived. Why: the feed is mostly non-specialists, and a problem only specialists can feel caps the piece at the specialty's size. Failure prevented: the insider hook that preaches to the already-converted. Test: could a smart reader from a different field feel this problem in their own work?

**3. The hook must stand out in a sea of 100, stop the scroll, and stand on its own.** Full context, no thread needed; the hook works as its own post. Why: distribution is screenshot-driven; the hook is the part that travels, and it usually travels alone. Failure prevented: the hook that only makes sense as post 1 of 9, which is read by almost no one. Tests: the sea-of-100 test and the stand-alone test, run as described in `hook-and-cta-patterns.md`.

## The body

**4. Simple, high-level language for multiple audiences.** No geeky detail, no math nobody outside the field cares about. Why: every layer of specialty detail is a door closing on a class of reader, and the middle's job is to be carried, not to be complete. Failure prevented: the body that becomes a spec sheet and loses the room. Test: read each paragraph and ask what a non-specialist would repeat at dinner. That repeatable sentence is the paragraph's payload; if there is none, rewrite.

**5. Numbers never sit bare; every metric gets its why.** The business impact, the second-order effect, who waits and what it costs — not just the granular goal. Why: a number means nothing to a reader who does not already share the author's context; the why is what makes it evidence. Failure prevented: "3 an hour, goal 10 an hour" as a self-evident drama. Test: for every number in the piece, the sentence that follows it (or the clause attached to it) says why it matters.

A statistic chosen to carry a point has a further rule, earned the same night as the rest, while picking the number for a launch's middle. **Stats multiply or reduce: they increase money, increase efficiency, reduce waste, or increase speed.** Three tests, each with the draft that failed it:

- **One visible number.** The eye needs to look at a number, visually. "Finished in less than a third of the time" failed: there is no number there.
- **No reader math.** One number, not two numbers and a ratio the reader has to work out. "At 95% busy, one slow step makes everything wait 19 times longer" failed: it is true, and it makes people do math, so it does not land.
- **A change, not a state.** The statistic shows something going up or down. "Juggling five fixes wastes 75% of the day" and "work spends 95% of its life waiting" are shares and states; both lost to the winner: "Companies that fixed the slow step first cut delivery times by 70%." A cut with a direction beats a share of a day or a state of the world.

**6. Name things as what they are.** A software factory, not "my agents"; the name a stranger understands instantly. Why: internal labels import the author's whole context as a prerequisite. A plain name carries its own explanation. Failure prevented: the piece that reads as a dispatch from inside a company the reader does not work at. Test: every named thing in the piece could be understood by a reader who arrived thirty seconds ago.

**7. Only what the reader needs to know.** Personal facts that do not serve the piece get cut. Why: every fact in a piece competes for the reader's remaining attention, and facts that serve the author's completeness spend it for nothing. Failure prevented: the launch that is half memoir. Test: for each paragraph, name what the reader gains or what the action gains. No answer, no paragraph.

## The call to action

**8. The CTA is a jab, not a generic ask.** It must stand out in a sea of 100 other CTAs and stop the scroll, same as the hook. Why: the end of a piece is where the author's sharpest earned sentence belongs; a generic "check it out" spends the whole piece's accumulated trust on nothing. Failure prevented: the strong piece with a shrug at the bottom. Test: the sea-of-100 test, applied to the CTA alone.

**9. The CTA stands on its own as its own post.** The idea, the name, and the link, full context. Why: like the hook, the CTA travels alone — it is the part most often quoted, screenshotted, and reposted. Failure prevented: the CTA that says "link below" to a reader for whom there is no below. Test: the stand-alone test, applied to the CTA alone.

**10. Ease into the link drop.** Never a bare label-and-link landing without a runway; the link arrives as the end of the piece's motion. Why: an abrupt link reads as an ad break inside the author's own piece and breaks the voice the piece just built. Failure prevented: the "You can download it for free here:" cliff edge. Test: read the two sentences before the link. Do they make the link feel like the conclusion, or like an interruption?

**11. Link to the author's own site, not to a raw repository.** People should land where they can leave an email; the link's preview card carries the work's icon or branding, not the author's photo. Why: a repository link converts attention into a star at best; a home page converts it into a relationship. And the piece promotes the work, so the work's face should be on the card. Failure prevented: the launch whose entire harvest is a handful of repository stars and no subscribers. Test: follow the link as a stranger. Can you leave an email within one screen, and whose branding did you see on the way?

## Formatting

**12. Paragraphs of one to two sentences; sentences flow together inside a paragraph; one blank line between paragraphs.** Never one sentence per line. Why: one-sentence-per-line formatting reads as a draft artifact and trains the reader to skim; flowing prose inside short paragraphs reads as someone speaking with intent. Failure prevented: the thread that looks like a changelog. Test: the piece, read at arm's length, shows paragraphs, not a staircase.

## Drafting discipline

**13. When the author gives an example, that is direction, not copy.** An illustrative jab, a naming aside, a side comment made while directing the work — none of it enters the copy verbatim unless the author marks it as copy. Why: direction is spoken in a different register than publication, and a drafter that cannot tell the two apart will keep publishing the author's margin notes. Failure prevented, from the founding session: the author's comment about what to name the thing was forced into four consecutive versions of the launch before anyone cut it. Test: diff the draft against the author's instructions. Anything that appears in both, word for word, is presumed a leak until proven copy.
