## 0.3.0 — 2026-11-21

Square 3 states what each surface can honestly expect, in seconds. Karen Nelson-Field's attention research was already cited in the square and graded in the nine-squares reference, vendor caveat and all; what was missing was the recorded expectation, one release after 0.2.0 added the recorded awareness stage beside it.

- **One line in Square 3, parallel to the awareness line.** Each channel and surface now states its realistic attention expectation — the active seconds it can honestly expect, set from measured attention evidence rather than platform promises — and the square's reach is planned in those seconds. Attention is treated as measured, never assumed: most advertising receives very little active attention, the first moments of an execution decide whether it earns any, and creative has to win the seconds before any message can land. A buy counted in impressions nobody looked at fails the square however large the number — the square's existing failure mode, now stated as a rule the plan is checked against.

- **The source's standing is already on the record and stays there.** Nelson-Field's work is modern measured research on large-scale viewing data, strongest on video and display attention, and the reference names the interest plainly: the research program is commercial, led by a firm that sells attention measurement, so the thresholds are direction, not constants. This release adds no new citation and changes no grade; it makes the plan spend by the evidence it already carried.

## 0.2.0 — 2026-11-21

Square 3 learns to name its audience's awareness stage. The diagnosis comes from the positioning skill, which gained Schwartz's two-stage diagnosis in its own 0.2.0; this skill consumes the half of it a media plan can act on.

- **One line in Square 3.** Each channel and surface in the media square now names the awareness stage it speaks to — unaware, problem aware, solution aware, product aware, or most aware. The stage is taken from the positioning record's customer awareness stage when a record exists, and from the plan's own Step 0 diagnosis when none does. The reasoning is the square's own attention discipline extended one step: a surface aimed at the wrong stage spends its seconds on buyers who cannot yet hear it — proof offered to buyers who do not know the problem, a problem story told to buyers already comparing products.

- **Nothing else moves.** Square 2 still consumes the record's five chain-out fields as given; the awareness stage reaches Square 3 from the record's supporting record, not through the message square. The nine-squares reference states the same line where it lists what Square 3 contains. Schwartz's intensification craft does not enter this skill in any form, for the same reason it stays out of positioning: the plan's honest-proof guardrail bars claims a buyer cannot check.

## 0.1.1 — 2026-11-19

The output contract now calls in the writing pass, replacing the one-paragraph write-it-like-a-person rule. When a marketing plan ships in raw strategy register, the nine squares read as a consultant's grid and the diagnosis and split hide behind imported terms. The pass runs the work-humanizer skill in rewrite mode over the one-page plan and its evidence notes, then applies the writing-styles skill's plain rules, and the plan is not done until the pass has run on the exact text being delivered. Facts, figures, quotes, names, dates, and statuses never change in the pass.

## 0.1.0 — 2026-11-18

First build. The skill takes a widely used practitioner skeleton — Allan Dib's nine squares, Before, During, After, on one page — and rebuilds what each square stands on, because the skeleton's own evidence is practice-grade and some of its sub-claims are assertion. Every square in this build stands on a named, measured source, labeled with where it was measured. Three layers the skeleton does not have were added around it: a diagnosis step before the grid, a budget layer over the grid, and a chain-in from the positioning skill at the message square.

- **Diagnosis before the grid.** No square is filled until a diagnosis, a guiding policy with its sacrifice stated, and a coherence test exist — Mark Ritson's order of work (diagnosis, strategy, tactics) standing on Richard Rumelt's strategy kernel. Ritson's Bothism referees the grid's standing arguments (niche versus penetration, brand versus activation, difference versus recognition), and the build's resolutions are sequencing and weighting, never camps: the niche is where a plan starts, with the wider category named and a broadening trigger stated; growth targets stay in the Before squares while the After squares are fenced to margin, retention economics, and referral yield.

- **The Before squares on the buying laws.** Square 1 (target market) and square 3 (media) stand on Sharp and the Ehrenberg-Bass Institute: growth from penetration, light buyers, mental and physical availability. Square 3 corrects reach with Karen Nelson-Field's attention measurements — reach planned in attention-adjusted terms, assets in the first seconds, the vendor caveat (the researcher sells attention measurement) carried in the plan. John Dawes's 95-5 rule runs over squares 3 and 5 as a computable frame, not a constant: the plan computes the user's own in-market ratio from their category's buying cycle.

- **The message square on measurement, with a chain-in.** Square 2 does two jobs in order — recognition before argument — standing on Jenni Romaniuk's distinctive-asset measurement (Fame and Uniqueness, consistency over redesign), Robert Cialdini's proof principles at their measured, single-digit effect sizes, and Jonah Berger and Katherine Milkman's sharing data for messages that must travel (the separate arousal-alone claim, which failed two larger replications, is named and not used). The chain-in: square 2 consumes a positioning record when one exists — Owned concept, Market category, Competitive frame, Best-fit customers, Trade-offs — and does not re-derive it; without one, it runs a light message step and names the positioning skill as the deeper route. The boundaries are stated in the skill: positioning owns the concept and the frame, this skill owns the plan that carries them to market; comms owns public moments and how a thing is said, this skill plans the system the moments live in.

- **The During squares on follow-up evidence.** Squares 4–6 stand on James Oldroyd's lead-response research: the 2,241-company audit (37 percent replied within an hour; average reply 42 hours; a quarter never replied) and the 1.25-million-lead field data (contact within the hour, nearly seven times the qualification odds of one hour later, over sixty times a day's wait). The plan sets a response-time standard as a number and audits the user's own times against it. The folklore version of this research — a "391 percent" figure from a software vendor's separate analysis — is flagged in the skill wherever the study is cited and barred from plans. Square 5 reframes nurture as memory-keeping for an out-of-market majority, not pressure. Square 6 uses Cialdini's principles in honest versions only: real proof, real scarcity, real deadlines, expectations at single digits.

- **The After squares on models and program data, not the loyalty gospel.** Square 8 stands on Peter Fader's lifetime-value models: value estimated from recency and frequency and tested against holdout periods, never asserted — with Fader's live disagreement with the Ehrenberg-Bass models carried openly and handed to the user's own cohort data to arbitrate. Square 9 stands on Schmitt, Skiera, and Van den Bulte's referral-program study: referred customers worth at least 16 percent more, churning about 18 percent less, and a reward capped below the measured value gap, the way the studied bank capped 25 euros under a gap of about 40. Square 7 stands on the skeleton's practice discipline, labeled as such, bounded by the penetration cap and by the finding that a referred customer's loyalty partly depends on the referrer still being a customer. Popular sources weighed for these squares and left out — a volume gospel whose checkable claims fail against audience data, a permission manifesto whose principle is kept but carried by law and practice, a loyalty score whose superiority failed independent tests and whose founding retention claim has no source data — are named with their reasons in the skill's limits, and nowhere else in the package.

- **The split over the whole grid.** Les Binet and Peter Field's brand-versus-activation layer: the finished plan states its split as a line with a review date, starting from roughly 60:40 (46:54 business-to-business) as a hypothesis, with the databank caveat — IPA award entries, 996 cases, not a random sample — traveling with the numbers. A fence rule keeps After-phase spending from eating the acquisition budget by drift; any change to the split is a stated decision, not a migration.

- **Evidence geography as a standing rule.** Every imported rule carries where it was measured — packaged-goods panels, UK award cases, US inbound leads, a German bank, business buying cycles — and the plan names the handover checks (response-time audit, asset test, cohort measurement, referral gap) on which the user's own numbers replace the imports. Where a plan serves a raise, its hypotheses stay labeled as hypotheses wherever the plan is cited; the fundraising skill owns the raise itself.

- **Publish shape from the first draft.** Output contract with the plain-style writing rule, the glance test, and loose-bullet formatting; a composite worked example in `references/` (invented names throughout — a bookkeeping service for construction firms, diagnosis to split line); limits and boundaries in the skill itself, including the grid's own practice grade and the three boundary lines against the positioning, comms, and fundraising skills.
