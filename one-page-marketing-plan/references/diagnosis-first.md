# Diagnosis first

The layer that runs before the grid. Dib's nine squares are a sequence of tactics with a discipline — one page, a sentence or two each — and a plan that starts by filling them in has skipped the step that decides whether they are the right tactics. Mark Ritson's order of work is diagnosis, then strategy, then tactics, and Richard Rumelt supplies the shape the strategy step must take. This file is that step, in full.

## Why the order is not negotiable

A filled grid hides its own emptiness well. Every square can be completed with plausible activities — a segment named, a message drafted, channels listed, a referral ask written — and the result reads like a plan while containing no decision at all. The failure is invisible precisely because the page is full. Diagnosis-first exists to make the decisions happen before the activities are chosen, and to make the plan falsifiable: a diagnosis can be wrong, a guiding policy can fail, and both can be found out. A tactic pile can only be busy.

Ritson is a synthesis, not a dataset — grade (b), standing openly on the grade (a) work this skill cites. His contribution here is the sequence and the refereeing. Rumelt is case-based argument, no dataset — also grade (b) — included because the strategy step needs a test for whether a strategy exists, and his kernel is that test.

## The kernel

A strategy, in Rumelt's terms, has three parts. A plan produced by this skill must be able to state all three before square 1 is filled.

### Diagnosis

A diagnosis names the difficulty. It simplifies a complicated situation into the one or two obstacles that actually govern it — not a list of goals, not a SWOT inventory, not a description of the market in general. "Nobody has heard of us" is not a diagnosis. "Buyers in this category choose at contract renewal, every three years, and we are not in the consideration set when renewal comes up" is one: it says how the category is bought, when the decision happens, and where the business is absent.

A working diagnosis draws on:

- **How the category is bought.** Purchase cadence, panel size, how many buyers are light buyers, when buying moments occur. This is where the Ehrenberg-Bass evidence enters before any square does: a diagnosis that assumes buyers are loyal, heavy, and attentive contradicts the measured shape of most categories.

- **The business's own numbers, where they exist.** Where leads actually come from, how fast they are answered, which customers bought twice, what a referred customer turned out to be worth. Thin numbers honestly stated beat a confident plan built on none.

- **The named obstacle.** The specific thing that, if it did not change, makes the rest of the plan pointless. Slow follow-up, absence at the buying moment, a message no one can attribute to the brand — one obstacle, named, is worth ten goals.

### Guiding policy

The guiding policy is the approach chosen to answer the diagnosis — and it is only a policy if it excludes something. Choosing a first segment excludes the rest of the category for now. Choosing recognition over argument excludes the clever campaign. Choosing follow-up speed over a new channel excludes the channel. Porter's point, carried through the positioning skill's gate, applies with full force here: a position, and a policy, exists only where there are trade-offs — named things the business will not do and customers it will not serve yet.

The plan states the policy in one or two sentences, with the sacrifice visible. If the sacrifice cannot be stated, the policy is a wish, and Step 0 is not finished.

### Coherent action

The nine squares are the action set, and coherence is their test. Each square must serve the guiding policy, and the squares must reinforce each other: the media buy carries the assets the message square defined; the nurture cadence keeps the memory the media bought; the referral program spends the experience facts the delivery square can prove. A square that contradicts the policy — a discount-led conversion square under a policy of premium recognition, say — is rewritten, or the policy is restated honestly. What coherence forbids is the quiet contradiction: squares written by different habits at different times, assembled into a page.

## Bothism: the referee

Ritson calls the refusal of false either/ors "Bothism," and the grid generates false either/ors reliably. The skill's standing resolutions:

- **Niche or penetration.** Both, in sequence. A first segment now (a small budget cannot register across a whole category), the wider category named, and a broadening trigger stated. Targeting is a choice, never a hiding place.

- **Brand or activation.** Both, in a stated proportion, on different clocks. The split is a line in the plan (see `the-split.md`), not a mood.

- **Difference or recognition.** Recognition first, difference second. The assets make the message attributable; the one clear thing, aimed at the chosen segment, is what gets attributed. A difference nobody can assign to the brand is spend without residue.

- **Imported evidence or the user's numbers.** Imported evidence sets the starting settings. The user's own numbers are the court of appeal, and the plan names the checks on which they take over.

Where a user's situation genuinely breaks a resolution — a category bought once in a lifetime, a business that is the category locally — the plan states the exception and its reason. Bothism is a referee, not a script.

## What Step 0 hands to the grid

Three things, and only three:

1. **The diagnosis**, in the form the output contract requires — what is happening, the obstacle, in a few lines.

2. **The guiding policy**, with its sacrifice stated.

3. **The evidence the diagnosis already gathered** — the category's buying shape (which square 3 turns into an in-market ratio), the business's response times (which square 4 turns into a standard), the customer base's shape (which square 8 turns into a measurement). Diagnosis is where the user's own numbers first enter the plan; the squares inherit them rather than starting cold.
