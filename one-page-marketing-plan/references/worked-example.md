# Worked example — a bookkeeping service fills the grid

A composite. Every name, business, and figure below is invented, and the shape is labeled for what it is: an illustration of the method, assembled to show what a finished page looks like. No real business appears in it.

**The business.** Ledger & Lane Bookkeeping: a six-person bookkeeping service for small construction firms — payroll, job costing, monthly closes — in one metropolitan area. Most clients arrive unhappy with a bookkeeper they have had for years. Fees are monthly, contracts annual.

## Step 0 — the diagnosis and the policy

**Diagnosis.** Construction firms change bookkeepers rarely — roughly every three years, usually at a tax-season failure or when a loan application exposes messy books. At any given moment only a small share of the region's firms are even open to switching. Ledger & Lane's own record: 31 inquiries last year, average first reply after 26 hours, 9 became clients. The obstacle is twofold: the firm is unknown at the moment a switch opens, and its follow-up is slower than the moment deserves.

**Guiding policy.** Be the familiar name when a firm's books embarrass it, and answer faster than anyone in the category when the moment comes. The sacrifice, stated: no discount-led offers, and no expansion into general small-business bookkeeping this year — construction only, at full fee, until the first segment is won.

## The nine squares

**1 — Target market.** First segment: construction firms of 10–50 staff in the metro area — about 400 firms. Wider category named in the same breath: all metro construction firms, then adjacent trades services. Broadening trigger: when a quarter of the first segment can recognize the firm's name and colors unaided, or at 60 active clients, whichever comes first. *(Seeded from the positioning record's Best-fit customers.)*

**2 — Message.** Square 2 consumes the positioning record, as given:

| Field | What it holds here |
| --- | --- |
| Owned concept | Books a bank will accept — job costing a lender can read |
| Market category | Bookkeeping for construction firms |
| Competitive frame | The incumbent bookkeeper kept too long; the owner's spouse doing payroll at midnight; the big generalist firm that returns calls in a week |
| Best-fit customers | Owner-operators burned by a tax-season surprise or a stalled loan |
| Trade-offs | No discount pricing; no non-construction clients; no tax filing — books only, done properly |

Assets first: a deep green and a ruled-ledger line motif, the phrase "books a bank will accept," and one format — the monthly one-page job-cost summary — carried identically everywhere. Fame and Uniqueness: untested; a 60-buyer recognition test is scheduled before any redesign. One clear thing, from the record's owned concept. Proof: real counts only — 214 construction clients' books closed monthly (the firm's actual number in this composite), two named clients on the record. Nothing in the message promises what the trade-offs refuse.

**3 — Media.** Attention-adjusted reach against the 400 first-segment firms and the wider category's owners: the regional construction association's channels, the trade press's digital editions, and presence at the two events owners actually attend — bought for environments where the green and the phrase get their seconds, assets in the first seconds of every execution. Own in-market ratio, computed: firms switch on roughly a three-year cycle, so in any quarter about 1 in 12 — **8 percent** — is open to switching; the other 92 percent are next year's market, and this square is mostly for them. Physical availability: one phone number and one page, three steps from search to a booked call.

**4 — Capture.** The offer: a free books-health check — a one-page verdict on whether a lender would accept the firm's job costing. Consent: the check is requested, follow-up is named at the point of request, nothing else is sent to anyone who did not ask. Response-time standard: **first contact attempt within one business hour**, audited monthly against the actual log. Last year's 26-hour average is the number this square exists to kill.

**5 — Nurture.** A monthly one-page letter to past inquirers and the wider list: one practical item each time (a lender's current documentation ask, a payroll deadline, a job-costing error and its price), the green and the phrase on every issue, no urgency, nothing expiring. Exit: unsubscribe, or a buying signal — a reply, a health-check request, a visit to the fees page — which routes to square 6 at square 4's speed.

**6 — Convert.** Follow-up cadence: attempts on day 0 (inside the hour), day 1, day 3, day 7, then back to nurture — never chased past day 7. Proof, checkable only: the two named clients, the actual client count, the health check itself as the demonstration. Risk reversal the firm can stand behind: the first monthly close redone free if the client's lender rejects its format. No discounts, per the policy; no deadlines that are not real.

**7 — Experience.** Three checkable facts: the monthly close lands by the fifth business day, every month; a client's question is answered by a person who knows their books, inside one business day; when Ledger & Lane makes an error, the client hears it from the firm first, with the fix already made. Each fact is stated so it could be found false.

**8 — Lifetime value.** Measured before money: the current base by recency and frequency — who pays monthly and current, who has gone quiet, whose fees have grown with their firm. In this composite, the quiet fifth of the list holds most of the churn risk and the growing tenth holds most of the expansion value; retention effort goes to the first, development to the second. Method stated with the figures: a recency-frequency cohort view, re-run every six months and tested against the following period before any figure is spent against.

**9 — Referrals.** Asked of clients whose last close landed early, at that moment, with something worth passing: the health check, offered for a fellow owner's firm. Reward: a fixed credit, capped below the measured gap — in this composite, referred clients stay on average five months longer in year one, worth roughly 900 in fees against 620 for other new clients, so the credit is set at 150, well under the 280 gap, and resets when the gap is re-measured. The program's edge is the gap measurement and referrers who are still clients, not the credit's size.

## The split line

**Split: 46% brand / 54% activation** — the business-to-business starting hypothesis from the IPA databank (award entries, not a random sample), counting the owner's hours as well as spend. Review date: two quarters from adoption. The fence applies: the referral credit and retention effort in squares 8–9 are funded inside the activation-and-after share and do not draw on the association and trade-press spend without a stated change to this line.

## What the example teaches

First, the diagnosis did the heavy lifting: once "unknown at the switching moment, slow when it comes" was named, squares 3 and 4 wrote themselves, and the media square's real audience — the 92 percent not switching this quarter — stopped looking like waste. Second, square 2 did no positioning work: the record arrived with its five fields filled, and the square's whole job was assets, consistency, and proof. Third, every number on the page is either measured, computed in the open (the 8 percent), or labeled a hypothesis with a review date (the split) — there is no fourth kind of number on the page. That, more than any single square, is the method.
