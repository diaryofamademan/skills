# The split

The layer over the whole grid. Dib's squares describe a system; they do not, by themselves, say how effort and money divide between making future demand and harvesting present demand. Les Binet and Peter Field's work is the evidence on that division, and this file is how the finished plan states it, fences it, and hands it over to the user's own numbers.

## Two jobs, two clocks

Brand building and sales activation are different jobs.

- **Brand building** works on people who are not buying yet — which, on Dawes's arithmetic, is most of the market most of the time. Its product is memory: familiarity, the brand coming to mind in a buying situation, a sense of what it stands for. It works slowly, broadly, and emotionally, and its returns compound over years. In the grid, this is chiefly the Before squares, and square 5's memory-keeping.

- **Sales activation** works on the small share who are buying now. Its product is response: the lead captured, answered inside the hour, converted. It works fast, narrowly, and rationally, and its returns are immediate and short-lived. In the grid, this is the During squares.

Neither job substitutes for the other. Activation without brand building harvests a pool that nothing refills, and gets more expensive as it thins — the in-market few are the most contested buyers in the category. Brand building without activation builds memory the business never cashes. The plan's job is not to choose between them but to state the proportion, in the open, where it can be reviewed.

## The evidence, and its caveat

Binet and Field's analysis rests on the IPA effectiveness databank: 996 cases, 700 brands, 83 categories, entries from 1980 onward, extended in later work including the business-to-business studies with LinkedIn. Findings the plan uses: effectiveness peaked at roughly a 60:40 brand-to-activation split across the databank, nearer 46:54 in business-to-business; and cases pursuing penetration goals outperformed loyalty goals by a wide margin — which is one reason this skill keeps growth targets in the Before squares and fences the After squares to margin and yield.

The caveat travels with the numbers, always: **the databank is award entries, not a random sample of campaigns.** Successful, well-documented, mostly large-brand work is overrepresented; a small firm's direct response is barely in it at all. Sharp has made this point in print, and the skill treats it as decisive for how the ratios may be used: 60:40 and 46:54 are **starting hypotheses** — the best available first guess, stated as a guess — and the plan's own results are the only authority that can move them. A plan that prints the ratio without the caveat and a review date has misread a case database as a law of nature.

## Stating the split

The finished plan carries one line:

> **Split: __% brand / __% activation** (starting hypothesis: 60:40 general, 46:54 business-to-business, from the IPA databank — award entries, not a random sample). Review date: ______. Basis for any future change: the plan's own response and memory measures, named below.

Effort counts as well as money: a solo operator's hours are a budget, and a split stated only in currency hides the real allocation. Where the two disagree — money mostly on activation, hours mostly on brand, say — the plan states both.

## The fence

The split is policed by one rule: **After-phase spending does not eat the Before budget by drift.** The After squares are where overspend hides, because their beneficiaries are known, liked, and already paying, while the Before squares' beneficiaries are strangers who have not bought yet. Every quarter, some of the brand share quietly migrates to retention offers, loyalty mechanics, and referral rewards. Each migration may be defensible; the sum of them is the acquisition budget dissolving.

So: any change to the split is a stated decision — new numbers, a reason, a date — recorded on the plan. A plan whose split changed without a decision being recorded has not been managed; it has been spent.

## The user's own ratio

Dawes's 95-5 is a frame, and its author presents it as one. The plan replaces it locally with a computation anyone can run:

1. **Find the category's buying cycle.** How often does a buyer in this category buy — a bank account every five years, a car every six, a coffee every day?

2. **Divide.** The share in market in any period is roughly the period divided by the cycle: a quarterly window on a five-year cycle gives about 5 percent; the same window on a six-month cycle gives about 50 percent. Long-cycle categories are memory games. Short-cycle categories are availability games. The plan's Before/During weighting follows from which game it is in.

3. **Refine with the user's own data when it exists.** Contract lengths, renewal dates, repurchase intervals from the actual base — these outrank the category estimate, which outranks the imported 95-5. The plan shows the number it used and where it came from.

## The handover checks

Imported evidence sets the starting settings. These are the checks on which the user's own numbers replace them, named in the plan with dates or triggers:

- **Response time.** Audit actual first-response times against the square 4 standard. One audit of real leads replaces the imported audit as the plan's evidence — and usually finds the standard is the cheapest growth available.

- **Assets.** Test the distinctive assets on Fame and Uniqueness with real category buyers, even a small sample, before the next redesign conversation. An asset's scores are the message square's facts.

- **The base.** Re-run the square 8 cohort view on a stated cycle, and test any lifetime-value estimate against a holdout — a period the estimate did not see — before spending against it.

- **Referrals.** Once referred customers exist in any number, measure the actual value gap between them and other customers, and reset the reward cap under the measured gap. The German bank's 16 percent is a prior, not a promise.

- **The split itself.** On the review date: what did the brand share buy, in memory terms (recognition, branded search, direct traffic, the assets' scores), and what did the activation share buy, in response terms? Move the ratio on that evidence, in writing, or keep it and say why.

A plan whose checks never run is a hypothesis that refused its own test — and this skill's plans are written to be tested, because that is the whole difference between the evidence they cite and the folklore they replaced.
