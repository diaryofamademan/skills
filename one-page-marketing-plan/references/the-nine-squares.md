# The nine squares

The grid in full. Dib supplies the skeleton — nine squares, three phases, in this order — and the discipline: one page, a sentence or two per square, every element measurable. What follows is what each square stands on in this skill: the buyer behavior underneath it, the evidence it rests on and where that evidence was measured, what the square must contain, and the failure mode it exists to prevent.

A note on how to read the evidence lines. Every source below is graded the way the build graded it: (a) measured — fitted to real buying, real campaigns, or controlled studies, and open to checking; (b) practice — a coherent framework from experience, used as practice and labeled as such. And every (a) source was measured somewhere specific. The geography is stated with each rule, because it is part of the rule: a finding from packaged-goods panels or a German bank is a starting setting for a business that sits inside neither, and the plan's own checks (see `the-split.md`) are where the user's numbers take over.

## Before — prospects

The brand job: building memory among people who mostly are not buying today, so the brand is thought of, and easy to buy, when their moment comes.

### Square 1 — Select target market

**The buyer behavior.** Buyers in a category are mostly light buyers. They buy rarely, from a small repertoire of brands they happen to know, and they do not think hard about any of them. A brand grows by being known to more of them — penetration — not by squeezing more from the few who already buy heavily. Loyalty, such as it is, follows size: big brands have somewhat more loyal buyers because they have more buyers, full stop.

**The evidence.** Byron Sharp with Andrew Ehrenberg and Gerald Goodhardt, and the Ehrenberg-Bass Institute's body of work — grade (a). The buying laws (Double Jeopardy, duplication of purchase, the NBD-Dirichlet model) were fitted to decades of consumer panel data across many categories and countries and replicated outside the institute. Geography: consumer panels, strongest in repeat-purchase categories; the shape — many light buyers, few heavy — is the most replicated finding in this skill.

**What the square contains.** A first segment, named narrowly enough that the budget can register with it; the wider category the segment sits in, named in the same breath; and the broadening trigger — the condition on which the target widens. If a positioning record exists, its Best-fit customers seed the first segment.

**The failure mode.** The niche as a hiding place. Dib's advice to pick a narrow market is sound sequencing for a small budget and a growth ceiling if it hardens into identity: a business that will only ever address its first segment has planned its own limit. His scored index for picking the segment is his assertion, grade (c) as evidence, and this skill does not carry it — the segment is chosen by the diagnosis and the guiding policy, with the sacrifice stated.

### Square 2 — Craft your message

**The buyer behavior.** Buyers barely perceive differences between brands. What survives in memory is recognition: the color, the sound, the phrase, the character that says whose message this is before a single claim is read. And when buyers pass a message on, they pass on what is useful to others or what makes them look good — not what the brand found clever about itself.

**The evidence.** Jenni Romaniuk's distinctive-asset measurement — grade (a): every asset scored on Fame (the share of category buyers who link it to any brand) and Uniqueness (the share who link it to this brand only), a method tested across 1,281 identity elements in 13 categories and 19 countries. Robert Cialdini's principles — grade (a) with an effect-size warning: controlled studies, mostly holding up, several at smaller sizes than the famous stories suggest; expect single-digit effects. Jonah Berger and Katherine Milkman's study of what readers actually forwarded — grade (a) for the newspaper dataset; the companion claim that arousal alone causes sharing failed two larger replications in 2024, and this skill does not use it.

**What the square contains.** First, the asset set: the distinctive assets the message will carry, their Fame and Uniqueness status (tested, or marked untested with a test planned), and a commitment to consistency — assets are not redesigned away. Second, one clear thing said to the chosen segment, drawn from the positioning record where one exists (see the input declaration in `SKILL.md`: Owned concept, Market category, Competitive frame, Best-fit customers, Trade-offs). Third, the proof: real reviews, real counts, real credentials — the Cialdini principles as honest design material. Fourth, if the message is meant to travel, its payload tested against the sharing evidence: practical value, and emotion of the high-arousal kind — awe, amusement, anger at a shared enemy — never sadness, never self-congratulation.

**The failure mode.** Argument without attribution: a beautifully reasoned message that buyers remember, if at all, as the category's message or a competitor's. And its mirror: differentiation theater, a claim of difference buyers cannot perceive, bought at the price of the consistency that actually builds memory.

### Square 3 — Reach prospects with media

**The buyer behavior.** In any given period, most category buyers are out of market — their next purchase is months or years away — and the few seconds of attention a message gets are the whole of what it can deposit in memory. Reach that is never attended to deposits nothing.

**The evidence.** Sharp and Ehrenberg-Bass on reach and availability — grade (a), geography as square 1. John Dawes's 95-5 rule — grade (a) as a derived heuristic, and its author says so: it falls out of purchase cadence (a company replaces a bank or a law firm roughly once in five years, so in any quarter only about 5 percent are buying), which means any user can compute their own ratio from their own category's cycle. Karen Nelson-Field's attention research — grade (a) with a vendor caveat: eye-tracking and biometric measurement at a reported scale of over a million people; about 85 percent of digital ads receive under 2.5 seconds of active attention; memory encoding starts at roughly 1.5 to 2.5 seconds of real attention; attention predicts brand choice better than impressions or time-in-view. The caveat: the researcher founded the company that sells attention measurement, so the thresholds are direction, not constants.

**What the square contains.** The buy planned in attention-adjusted reach: broad against the category, per Sharp, but weighted to the environments and formats where the assets get their seconds, with the assets arriving in the first seconds of every execution, per Romaniuk. The user's own in-market ratio, computed and shown. The awareness stage each channel and surface speaks to, named per surface — the positioning record's customer awareness stage when a record exists, the plan's own diagnosis when none does. The realistic attention expectation of each channel and surface, stated in active seconds from the attention evidence above — the square plans in seconds a buyer actually gives, not in impressions a platform delivers. And the availability half of the square: physical availability — how a buyer who remembers the brand at their moment actually buys, in as few steps as the category allows.

**The game in the square.** Media is bought in auctions, against competitors who see the same formats and react within weeks. Two consequences belong in the plan. Bid to the value of the attention, not to win the auction — an auction won at any price is a loss with a receipt. And expect every format that works to be copied: the defensible asset in the buy is the distinctive asset set, which a competitor cannot copy without advertising the original. Platform prices and formats also move under the plan; a plan written in one platform's current format prices is a plan with an expiry date, so the square is written in attention and availability, and formats are listed as the current tenants of those jobs.

**The failure mode.** Impressions counted as reach, and a dashboard full of delivered ads that nobody looked at — spend that cannot have built memory, reported as if it had.

## During — leads

The activation job: the small in-market share, deciding now. This phase's losses are mostly operational, and its evidence is mostly about speed and cadence.

### Square 4 — Capture leads

**The buyer behavior.** A person who asks is at the peak of their interest, and the peak is short. Interest decays in hours, and the first competent response usually frames the choice — a lead answered tomorrow is answered into a decision someone else already shaped.

**The evidence.** James Oldroyd's lead-response research, with Kristina McElheran and David Elkington — grade (a), two datasets. The audit: a test lead sent to 2,241 US companies; 37 percent replied within an hour, the average reply took 42 hours, about a quarter never replied. The field data: 1.25 million real leads at 42 companies; firms that tried contact within an hour were nearly seven times as likely to qualify the lead as firms that waited one more hour, and over sixty times as likely as firms that waited a day. Geography: US companies, inbound web leads — the user's own response times, once audited, outrank the import. And the folklore flag, stated plainly because this square is where it breeds: the famous "391 percent" improvement figure attached to fast response comes from a software vendor's separate analysis. It is not in this study, and it appears in no plan this skill produces.

**What the square contains.** The capture mechanism — what the prospect is offered and what is asked in return, kept proportionate. The consent basis: permission before contact, carried by consent law and deliverability practice as operating rules. And the response-time standard, written as a number with a scope: a first contact attempt within one hour, inside business hours, measured from the lead's arrival — plus the audit that checks the business against its own standard.

**The failure mode.** A capture page tuned for conversion rate feeding a follow-up process that answers in two days. The square's arithmetic is unforgiving: the best capture mechanism in the category loses to a mediocre one answered within the hour.

### Square 5 — Nurture leads

**The buyer behavior.** Most leads are not refusing; they are not buying yet. Their purchase sits on their calendar, not the seller's quarter, and the brand's task across the interval is to still be familiar — and favorably so — when the date arrives.

**The evidence.** Dawes's 95-5 rule again — grade (a) as a heuristic — applied at the level of the list: the same cadence arithmetic that makes most of the market out of market today makes most of any lead list out of market this month. Romaniuk's consistency discipline supplies the content rule: the cadence carries the same assets, recognizably, every time.

**What the square contains.** The cadence (how often, through which channels), the value it carries (practical value often enough to earn the next opening — Berger's sharing evidence doubles as a content test here), the memory it is keeping (which assets, which one clear thing), and the exit and the signal: how a lead leaves the sequence, and what a buying signal looks like when one appears — a return visit, a question about terms, a reply — so the signal routes to square 6 at square 4's speed.

**The failure mode.** Pressure mistaken for nurture: escalating urgency, expiring offers that expire weekly, aimed at people whose only fault is a buying date in the future. Pressure spends familiarity; nurture was supposed to bank it. A lead list burned by pressure is worse than no list — it is a segment that has learned to ignore the assets.

### Square 6 — Convert sales

**The buyer behavior.** A buyer at the point of decision is resolving risk, not seeking more information: is this the safe choice, do people like me buy it, what happens if it goes wrong. And most conversion failure happened upstream — the lead that cooled while it waited.

**The evidence.** Oldroyd again, on cadence: qualification odds collapse with delay, so the follow-up standard is a conversion variable, not an administrative one. Cialdini's principles as design material — grade (a) with the standing effect-size warning: social proof with field evidence at scale, several signature demonstrations replicated weakly, the seventh principle (unity) on thinner ground. Set expectations at single-digit percentage effects.

**What the square contains.** The follow-up cadence restated (attempts, spacing, the point at which a lead returns to nurture rather than being chased). The offer's proof, honest versions only: real customers, real counts, real deadlines, earned authority — and proof a buyer can check, because proof races inflate: when every competitor displays five stars, unverifiable proof is discounted to zero, and checkable proof is the only kind that still pays. The risk reversals the business can genuinely stand behind.

**The failure mode.** Manufactured proof and fake urgency — out as a guardrail, and also as arithmetic: they convert once, against a buyer who then discounts everything else the brand says, including its assets. Conversion bought by deception is borrowed from squares 5 and 9.

## After — customers

Margin, retention economics, and referral yield. This phase is fenced: its spending is justified by measurement, and it does not draw on the Before budget by drift (see `the-split.md`).

### Square 7 — Deliver a world-class experience

**The buyer behavior.** Customers judge an experience by what they can check — was it on time, did it work, what happened when something went wrong — and they retell the checkable parts. The experience is also the referral program's raw material: nobody passes on an adjective.

**The evidence.** This square stands on Dib's practice discipline — grade (b) — and the skill says so rather than dressing it in borrowed measurement. Two measured findings bound it. The penetration cap (Sharp, grade (a)): experience quality does not repeal the buying laws; a delighted small base is still a small base, and loyalty follows size. And a condition from the referral follow-up research (Van den Bulte and colleagues, 2018, grade (a)): a referred customer's loyalty partly depends on the referrer still being a customer — so the experience of existing customers is load-bearing for square 9, not a separate virtue.

**What the square contains.** The experience defined in observable terms: the two or three facts a customer could verify and repeat — delivery time kept, the failure protocol, the thing competitors in the category reliably get wrong and this business reliably gets right. Each fact stated so it could be checked and found false; an experience claim that cannot be false cannot be passed on either.

**The failure mode.** World-class as a self-awarded adjective, and experience spending justified as a growth strategy. It is a margin and referral strategy, measured in squares 8 and 9, and the plan keeps it there.

### Square 8 — Increase lifetime value

**The buyer behavior.** Customers are not a pool of equal value, and they are not static: some are mid-relationship, some have quietly left while still on the list, and recent behavior — who bought lately, who buys often — is the best available signal of who buys next.

**The evidence.** Peter Fader, with Bruce Hardie — grade (a). The BG/NBD and RFM-to-CLV models estimate future value from recency and frequency, fitted to real transaction data (the CDNOW cohort is the standard demonstration) and checked against holdout periods the models never saw: the estimate is tested, not asserted. Geography: transaction datasets, strongest where repeat purchase exists to be modeled. One disagreement is carried openly: Fader holds that top customers are worth more than the Ehrenberg-Bass models imply, because those models treat buying as static and customers change. The plan does not settle this from authority — the user's own cohort data, tested on a holdout, settles it locally. What both sides agree on, against folklore: the top fifth of buyers supply about half of sales, not four-fifths.

**What the square contains.** The measurement before the money: the cohort view of the current base (recency and frequency, at minimum), the estimated value distribution, and the identification of which customers are actually worth more. Then the spending, aimed by the measurement — retention effort where the model says value is leaving, development where it says value is growing. Every lifetime-value figure in the plan carries its method next to it; a figure without a method is an assertion and is struck.

**The failure mode.** Lifetime value as a slogan for spending on the customers who shout loudest or arrived first, and After-phase budgets justified by an 80/20 split that the data does not contain.

### Square 9 — Orchestrate referrals

**The buyer behavior.** People refer when it costs them little, reflects well on them, and hands the recipient something of practical value. A referred customer also arrives pre-sold by a person they trust — which is why referred customers behave differently in the data, and why the referrer's own standing is part of the asset.

**The evidence.** Philipp Schmitt, Bernd Skiera, and Christophe Van den Bulte — grade (a): about 10,000 customers of a German bank, tracked nearly three years. Referred customers were worth at least 16 percent more than comparable customers acquired other ways; they churned about 18 percent less, and that gap held; the margin gap faded over time. The bank paid a 25 euro reward for a customer worth about 40 euros more — the worked example of a reward ceiling. The 2018 follow-up adds the condition carried in square 7: the referred customer's loyalty partly depends on the referrer still being a customer. Geography: one German bank's retail customers — a starting setting, replaced by the user's own measured gap as soon as referrals exist to measure. Berger's sharing evidence (grade (a), the newspaper dataset) designs the ask: practical value and social currency travel.

**What the square contains.** A program, not a hope: who is asked (selectively — customers whose square 7 experience gives them something true to pass on), when (at the moment the value is freshest and checkable), what they are given to pass on (practical value, phrased so the referrer looks good), and the reward, capped below the measured value gap between referred and other customers — with the gap itself measured on the user's own base once volume allows. The program's economics are stated in the plan: reward, expected referred-customer value, the gap between them.

**The game in the square.** Rewards are visible and copyable; a competitor can match a reward in a day. What they cannot copy quickly is the measured gap — knowing what a referred customer is actually worth, and a base of referrers who are still customers themselves. The plan therefore treats reward size as the weakest part of the program and the measurement as the strongest.

**The failure mode.** Referral as a slogan ("our customers love us, they will tell their friends") with no program, no cap, and no measurement — or its opposite, a reward set by what competitors pay, which is how a program ends up paying 40 euros of reward for 25 euros of value.
