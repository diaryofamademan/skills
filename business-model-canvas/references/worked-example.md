# Worked example — a scheduling tool for independent dental clinics

A composite worked example: the business is invented, the shape of the run is real. It is included because it shows the two moments that decide whether this skill did anything — the fit test refusing the flattering sheet, and the assumptions list outranking the canvas. Details are illustrative, not a real company's record.

## Step 0 — The fit test

The business: software that fills cancelled appointment slots in independent dental clinics by automatically offering them to waitlisted patients.

1. **Is the problem proven?** Partly. Empty slots from late cancellations are a named, costed pain — clinic managers can state the weekly number and what a hygienist hour is worth. Eleven of fourteen clinics interviewed described a manual workaround (a staff member phoning a paper waitlist). Behavior exists; money has not yet moved. Call it a qualified yes.

2. **Is the solution bought repeatedly?** No. Three clinics run a free pilot; none has been asked to pay. Stop here.

**Verdict: still searching.** The Lean Canvas, not the nine blocks. The run notes what the nine-block sheet would have hidden: key partners and customer relationships would have been filled with plausible inventions, and the sheet would have looked like a business. The Lean sheet's blocks ask only what a searching business can answer honestly.

## The Lean Canvas, filled

- **Problem** [evidence]: cancelled slots go unfilled; the manual waitlist call-around takes staff time and usually fails. A filled hygienist hour is worth a known amount to the clinic.

- **Existing alternatives** [evidence]: the paper waitlist and phone calls; doing nothing and absorbing the gap; general practice-management software with a reminder feature that does not backfill.

- **Customer segments** [evidence]: independent clinics of two to six chairs, not chains (chains have central IT and a different buyer). **Early adopters** [assumption]: clinics with a hygienist shortage, where an empty slot cannot be covered by shifting staff.

- **Unique value proposition** [assumption, in customer words from interviews]: "Cancelled slots fill themselves."

- **Solution** [evidence — it exists and runs in three clinics]: automatic offer of freed slots to waitlisted patients by text, first reply takes the slot.

- **Channels** [assumption]: dental supply representatives and regional study clubs; direct outreach by the founder so far, which is a channel of one and does not scale.

- **Revenue streams** [assumption]: monthly subscription per clinic, priced against roughly two filled slots a month. No clinic has yet been charged.

- **Cost structure** [evidence]: messaging costs per slot offered, founder time, hosting. Small and mostly variable at this stage.

- **Key metrics** [assumption]: slots offered per clinic per week; fill rate; staff minutes saved; pilot-to-paid conversion — the metric the whole model currently hangs on.

- **Unfair advantage**: empty, recorded as empty. A head start in one region's study clubs is a lead, not an advantage, and the sheet says so.

## Step 2 — The fit module, on the lead segment

Jobs: keep the schedule full; keep hygienists utilized; avoid front-desk overload. Pains: cancellation gaps, the call-around chore, no-shows. Gains: a full book without staff effort. The value map touches the central pain directly (pain reliever: automatic backfill) and one gain (a full book). It does not touch no-shows, which the clinics named almost as often — recorded as a gap, not argued away. **Fit verdict: problem-solution fit, on the strength of the workaround behavior; product-market fit not yet testable, because no one has paid.**

## Step 3 — The environment, in brief

Industry: the practice-management incumbents could add backfill; their incentive to do it well is weak (it is a feature, not their business), but their distribution is total — every clinic already runs one. Market: independent clinics are consolidating into groups, shrinking the segment at the edges. Partners as incentives: the messaging providers are commodity; the study clubs gate trust and know it. Trends: patients accept text-first scheduling readily, which the model quietly depends on.

## Step 4 — Riskiest assumptions, ranked

1. **Clinics will pay the subscription once the free pilot ends.** If false, there is no model, only a liked feature. Uncertainty: total — never tested. **Next test:** a paid-pilot letter to the three pilot clinics and seven interviewed clinics: the stated price, a start date, a signature line. **Kill result:** fewer than 3 of 10 sign within a month. Owner: the founder. Date: four weeks from the run.

2. **Fill rate holds outside the pilot clinics.** Pilots self-selected for pain. If the true rate is half, the price stops making sense. **Next test:** two non-pilot clinics on the same paid letter, measured over six weeks. **Kill result:** fill rate under 15% of offered slots.

3. **A channel beyond the founder exists.** Supply reps or study clubs must actually carry this. **Next test:** one rep group pilots the offer to its clinic list for a revenue share. **Kill result:** no partner agrees to carry it within two months of asking.

## Step 5 — Triggers

Redraw if: a top assumption fails its test; an incumbent ships backfill as a free feature; the first chain group asks for a contract (a different model, on the nine-block sheet, if it ever happens). Review date: at the read of the first test, six weeks out.

## What the example teaches

The canvas itself took an afternoon. Everything that mattered is around it: the fit test choosing the less flattering sheet, the empty unfair-advantage block recorded instead of filled, the fit verdict naming the no-show gap, and a first assumption whose test is a letter with a price on it. That is the skill's claim in miniature — the sheet is the record; the ranked, testable doubts are the work.
