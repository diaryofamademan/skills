# The two sheets, and the test that chooses

The skill covers both sheets because they answer different states of the same question. The nine-block canvas describes a model that exists. The Lean Canvas interrogates a model that is still a hypothesis. Using the wrong one is not a style error: the nine blocks invite a searching business to invent partners, activities, and relationship programs it has no evidence for, and the Lean sheet invites a found business to keep "searching" past the point where its real questions are about scale. Step 0 exists to make the choice on evidence.

## The nine blocks (Osterwalder and Pigneur)

Filled in the skill's order — demand side first:

1. **Customer segments.** Who, named narrowly. The block the one empirical test found carrying weight (Ladd, 2018) — treat its precision as the sheet's foundation, not its first box.

2. **Value propositions.** What each segment gets, in the customer's terms: the job done, the pain removed, the gain delivered.

3. **Channels.** How the offer reaches the segment — and who owns each channel, because an owned channel and a rented one are different models wearing the same word.

4. **Customer relationships.** What kind of relationship the model runs on: self-serve, assisted, community, account-managed. Each has a cost shape; name the one the economics assume.

5. **Revenue streams.** Who pays, for what, priced how. Numbers where they exist.

6. **Key resources.** What the model cannot run without — assets, people, data, licenses, brand.

7. **Key activities.** What must be done well, continuously, for the demand side to be true.

8. **Key partners.** Who supplies what the company chose not to own — read, in the environment pass, as incentives.

9. **Cost structure.** The costs the blocks above commit the business to, fixed and variable, in the order they bite.

## The Lean sheet (Maurya)

1. **Problem.** The top pains, stated as the customer states them.

2. **Existing alternatives.** What customers do today instead — including nothing. The block the nine-block sheet most conspicuously lacks, and the one that keeps a searching team honest about its real competition.

3. **Customer segments**, with early adopters named separately — the first buyers are a narrower group than the market, and the sheet makes the run say who they are.

4. **Unique value proposition.** One line: the difference, for the early adopter, in their words.

5. **Solution.** The smallest offer that addresses the problem — today's version, not the roadmap.

6. **Channels**, **revenue streams**, **cost structure** — as on the nine-block sheet, at searching-grade honesty: mostly assumptions, labeled.

7. **Key metrics.** The few numbers that say whether the model is coming true — the searching business's substitute for a P&L.

8. **Unfair advantage.** What cannot be easily copied or bought. The hardest block to fill honestly; an empty one is a finding, and most early canvases should have it empty or nearly so.

## The fit test, worked slowly

Ask, in order, and stop at the first *no*:

1. **Is the problem proven?** Has the segment, unprompted, named the pain and paid — in money, time, or a workaround — to relieve it? Interviews where people are polite do not count; behavior counts.

2. **Is the solution bought repeatedly?** Not trialed, not praised — bought, more than once, by people who could stop.

3. **Are the economics demonstrated?** Does a real channel deliver customers at a cost the revenue covers, at any scale, even a small one?

Three yeses: the model is found; the nine blocks. Any no: the business is still searching; the Lean sheet, and the failed question tells the run which assumptions will top the list in Step 4.

**The mixed-state rule.** Decide by the weaker half. A proven product with an unproven channel is searching. A proven channel with a churning product is searching. The model is the unit, not the product — a business is found when the whole machine has run, not when its best part has.
