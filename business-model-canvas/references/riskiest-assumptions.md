# Riskiest assumptions — the method

The sheet is the middle of the work. This list is the end of it: the assumptions the model rests on, ranked by how dead the model is if they are false and how little is actually known, each with the cheapest test that could kill it. A run that produces a beautiful canvas and a vague list has the values exactly backwards.

## Extraction

Walk the filled sheet block by block. For every entry, ask: *what must also be true for this to hold?* The answers come in three kinds:

- **Stated assumptions.** Entries Step 1 already labeled assumption: the price a segment is expected to pay, the channel expected to carry the offer.

- **Hidden assumptions.** The beliefs underneath entries labeled evidence. A contract is evidence of today's price and an assumption about next year's. A working channel is evidence of today's conversion and an assumption about its cost at ten times the volume.

- **Load-bearing absences.** What the sheet does not say at all: the partner who never appears because the model quietly assumes they keep supplying; the regulation assumed stable; the founder's own hours assumed infinite. Absences are extracted by asking, of the whole sheet: *what does this model take for granted that it never wrote down?*

## The ranking

Score each assumption on two plain questions:

- **Impact.** If this is false, what dies? The whole model, one segment, one channel — or merely a margin?

- **Uncertainty.** How much is actually known? Behavior observed beats money paid beats words spoken beats reasoning from analogy. Most ranking errors come from scoring uncertainty by confidence instead of by evidence quality; the scale here is evidence, not feeling.

The top of the list is high impact and high uncertainty. Five entries is the working ceiling: a list of twenty is a way of prioritizing nothing, and the top five are where the next tests belong. Everything below the cut stays in the record, unranked, so a later run can promote what the first tests teach.

## What makes a test a test

Each top assumption gets four fields, and a test missing any of them is an intention, not a test:

- **The move.** The cheapest action that could kill the assumption — cheapest in money and weeks, not in comfort. A landing page before a product, a price asked before a discount schedule, a letter of intent before a partnership.

- **The kill result.** Stated in advance: the number or event that ends the assumption. "Weak interest" is not a kill result; "fewer than 8 of 30 clinics sign the paid pilot letter" is.

- **The owner.** One name. Assumptions owned by "the team" are owned by no one and tested never.

- **The date.** When the result will be read. A test without a read date drifts until it agrees with whatever happened.

**The honesty rule.** A test designed so it cannot fail — a survey of friends, a pilot with no price, a metric chosen after the results — is recorded as no test. The record says so, in those words, because a fake test is worse than none: it converts an assumption into false evidence, the one downgrade worse than uncertainty.

## Failure modes

- **Testing the easy ones.** The list fills with low-impact assumptions because their tests are pleasant. The ranking exists to prevent exactly this comfort.

- **The assumption that is really a hope about people.** "Partners will keep their pricing," "the segment's budget holder will care." These are incentive questions; the environment pass's reading — what they want, what they could take — is the extraction tool, and the test is usually a conversation held before it has to be.

- **Killing the model quietly.** A top assumption fails its test and the canvas is never redrawn — the record updated everywhere except the one page that matters. Step 5's triggers exist for this: a failed top test is a redraw trigger by definition, and the re-run starts at Step 0, because a dead assumption can move a business from found back to searching.
