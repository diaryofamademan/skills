# The strategy gate

Step 6 of the procedure, in full. The gate exists because most positioning work fails in a specific way: it produces a sentence everyone likes that commits the company to nothing. The gate's tests — drawn from Michael Porter's strategy work and Kevin Lane Keller's brand-equity framework, with Geoffrey Moore's whole-product test — convert the sentence back into a set of commitments, and fail the run if the commitments are not there.

A position that fails the gate is not edited at the gate. It is returned to the step whose weakness the failing test names.

## Test 1 — Trade-offs (Porter)

Porter's core claim, from "What Is Strategy?" (1996): a strategy is a unique and valuable position built from a set of activities different from rivals' — and choosing a position requires trade-offs, because some activities are incompatible and more of one means less of another. A company that refuses trade-offs has chosen to resemble everyone, and will be beaten on execution by whoever executes cheapest.

The gate requires the record to name three lists:

- **Customers not served.** Buyer groups the company could chase and will not, because serving them would bend the product or the model away from the best-fit buyers.

- **Offers not made.** Features, services, price tiers, or contract terms the company will not provide — including ones competitors advertise — because providing them would break the activity system the position rests on.

- **Deals walked away from.** The recognizable shape of a bad-fit deal the sales team is expected to decline: the logo that would require the forbidden feature, the segment that would pull the roadmap.

The lists are read for cost. A trade-off that costs nothing — refusing customers who would never buy anyway — is not a trade-off. The test is whether each refusal would be felt: in a lost deal, a declined feature request, a smaller addressable market on a slide. If nothing on the lists would ever hurt, the position has not yet been chosen; return to Step 4.

## Test 2 — Activity fit (Porter)

The position must be visible in what the company does, not only in what it says. List the activities the position depends on — how the product is built, priced, sold, onboarded, supported, and renewed — and check two things:

- **Reinforcement.** Each activity should make the others stronger in the direction of the position. A product built for speed to value, sold on a short cycle, priced without a procurement gauntlet, and onboarded in a day is a system. A product built for depth, sold on speed promises, is a contradiction the market will find before the team does.

- **Consistency with the trade-offs.** An activity that quietly serves a refused segment or delivers a refused offer voids Test 1. The classic failure: the position refuses enterprise deals, but the pricing page carries an enterprise tier "just in case."

Fit is also the position's defense, and the gate names it as such. A rival can copy the record's sentence in a day. To copy the position, the rival must copy the activity system and accept the trade-offs — abandoning the customers and offers their own position serves. The more coherent the fit, the higher that price. A position any competitor could adopt by changing a headline is recorded as fragile, and the fragility is stated in the record rather than discovered in the market.

## Test 3 — Parity versus difference (Keller)

Keller's framework: a brand's position is built from points of parity — the associations buyers require of any credible member of the category — and points of difference — the associations that distinguish this brand and motivate choice. Both are necessary; confusing them is the error.

Every element of the position is sorted:

- **Points of parity** are tested by removal: if the product lacked this, would buyers strike it from consideration? If yes, it is parity. Parity elements are delivered to the category's standard — no better than needed, unless the excess is itself a proved difference — and they are never the headline of the position. A run whose lead claim fails this test ("we're secure, too") has no difference at its head and returns to Steps 2–3.

- **Points of difference** are tested three ways: buyers must actually want it (desirability, from the Step 3 evidence), the company must actually deliver it (deliverability, today, not on the roadmap), and it must actually distinguish (against the Step 1 frame, not against a straw man). A difference that fails any leg is removed from the position — it may live in the unproved-claims backlog until the failing leg is fixed.

The sort also catches the reverse error: a genuine difference treated as parity and undersold. If an attribute passes all three legs and the current market presentation buries it, the gate flags it as the position's underused asset.

## Test 4 — The whole product (Moore)

For the beachhead segment named in Step 4: can those buyers reach the promised outcome with what exists today — the product plus everything around it that the outcome requires? Moore's whole product is the complete set: integrations with the tools the segment already runs, data migration, onboarding, training, support shaped to the segment, partners where the product stops short, and proof (references from inside the segment) that it has worked.

The gate walks the beachhead buyer's path from trigger to outcome and lists every gap. A gap the company will close before selling into the beachhead is recorded as a launch condition. A gap it will not close means the beachhead cannot receive the position — narrow the beachhead to buyers who do not hit the gap, or return to Step 4 and choose again. Promising a segment an outcome it cannot reach does not produce disappointed customers; it produces the segment's reference network carrying the opposite position.

## The forcing exercise — onlyness

One exercise is permitted at the gate, labeled as an exercise: complete "the only ___ that ___" in one breath. It is useful precisely because it is brutal — most drafts fail it, and the failure shows where the position is still a list. Its answer has no authority: it goes back through Tests 1–3 like any other draft language, and it enters the record only in the form those tests approve. Radical-difference claims are the claim the buying evidence supports least; the exercise borrows the form, not the doctrine.

## The convergence warning

Posted at the gate, one line, from Youngme Moon's diagnosis: **rivals who benchmark each other converge** — the frame leader's attribute list, copied with better adjectives, produces a position indistinguishable from the leader's, at a smaller budget. Step 1 studies the alternatives to find the frame's real shape; the gate checks the output is not the leader's record with the names changed. If it is, the run returns to Step 2 and looks for the attributes the leader cannot claim — or concedes the parity position and competes on availability and memory instead.
