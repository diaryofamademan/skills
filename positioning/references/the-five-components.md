# The five components, in full

The procedure core of the positioning skill: April Dunford's five components, worked in her order, plus the optional sixth. `SKILL.md` carries the steps at working length; this file carries the depth — what good evidence looks like at each component, the failure each one catches, and how the components hand off to each other.

Dunford's method is a practice framework — the strongest in the field, built over 25 years as an operator and then a consulting practice on this one method — and its strength is that every component is checkable against customer evidence. Nothing in the five components is settled by opinion in a meeting. Each component has a source in what buyers do and say, and a component filled from the team's beliefs about buyers is marked as such and carried as an evidence gap.

## The order is the method

The components form a chain: alternatives define what "unique" can mean; unique attributes define what value can honestly be claimed; value defines who cares most; best-fit customers define which frame will make the value obvious; the category is picked last, to fit everything before it. Running them out of order produces the standard failures:

- Category first → the team hunts for attributes that fit a frame chosen for its glamour, and the value gets reverse-engineered.

- Value first → benefits are claimed with no attribute underneath them, and no proof can be attached because there is nothing to prove.

- Customers first, defined by industry → the value is averaged across buyers who want different things, and lands with none of them.

## Component 1 — Competitive alternatives

**What buyers would do if the product did not exist.** The source is win/loss evidence: post-decision interviews, sales-call recordings, the CRM's loss reasons read skeptically (buyers tell salespeople polite fictions; "price" usually means "the value never became clear against what I already do").

Build the list in buyers' categories, which rarely match the industry's:

- **Status quo.** The default alternative, and in many business markets the winner of most "lost" deals. Naming it honestly matters because its "features" are formidable: zero switching cost, zero risk, already paid for, already understood.

- **Makeshift systems.** Spreadsheets, shared inboxes, paper, a general tool used off-label, a person whose job quietly includes the workaround. Makeshift systems reveal what buyers actually need — they built it themselves, badly, because the need was real.

- **Internal builds and hires.** Especially in larger accounts: a team, a contractor, an agency retainer.

- **Direct competitors.** Only those buyers actually evaluated. A competitor nobody in the evidence ever named is not in the frame, however much the team watches them.

For each alternative, note its real strength — the reason a reasonable buyer picks it. A frame that caricatures the alternatives produces a position that wins arguments in the office and loses deals outside it.

**Handoff test:** the list is done when every attribute in Component 2 can be checked against it, and when the largest alternative by deal volume is named first, even when that is the status quo.

## Component 2 — Unique attributes

**Facts about the product that the alternatives do not share.** Attributes are stated so a skeptic could verify them: a capability, a mechanism, a data asset, an architecture choice, a term of service, a team's specific history. The discipline is subtraction: for each candidate attribute, check it against each alternative from Component 1. Three outcomes:

- **Unique** — no alternative has it. It proceeds to Component 3.

- **Shared with some** — it differentiates against part of the frame only. It proceeds, tagged with the alternatives it does and does not beat; the record will need that precision.

- **Universal** — every alternative has it. It leaves this component and is held for the gate's parity test: it may be a point of parity (table stakes) or simply noise.

Attributes are not benefits. "Processes a claim in four minutes" is an attribute with a number in it. "Saves time" is a benefit claim that belongs, if anywhere, in Component 3 with proof. Keeping the layers separate is what makes Component 3 auditable.

If the unique list is empty, the run reports that. It is a real and common result, and the responses are honest ones: compete on parity plus availability and memory (the record says so, and Step 7 becomes the strategy), or take the gap back to the product. Inventing a distinction at this desk is the one move the method forbids.

## Component 3 — Value, with proof

**What each unique attribute makes possible, for the buyer, that the alternatives do not.** Dunford's move is translation: attribute → capability it gives the buyer → outcome the buyer cares about. The translation is done in the buyer's economics: money, time, risk, revenue, embarrassment avoided — whatever the evidence says this buyer group actually optimizes.

Then proof, attached claim by claim. In descending strength:

- **Measured results from real use** — a number, its method, and its population ("across 41 accounts in the first year, median X fell from A to B").

- **Named customer accounts** — a specific buyer describing the outcome, quotable, with permission.

- **Demonstrable mechanism** — the buyer can watch the attribute produce the outcome, in a trial or a walkthrough, rather than take it on trust.

- **Reasoned projection** — the arithmetic from the attribute to the outcome, assumptions visible. This is the weakest form and is labeled as projection wherever it is used.

A claim that cannot carry at least the fourth form is logged as an unproved claim. Unproved claims are valuable — they are the research and product backlog, in priority order — but they do not enter the record as value.

**The buying-type scope.** Before the value list is finalized, the run names the buying type. In considered purchases, this component is the argument that wins: buyers compare, and the proved differences are what they compare. In repeat, low-deliberation purchases, buyers mostly do not compare at all — they buy what comes to mind — and this component's role shrinks to the moments of switching and first choice. Misreading the type wastes the run: sharpening claims for buyers who never compare, or neglecting proof for buyers who compare ruthlessly.

## Component 4 — Best-fit customers

**The buyers for whom the proved value matters most, described by shared characteristics.** Dunford's source is the existing customer base read backwards: who bought quickly, who succeeded visibly, who renewed and expanded, who would be genuinely harmed if the product vanished. The characteristics that predict that fit are usually situational, not demographic:

- A trigger — a growth stage, a new hire, a regulation, a failure — that makes the problem expensive now.

- A constraint — team size, technical setup, budget shape — that makes the alternatives fit badly and this product fit well.

- A behavior — they already built the makeshift version; they already pay for an adjacent tool; they already tried an alternative and left.

Firmographics (industry, size, geography) enter only where they proxy one of the above. The output is a description a salesperson can apply to a real account in a minute, and a description of who is *not* best-fit, which the gate will convert into trade-offs.

**The beachhead cut (Moore).** From the best-fit description, one segment is cut as the beachhead: the narrowest group that shares one trigger, one use, and references each other. The beachhead is where the position is proved in the market first. Segments beyond it are named as later moves, in order, so the record shows a sequence rather than a wish.

**The owned concept.** Drafted here, from customer language: how the happiest customers describe what the product is, in their words. Harvest phrases from interviews and reviews before writing any. The concept that survives is usually embarrassingly plain — which is the framing axiom's point: it has to live in buyers' minds, in the words already there.

## Component 5 — Market category

**The frame that makes the proved value obvious to the best-fit buyers.** A category does three jobs for a buyer: it names the comparison set, it sets expectations of what the product does, and it anchors a price range. The choice is made by asking which available frame does those three jobs most favorably *and truthfully* for the value proved in Component 3.

The candidate frames, in the order they should be considered:

- **The head-on frame** — the established category, entered as a better member of it. Right when the value is a clear improvement on the category's main axis and buyers already budget for it.

- **The subcategory frame** — a named niche inside the established category, defined by the best-fit characteristic ("X for teams that Y"). Right when the value is obvious only to the best-fit group and muddy to the category at large. This is the most common correct answer.

- **The against-the-alternative frame** — positioned explicitly against the status quo or the makeshift system rather than against products ("the end of X"). Right when the real competition is doing nothing.

- **The new-category frame** — a new name for the market. Allowed only on evidence that every existing frame actively misleads buyers about the value, and costed honestly: the creator pays to teach the market what the category is before it can sell into it, and the teaching benefits every later entrant.

Whichever frame is chosen, the record names who it advantages. A frame that requires out-educating a rich incumbent on their home ground is a strategy decision, not a wording decision, and it is recorded as one.

## Component 6 (optional) — Trend

A trend may be attached when it genuinely raises the urgency of the proved value for the best-fit buyers: a regulation with a date, a platform shift, a cost curve crossing. The test is subtraction: remove the trend from the record, and the position must still stand complete. A position that collapses without its trend was a trend with a product attached, and the run restarts at Component 1 with the trend listed among the buyer's circumstances, where it belongs.
