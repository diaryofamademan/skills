# Memory and measurement

The evidence layer of the positioning skill: how positions actually work in buyers' minds, drawn from the Ehrenberg-Bass research program — Byron Sharp's *How Brands Grow* volumes and Jenni Romaniuk's work on distinctive brand assets and category entry points. This is the skill's class-(a) layer: measured, replicated patterns of buying behavior. `SKILL.md` states the objective and Step 7's working rules; this file carries the mechanics and the measures.

## What the data says, briefly

- **Brands grow by penetration, not by loyalty engineering.** Across categories, big brands have many more buyers who buy slightly more often; small brands have far fewer buyers (the Double Jeopardy pattern). A position aimed at making a narrow base love the brand harder is aimed at the smaller lever.

- **Buyers buy from memory.** Most buying is light-deliberation: the buyer retrieves a small set of brands from memory, cued by the buying situation, and chooses among what comes to mind and is available. Brands not retrieved are not rejected — they are never considered.

- **Perceived differentiation is weak.** Buyers rate brands they use as somewhat distinctive and brands they don't use as much alike; perceived difference follows usage more than it causes it. This does not make real difference useless — in considered purchases it decides comparisons (see `the-five-components.md`, Component 3) — but it caps what claim-sharpening alone can do.

- **Light buyers matter.** Most of a brand's buyers buy rarely, and growth requires reaching them too. A position that only speaks to heavy, expert buyers is tuned for the minority of the volume.

The working conclusion the skill encodes: a position's job is to get the brand **thought of, by more category buyers, in more buying situations, with its proved value attached to the memory**. Steps 1–5 decide what the memory should hold. This file is about whether it gets held, and how anyone would know.

## Category entry points

A category entry point (CEP) is a cue that starts category thinking: the internal and external triggers from which a buyer retrieves brands. Romaniuk's research maps them across the **7 Ws** of a buying situation:

- **When** — the trigger moment, the time pressure, the stage of work or life.

- **Where** — the place, physical or digital, the need arises.

- **While** — what else the buyer is doing when the need surfaces.

- **With whom** — who is present, who else is involved, whose problem it also is.

- **With what** — the adjacent products, tools, or tasks the need arrives alongside.

- **Why** — the underlying goal or worry the purchase serves.

- **How feeling** — the emotional state: urgency, anxiety, ambition, embarrassment.

A run builds its CEP list from buyer evidence — interviews that ask recent buyers to reconstruct the last purchase moment by moment are the standard instrument — and ranks entry points by how many category buyers pass through them and how contested they are. The owned concept is then checked against the top entry points: can this idea be retrieved, whole, at that moment, by a light buyer who has never compared anything? If the concept only makes sense after a ten-minute explanation, it will not survive contact with a real buying situation, however well it did in Steps 1–5.

Breadth belongs here. The position and the beachhead stay narrow; the CEP list should cover the category's real buying situations widely, because each entry point the brand is absent from is a set of purchases it cannot win. Focus in position, breadth in memory — this is where the breadth lives.

## The distinctive-asset audit

Distinctive brand assets are the non-name cues that trigger the brand: a mark, a color, a phrase, a format, a character, a sound, a layout. Romaniuk's method scores each asset on two measures, taken from category buyers (not just customers):

- **Fame** — of buyers shown the asset with the brand removed, the share who link it to the brand at all.

- **Uniqueness** — of those who link it to some brand, the share who link it to *this* brand only.

The four cells of the grid drive four different decisions:

- **High Fame, high Uniqueness** — a working asset. It carries the position into memory. Protect it; use it everywhere the position is carried.

- **High Fame, low Uniqueness** — a dangerous asset. Buyers recognize it but split it with competitors — or worse, hand it to one competitor. It spends the brand's money building the category leader's memory. Rebuild it toward uniqueness or retire it.

- **Low Fame, high Uniqueness** — a seed. Only this brand owns it, but few buyers know it yet. This is the asset to invest behind, consistently, until fame builds.

- **Low Fame, low Uniqueness** — decoration. It does no memory work. Retire it and stop maintaining it.

Two disciplines make the audit honest. Assets are measured with the brand name removed — an asset that only works with the name beside it is the name working, not the asset. And assets are scored among category buyers, because an asset famous among current customers and invisible to everyone else cannot do the growth job. The audit's output goes into the record: which assets carry the position, which are seeds to build, which are retired — and the position is not considered fully shipped until at least the carrying assets are named.

## Measures — how a run knows the position is landing

Chosen at Step 7, read on a cadence the record names. Four families, in the order they move:

- **Retrieval.** In surveys of category buyers, unaided and CEP-cued recall: when the buying situation is described, which brands come to mind? The position is landing when the brand's retrieval at its named entry points rises among category buyers — not just among customers.

- **Concept association.** Of buyers who retrieve the brand, how many attach the owned concept to it — and how many attach it to a competitor instead? A concept buyers love but credit to the leader is the high-fame, low-uniqueness problem at the level of the idea itself.

- **Asset movement.** Fame and Uniqueness re-measured for the carrying assets. Slow-moving, and the most durable signal when it comes.

- **Market read.** Penetration within the beachhead segment first (the beachhead exists so this number can move visibly), then across best-fit customers; win/loss reasons shifting toward the proved differences and away from "we stayed with what we had."

What not to measure the position by: loyalty intensity among existing customers (it barely moves and barely matters to growth), and internal agreement — the team repeating the statement in a meeting is the completeness check passing, not the market learning anything.

## The Kantar note

One counter-dataset deserves a named paragraph, because a careful reader will meet it. Kantar's brand work with Oxford's Saïd Business School finds being "meaningfully different" the largest brand factor in predisposition and in share-price outperformance — apparently against the Ehrenberg-Bass reading. The two camps measure different things: predisposition (what buyers say and feel before buying) versus actual purchasing behavior across millions of transactions. This skill's resolution: perceived difference is valuable where it is measured in buyers and attached to a brand they can retrieve — which is why the concept-association measure above tracks difference as an association, not as a claim. What the skill rejects is only the inference from "difference matters" to "a sharper claim, unread, is a strategy."
