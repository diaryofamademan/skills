# Worked example — the costing tool that was not accounting software

A composite. Every name, company, and figure below is invented, and the shape is labeled for what it is: an illustration of the method, assembled from the failure patterns the method exists to catch. No real person, company, or case appears in it.

**The situation.** Copperline makes a job-costing product for electrical contractors: crews log materials and hours against jobs from their phones, and the owner sees each job's real margin while the work is still running, not weeks after it closes. The company has sold for two years as "accounting software for contractors," grown slowly, and lost most competitive deals it can identify. The founder's diagnosis is that the website copy is weak. A positioning run is commissioned before a planned relaunch.

## Before the steps — the evidence base

Twenty-two win/loss calls and a review of forty lost-deal notes produce the run's raw material. Two findings matter immediately. First, of the forty lost deals, twenty-six were lost to no decision at all — the contractor kept doing what they were doing. Second, the happiest customers, unprompted, describe the product the same way: "I know which jobs are bleeding before they're done." Nobody interviewed describes it as accounting.

## Step 1 — Competitive alternatives

From the evidence, what a 5–30-person electrical contractor actually does about job costs:

- **The status quo (the largest alternative).** Costs reviewed when the bookkeeper closes the month — four to eight weeks after the money is spent. Margin surprises are discovered as history.

- **Makeshift.** A spreadsheet the owner maintains at night, fed by paper job cards and material receipts from the truck. Real, widespread, and hated — but free and understood.

- **The accounting platform's job module.** The general accounting package most contractors already pay for has a job-costing module; it is fed at invoice time, so it reports costs after the job, not during it.

- **Direct competitors.** Two contractor-software suites with costing features, named in a handful of calls each — a far smaller presence than the founder assumed.

Reaction check: the accounting platform could ship live cost feeds within a year if it chose to; the suites could not match a during-the-job workflow without rebuilding their data model. The position, whatever it becomes, should be built on the during-the-job ground.

## Step 2 — Unique attributes

Checked against the frame, three attributes survive:

- **Costs land during the job.** Crew entries and supplier invoices post to the job the same day, so margin is live while corrective action is still possible. Unique against every alternative in the frame.

- **Crew-side capture takes under a minute.** Photo of the supplier slip, hours in two taps, designed for gloves-on use. The suites require office entry; the makeshift route requires the owner's evening.

- **No bookkeeper required to run it.** The product reconciles against the accounting platform rather than replacing it. Shared with the makeshift route in spirit, unique among the software alternatives.

Filed for the parity test instead: bank-grade security, mobile apps for both phone platforms, "integrates with your accounting software" as a bare claim — every software alternative says all three.

## Step 3 — Value, with proof

- **Value: catch a bleeding job while it can still be saved.** Proof: in a nine-month pilot across 14 firms (invented figures, labeled as such in the real record's method note), owners flagged an average of 3 jobs per firm mid-run that closed in the black after a scope conversation or a crew change, jobs the owners said would previously have surfaced as losses at month-end.

- **Value: the owner's evening back.** Proof: named-account accounts from the win/loss calls — owners describing the spreadsheet ritual ending. Quotable with permission; the weakest of the three proofs, and labeled so.

- **Value: price the next bid from real costs.** Proof: reasoned projection only — the arithmetic from live job costs to bid accuracy, assumptions visible. Logged at projection strength, and worded as projection wherever the record uses it.

- **Logged as unproved:** "win more bids." Owners believe it; nothing in the evidence can check it. It goes to the backlog and appears nowhere in the position.

Buying type: a considered purchase. Owners compare, trial on live jobs, and ask peers. The proved differences are the argument — and the peers are the beachhead's reference network, which Step 4 uses.

## Step 4 — Best-fit customers, beachhead, owned concept

Reading the happy customers backwards, the fit characteristics are situational: 5–30 field staff (below 5, the owner sees every job personally; above 30, there is an operations manager and a different buying process); running 8+ jobs at once, so no one can hold the margins in their head; already burned once by a job that "looked fine" and closed at a loss — the trigger nearly every happy customer names.

**Beachhead:** electrical contractors in that band who have had a loss-surprise job in the last year. One trigger, one use, and a trade community that talks to itself — owners meet at the same supply houses and association chapters, so references radiate.

Not best-fit, named plainly: solo operators; general contractors (different cost structure, different buyer); firms above 30 field staff, for now.

**Owned concept**, in the customers' own sentence: **"know which jobs are bleeding while you can still stop it."** The concept is live job margin, in the words the buyers already use.

**The two stages.** Awareness: solution aware — every owner in the band already fights the problem with month-end reports and spreadsheets, and none interviewed knew margin could be seen live, mid-job. Sophistication: three of five — the suites have enlarged the reporting claim for years ("real-time insights," "total visibility"), and owners discount all of it. So the concept leads with the checkable mechanism, not a bigger reporting claim: crew time and material costs posted the same day, visible on a live job the owner picks. That match is what the record states.

## Step 5 — Market category

The incumbent frame — "accounting software for contractors" — fails on all three category jobs: it sets the comparison set as accounting platforms (which win on breadth), sets the expectation as month-end reporting (the opposite of the value), and anchors price against a tool already owned. The new-category option ("margin protection software") fails the evidence test: buyers have no such budget line and the frame would need a year of teaching.

Chosen frame, a subcategory: **job-costing software for electrical contractors** — with the position stated against the status quo first ("the end of finding out at month-end"), because doing nothing is the largest alternative. Frame-advantage check: the frame is too narrow to interest the accounting platform's education budget, and the suites cannot follow without rebuilding — the frame advantages Copperline precisely where its attributes are unique.

No trend attached. A labor-cost rise makes margins tighter, but the position stands complete without it, so the record leaves the slot empty rather than borrow urgency.

## Step 6 — The gate

- **Trade-offs, named.** Customers not served: general contractors and firms above 30 field staff, declined even when they ask — the workflows diverge and the roadmap would bend. Offers not made: payroll, invoicing, and a free tier — the product feeds the accounting platform, never replaces it. Deals walked away from: any deal requiring month-end batch reporting as the primary workflow, since that is the status quo wearing the product as a costume.

- **Activity fit.** Pricing per crew seat rather than per company (matches how the value scales); onboarding defined as "your first live job costed today," run by a former contractor, not a generic success team; the sales motion built around a trial on real in-flight jobs, because the value only proves itself live. The pricing page carries no enterprise tier. Fit holds.

- **Parity versus difference.** Parity, recorded as parity: security, both mobile platforms, accounting integration as a bare claim. Points of difference, all three legs checked: live during-the-job margin (wanted — the trigger evidence; delivered — the pilot; distinguishing — against the whole frame), under-a-minute crew capture, no-bookkeeper operation.

- **Whole product.** The beachhead path needs two things the product lacked: a supplier-invoice import for the two dominant regional suppliers, and a half-day crew-training visit, because foremen's habits are the real deployment risk. Both are recorded as launch conditions and scheduled before the beachhead push — the position is not promised to the segment until they exist.

- **Onlyness exercise.** "The only job-costing tool that shows an electrical contractor which jobs are losing money while the work is still running." Run back through the tests, it survives — as a summary of the record, not a replacement for it.

## Step 7 — Memory and measures

**Category entry points** (from the buyer interviews, ranked): the morning a job's material bill looks wrong; the month-end close that reveals a surprise loss; quoting a job similar to one that lost money; a foreman leaving, taking the job knowledge with them; the supply-house conversation where another owner mentions a bad job. The owned concept is checked against the first two and holds — it is stated in exactly the words owners use at those moments.

**Asset audit.** The company's one real asset: a red "bleeding job" margin indicator in the product, which owners screenshot and share in trade forums. Measured without the name attached (small sample, flagged as such in the record): owners who have seen it link it to Copperline and to no one else — high uniqueness, low fame. A seed: the relaunch puts it on every surface. The old asset — a generic lightning-bolt mark shared visually with a dozen trade apps — scores as decoration and is retired.

**Measures chosen:** retrieval at the top entry points among electrical contractors in the band; concept association ("live job margin" credited to Copperline versus the suites); the indicator's fame movement; penetration inside the beachhead, counted firm by firm.

## Step 8 — The statement, and what shipped

> For electrical contractors running 8 or more jobs at once, who have been surprised by a job that closed at a loss, Copperline is job-costing software that shows each job's real margin while the work is still running. Unlike month-end accounting reports, Copperline posts crew time and material costs the same day, so a bleeding job can be caught while it can still be saved.

Every slot fills from the steps; said out loud, it takes one breath. The record ships with the chain-out block leading — owned concept, category, the named alternatives with the status quo first, best-fit customers, and the trade-offs — ready for the marketing plan's message square to consume without re-derivation.

**What the example teaches.** First, the founder's diagnosis was wrong in the instructive way: the copy was weak because the position underneath it was borrowed — "accounting software" was the accounting platform's frame, and every month inside it made Copperline a worse version of someone else. Second, the largest competitor was never a company; twenty-six of forty losses were the status quo, and the position had to be stated against doing nothing before it was stated against any product. Third, the gate did the deciding: the trade-offs — refusing general contractors, refusing payroll, refusing the enterprise tier — are what turned a good sentence into a strategy the rest of the company could execute without re-deciding it weekly.
