# Narrative and position — the message-building tools

Two tools, used in order: first diagnose the position you hold in the audience's mind, then build the narrative that the moment needs.

## The position diagnostic (Ries)

Sources: Al Ries and Jack Trout, Positioning: The Battle for Your Mind (1981); Al Ries and Laura Ries, The Fall of Advertising and the Rise of PR (2002). Paraphrased, scoped to comms.

Positioning is not what you do to the product or the statement. It is what happens in the mind of the audience. That mind is oversimplified and defends itself: it keeps a short ladder of names per category, holds the leader at the top, and rejects information that does not match what is already there. You do not create a position from nothing; you work with the connections that already exist — reinforce them, retie them, or, to move a new idea in, move an old one out.

Ask, for each audience that matters:

1. What is our position in their mind today, in a few words?
2. Is it the position we would choose? If not, whose is it — and is the gap a wording problem or a reality problem? (A reality problem cannot be fixed by comms, and comms that tries becomes the next crisis.)
3. Does this moment reinforce, contest, or vacate the position?
4. If vacated, what rushes in? In a scandal, the replacement position is chosen by whoever narrates fastest and most completely.

Own one position, ideally expressible in one word or short phrase. Chasing every position is standing for none.

### The witness rule

From The Fall of Advertising and the Rise of PR: credibility is built by third parties. Advertising — and by extension any self-interested claim — defends a position; it rarely creates one. So sort claims:

- Your story, reasons, vision, decisions: say them yourself, in your own voice, on your own channels. No witness can do this for you and outsourcing it reads as absence.
- Facts about yourself the audience cannot verify (character, innocence, "it is over"): your saying them is a claim; a trusted third party saying them is evidence. Engineer the witness version — release the document, let the filing speak, let the coverage carry the headline — and keep your own statement to what the record supports.

## The narrative structure (Raskin)

Source: Andy Raskin, "The Greatest Sales Deck I've Ever Seen" (2016), on the Zuora deck. Paraphrased.

1. **Name a big change in the world.** Not your product — a shift already underway that the audience can feel.
2. **Show winners and losers.** The change divides the world; the audience must see which side they are currently on.
3. **Tease the promised land.** The desirable future the change makes possible, described before any product appears.
4. **Introduce features as gifts.** What you built, framed as the specific means of reaching the promised land — each feature the answer to an obstacle on the way.
5. **Present evidence.** Proof the story is already coming true: customers, numbers, events.

The test of the finished narrative: a supporter can retell it in one breath, in order, without notes. If they cannot, the structure is not done.
